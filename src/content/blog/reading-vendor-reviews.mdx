say you have two photographers open in two tabs on the same Tuesday night. both sit at five stars. both have a few hundred reviews. both have a gallery that makes you want to cry a little. you have no idea how to choose, so you pick the one whose Instagram grid you like more and you move on with your night.

that is the moment this post is for. a five-star average tells you people were happy. it does not tell you whether your vendor replies in two hours or two weeks, whether they fixed something that went sideways, or how they talk about a couple who left a hard review.

those three things are buried in the text, and most people skip straight to the number. response time, problem handling, and how a vendor replies to criticism are the details that actually predict what your day feels like behind the scenes.

## why the star rating stops being useful fast

once you have shortlisted vendors in your budget and style, the ratings cluster. almost everyone you are seriously considering will be rated highly, because vendors who are not rated highly get filtered out before they reach your shortlist. a number that every finalist shares cannot be a tiebreaker.

the rating also only measures one thing: whether the outcome was good. weddings are mostly a logistics relationship. you will spend months emailing this person about timelines, meal counts, load-in windows, and a cousin's last-minute dietary thing. the photos might be beautiful and the eight months leading up to them might still be stressful. the stars do not separate those two experiences. the text does.

so read the text. specifically, read it for these three things.

## 01. response time

scroll the reviews looking for lines like "replied within hours" or "hard to reach after booking." people mention communication constantly, usually in passing, usually as the first or last sentence of a review.

what you are building is a picture of how this vendor behaves when there is no event on the calendar yet. a vendor can be wonderful on the day and genuinely difficult to reach in the quiet months, which is most of your engagement.

### what to actually search for

use the search bar inside the review platform if it has one, or ctrl+F the page. look for:

1. "responded," "replied," "got back to me," "answered"
2. "email," "text," "phone," "call"
3. "after booking," "after I paid," "after the deposit"
4. "chased," "followed up," "never heard"
5. "last minute," "week of," "day before"

that fifth one matters more than people expect. the week-of stretch is when the timeline changes, the rain plan activates, and you need someone to pick up. a vendor who is excellent in month two and silent in week fifty-one is a specific kind of problem.

### read the pattern, not the outlier

one review saying someone was slow to reply in August might mean they were slow to reply, or might mean they were shooting four weddings that month. five reviews across two years saying the same thing is a pattern. weight accordingly.

## 02. problem handling

here is the counterintuitive part: a review that names a problem and says it got fixed tells you more than a perfect one.

a wall of "amazing! so great! perfect day!" tells you nothing you can use. it tells you a lot of weddings went fine, which is what usually happens. the review you want is the one where the cake arrived late, or the second shooter cancelled, or the linens were the wrong color, and the writer walks you through what the vendor did next.

that is the only kind of review that shows you the vendor under pressure. and something will go sideways at your wedding. not catastrophically, probably, but something. you are not hiring someone to prevent that. you are hiring someone who handles it without it becoming your problem at 4pm in a dress.

### what a good problem review sounds like

the strongest ones have three parts: here is what went wrong, here is what they did, here is how I found out. pay attention to that third part. "I didn't even know until after" is the gold standard. it means the vendor absorbed it. "they told me right away and gave me two options" is also excellent, it means they communicated under stress.

the version to be wary of is "there was an issue but it worked out," with no detail. that could mean the vendor fixed it. it could also mean the couple fixed it themselves and is being polite in writing.

### sort by lowest first

most review platforms let you sort or filter by rating. go straight to the lowest ones and read every single one. you are not looking for reasons to disqualify. you are looking for whether the complaints are about the same thing. scattered one-off complaints across different issues is normal for any business with volume. three people describing the identical failure is information.

## 03. how the vendor replies

how they respond to a negative review says exactly how they'll handle your wedding day.

this is the one almost nobody reads, and it is the most predictive of the three. a negative review is a vendor's worst day in public. watch what they do with it.

a reply that acknowledges the specific issue, does not relitigate the facts, and offers something concrete is a person who will tell you the truth when something breaks. a reply that is defensive, that corrects the customer's account point by point, that mentions how much the customer paid or how difficult they were, that uses the phrase "as I explained multiple times," is a preview. that energy does not stay in the review section. it shows up in your inbox the first time you ask for a change.

also notice silence. a vendor with several negative reviews and no replies to any of them is not necessarily bad, plenty of people do not monitor their listings. but combined with reviews mentioning slow responses, it starts to say something.

## red flags to watch for

- replies to criticism that blame the couple, the weather, the venue, or another vendor, with no acknowledgment of their own part
- a cluster of reviews posted in a short window that all sound alike and mention nothing specific
- reviews that praise the final product but repeatedly describe the lead-up as stressful or confusing
- "hard to reach after booking" appearing more than once, in any wording
- no negative reviews at all across a long history and high volume (possible, but worth a second look)
- a vendor who asks you to review them before the wedding happens
- replies that reveal private details about the couple's budget or behavior

## how to keep this organized

the reason people skip the text is that reading it properly takes twenty minutes per vendor and there is no good place to put what you find. the fix is to write it down in the same place you keep the quote, so that when you are comparing three caterers you are comparing price, inclusions, and communication at the same time, not from memory a week later.

for each vendor on your shortlist, note one line on response time, one on problem handling, one on how they reply to criticism. three lines. that is enough to break a tie between two five-star vendors, which is the actual decision you are making. you can [add your quotes free and compare vendors side by side](/get-started) so the notes live next to the numbers.

## the short version

1. read the text, not the number, once everyone on your list is highly rated
2. search reviews for communication words, especially anything about after booking
3. treat a named problem that got fixed as a better signal than a flawless rave
4. sort lowest first and read every negative review for repeating patterns
5. read the vendor's replies to criticism and take them literally as a preview
6. write three lines per vendor and keep them next to the quote

save this for the next time you're reading reviews, and send it to a friend who is still in the vendor search.
