You sign the venue contract, put down the deposit, and feel like the hard part is over. Then eight months later three invoices land in the same two weeks, your caterer wants a final headcount by Thursday, and you are moving money between accounts on a Tuesday night wondering how you missed all of this.

Here is the thing nobody hands you at booking: the deposit is just the beginning. Every contract you sign has its own quiet cash-out schedule buried in the fine print, and those schedules do not politely space themselves out. They cluster. They cluster hardest in the 4-6 weeks before your date, which happens to be the exact window when you have the least mental energy to deal with money.

So let's lay it out. Six vendor payments, when they are really due, and how to see the whole thing before you are in the thick of it. Save this before you book.

## the six payments, in the order they actually hit

Most budget spreadsheets track *totals*. Almost none track *timing*. That gap is why couples panic-transfer money the week of the wedding. Here is the real sequence, from the day you sign to the day you say vows:

1. **Venue deposit**: 25-50% of the total, due the day you sign.
2. **Photographer retainer**: due at booking, with the balance landing 2-4 weeks before your date.
3. **Florist balance**: final invoice hits 4-6 weeks before the date.
4. **Caterer final**: headcount locks about 2 weeks out, and the full balance is collected then.
5. **Band or DJ**: 50% retainer at booking, 50% the week before.
6. **Vendor tips**: cash envelopes, day of, budget $50-200 per vendor.

Notice how the front end (deposits and retainers) is spread across your engagement, while nearly everything else piles into the final 6 weeks. That is the part that ambushes people. Let's walk each one.

## 01 and 02: the money you spend at booking

### venue deposit

The venue deposit hits the day you sign, and it is the biggest single upfront number most couples will write. Expect **25-50% of the total** contract value. On a $20,000 venue, that is a $5,000 to $10,000 check the same day you commit. Plan for it before you fall in love with a space, not after.

Read your contract for *when the remaining balance is due*, because this varies wildly. Some venues want the rest 30 days out. Some want it split into thirds. A few want it in full 90 days before. That single detail changes your entire cash timeline, so don't assume.

### photographer retainer

Your photographer's retainer is due at booking too, usually a fixed amount or a percentage that holds your date. Photographers book 12-18 months out, so this is often one of your earliest payments. The **balance is due 2-4 weeks before your date** in most contracts.

That means the photographer balance lands right in the crowded pre-wedding window, so flag the exact date now. If your photographer's balance, florist's final invoice, and caterer's final all hit within the same fortnight, you want to know that in advance, not discover it live.

## 03 and 04: the 4-to-6-week crunch

This is the stretch that quietly wrecks budgets. Two of your biggest remaining balances come due here, back to back.

### florist balance

Florists typically send their **final invoice 4-6 weeks before the date**. By this point your final counts are set (how many centerpieces, how big the arch, how many bouquets and boutonnieres), so they finalize the number and bill you. If you have added anything since your original quote (an extra installation, a bigger head table, a last-minute ceremony arch), it shows up here. That is normal, but it is also why the final florist number is often higher than the number you remember agreeing to.

### caterer final

Your caterer's timing is the one to circle in red. The **headcount locks about 2 weeks out, and the full balance is due then.** Catering is usually priced per head, so this is frequently the largest balance left on your entire budget, and it comes due on a hard deadline you cannot really move.

Here is the trap: the headcount you lock is the headcount you *pay for*, whether or not those people show up. If you told the caterer 120 and only 108 come, you still pay for 120. Guests who RSVP'd yes and then bail do not get refunded. So do not pad your count "just in case." Lock the real number.

A quick example of how these two stack: say your florist final is $2,800 and your caterer balance is $9,000. Those can both land inside the same two-week window, on top of a photographer balance. That is nearly $12,000 moving in fourteen days, and if it surprises you, it feels like an emergency instead of a line item.

## 05: the band or dj split

Music is usually the friendliest structure on this list. Bands and DJs **typically split it 50/50: half at booking, half the week before.** The retainer holds your date, and the remaining 50% clears a few days out.

The reason this one is easy to forget is precisely because it is easy: you paid half so long ago that the second half feels like a surprise. Put the "week before" payment on your calendar the day you sign, and check whether your vendor wants it by cash, check, or transfer. Some entertainment vendors won't set up until the balance clears.

## 06: vendor tips, the line everyone forgets

And then there are vendor tips. **$50-200 per vendor, in cash envelopes, due the day of, and almost always missing from every budget spreadsheet.** Most couples forget this line entirely.

Tips are not optional in most people's plans, but because they are cash and last-minute, they never make it into the tracked total. Then the week of the wedding you are hitting an ATM in a panic trying to get clean bills and figure out who gets what.

Build it in early. Tip ranges vary by vendor, but a workable planning approach:

1. **List every vendor and helper** who will be on-site: caterer/servers, bartenders, hair and makeup, DJ or band members, photographer and second shooter, videographer, delivery drivers, officiant (a donation, if not a religious one), and coordinator.
2. **Assign a dollar figure** to each, staying inside the **$50-200 per vendor** range for individuals (larger service teams like catering are often tipped as a percentage or a pooled amount).
3. **Label envelopes now** and hand the stack to a trusted person (your coordinator, your MOH, a parent) to distribute on the day so you are not carrying cash in a wedding dress.

For a wedding with 8 to 10 tip-worthy vendors, that is easily $600 to $1,500+ in cash you need on hand the morning of. That is not a rounding error. Put it in the budget as a real line before you book, not after.

## red flags to watch for in your contracts

Before you sign anything, read the payment section like it is the most important paragraph in the document, because for your cash flow, it is. Watch for:

- **"Balance due" with no date.** A contract that says the balance is due "prior to the event" without a specific number of days is vague on purpose. Ask for the exact date in writing.
- **Non-refundable everything.** Deposits being non-refundable is standard. An entire balance being non-refundable months early is not. Know what you lose if plans change.
- **Auto-charged cards on file.** Some vendors auto-run the balance on a set date. Make sure you know when, or an $9,000 caterer charge can hit an account you forgot to fund.
- **Headcount minimums.** Many caterers and venues have a floor you pay for even if fewer guests come. Find your minimum before you finalize counts.
- **Overtime and "day-of" fees.** Late-running receptions, extra setup, and delivery windows can add charges that appear only on the final invoice. Ask what triggers them.

If a vendor won't put payment dates in writing, that is your answer. Every professional you hire should be able to tell you exactly what is due and when.
