Picture the family group chat lighting up at 9pm on a Tuesday. Someone's mom asks who's "handling the flowers," someone's dad replies that he "assumed that was covered," and within four messages nobody is sure who's paying for anything. Nobody wanted a fight. They just walked into the conversation without a map.

That's the thing about wedding costs. Most couples go into planning without a clear picture of who traditionally covers what, and then the family conversations get awkward fast. The traditional split isn't a rulebook you have to follow. But it is a useful starting point, a shared reference everyone can point at so the talk stays about logistics instead of feelings.

Here's the short version, expense by expense, with the dollar ranges that actually show up on quotes. Save this before those conversations happen.

## The bride's family: venue and ceremony flowers

Historically, the bride's family carries the two biggest ceremony-and-reception anchors.

### 01. The venue

The venue is usually the single largest line on the whole budget, and traditionally it falls to the bride's family. Expect a venue running $6,000 to $12,000 of the total budget, depending on your city, your guest count, and whether the date lands in peak season.

That range is exactly why you want the who-pays question settled early. A $6,000 venue and a $12,000 venue are very different conversations to have with a parent who thought they were chipping in for "a room." Get the actual quote in hand before anyone commits to covering it, so the number is real instead of imagined.

### 03. Ceremony flowers

Also traditionally on the bride's family: the ceremony flowers. That means the bouquets, the altar arrangements, and the aisle, which together average $2,000 to $5,000.

Florals are sneaky because the price scales with your choices in ways that aren't obvious up front. A larger bridal party means more bouquets. A dramatic altar installation or a lined aisle can double a quote fast. If the bride's family is covering this, walk them through what's included so a $2,000 assumption doesn't quietly become a $5,000 invoice.

## The groom's family: rehearsal dinner and boutonnieres

The groom's side traditionally takes two costs, one big and one that adds up quietly.

### 02. The rehearsal dinner

The rehearsal dinner is the groom's family's headline expense, typically $1,500 to $3,000 for the wedding party. This is the night-before meal for your closest people, often the immediate families, the wedding party, and their plus-ones.

The reason this one surprises people is guest count creep. What starts as "just the wedding party" turns into out-of-town relatives, then officiant, then the friend who flew in early. Every added seat moves you up that $1,500 to $3,000 range. Lock the headcount before booking the restaurant, and confirm whether the quote includes drinks, because bar tabs are where rehearsal dinners quietly blow past budget.

### 05. Boutonnieres

The other groom's-family item is boutonnieres, and this is the classic "small number, big total" trap. Each boutonniere runs $25 to $45, which sounds trivial until you count heads. With 6 to 10 groomsmen, plus fathers, grandfathers, and ushers, it adds up fast.

Do the quick math:

1. 8 groomsmen at $40 each is $320
2. Add 2 fathers and 2 grandfathers at $40 each, another $160
3. Now you're at $480 for something everyone assumed was a rounding error

None of these are budget-breakers on their own. But they're exactly the items that "someone assumed was covered," which is how you end up settling up in the parking lot after the rehearsal.

## The couple: bands, officiant, and guest transport

Then there's the set of costs the couple traditionally carries themselves. These are the ones worth budgeting for first, because there's no ambiguity about who's paying.

### 04. Wedding bands

Traditionally, each partner buys the other's wedding band, averaging $300 to $900 each. This is a nice symbolic split and an easy one to plan for, since it's just the two of you.

The number moves with metal and width. A simple band sits near the bottom of that range, while a wider band, a different metal, or added detailing pushes toward the top. If you're on a tight overall budget, this is a genuinely flexible line. You can spend $300 a side and nobody at the wedding will know or care.

### 06. Officiant fee

The couple pays the officiant, generally $300 to $800. If your ceremony is nonreligious, plan to tip $50 to $100 on top.

That tip line trips people up, so read it twice. A religious officiant is often covered by a donation to the house of worship, and tipping norms differ. A nonreligious or independent officiant is running a small business, and the $50 to $100 tip is standard practice on top of the fee. Budget for both pieces so you're not scrambling for cash on the day.

### 07. Guest transportation

Finally, the couple covers guest transport. Shuttle buses run $600 to $1,500 for the night, depending on how many runs you need and how far apart the venue and hotels sit.

Transportation is easy to forget until three weeks out, when someone points out that half your guests have no way to get from the hotel to a venue with no parking. At that point you're booking whatever's available at whatever it costs. Decide early whether you're providing shuttles at all, because "we'll figure it out later" is how a $600 line quietly becomes a $1,500 one.

## Watch for these red flags

The traditional split is a starting point, not a contract. Where couples get burned is in the gap between what people assume and what people actually said. A few things to watch for:

- **"I assumed that was covered."** The most expensive four words in wedding planning. Nobody's lying. Two people just filled the same blank with different answers. Write down who's covering what, in one shared place, so there's no assuming.
- **Strings attached to money.** A contribution toward the venue can quietly turn into a guest list demand or a color-scheme opinion. If a family member is covering a cost, get clear early on whether that comes with input, so it doesn't surprise you later.
- **Quotes that shift after booking.** The $6,000 venue with a service charge and a bar minimum can land closer to $12,000. The $2,000 floral estimate that didn't include the altar install. Always confirm what's inside the number before anyone agrees to pay it.
- **The small stuff that adds up.** Boutonnieres, the officiant tip, an extra shuttle run. Individually tiny, collectively a few hundred dollars nobody budgeted. These are the items that get argued over precisely because they felt too small to discuss.
- **Skipping the conversation entirely.** The worst plan is hoping it sorts itself out. It doesn't. It sorts itself out loudly, in the group chat, mid-negotiation.

## Make it your own map

Here's the part that matters most: knowing the traditional split gives everyone a starting point, even if you end up dividing things differently. Plenty of couples pay for everything themselves. Plenty split it three ways, or by whatever feels fair given who has what. The tradition isn't the goal. A clear, agreed plan is.

The move is to have the numbers in front of you before the family talks start.
