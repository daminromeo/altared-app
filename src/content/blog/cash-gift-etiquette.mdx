Picture a guest sitting on their couch three weeks before your wedding, invitation in hand, scrolling your registry. They see a line that just says "cash gifts appreciated" and no context, no link, no idea whether $50 is generous or stingy. So they freeze. Maybe they venmo you something and feel weird about it. Maybe they grab a random blender off a backup registry instead. Either way, the awkwardness you were trying to avoid just landed on them.

asking for cash instead of gifts is completely normal now. Couples already have the toaster. They have the towels. What they actually need is help with the honeymoon, the deposit, the first month in a new place. The concept isn't the problem. The wording is. Do it clumsily and a guest feels put on the spot. Do it well and nobody feels anything except happy to click a button.

Here are five ways to make the ask without a single guest feeling cornered, mapped to what actually shows up on your invite and wedding website.

## 1. name something real, not "cash"

"Cash" sounds like a demand. A specific, tangible thing sounds like an invitation to be part of something. The fix is to fund a real experience or item people can picture.

Instead of a blank money jar, name it:

1. A night's stay at the hotel you're honeymooning in
2. A dinner out on the trip
3. A flight upgrade so you're not folded into economy for eleven hours
4. The honeymoon fund itself, broken into pieces
5. A specific home goal, like the first month's rent in your new place

The psychology is simple. When a guest gives $50 to "something," it feels like handing over cash. When they give $50 to "your first dinner in Lisbon," it feels like a gift. Same money, completely different feeling. Name a real thing and let guests give $50 to something tangible.

This is also where a good honeymoon-fund platform earns its keep. Most of them let you list fund "items" with photos and short descriptions, so your registry reads like a wish list instead of an invoice.

## 2. let the wording on the invite do the heavy lifting

The wording on your invite does most of the heavy lifting, and the quietest version is almost always the best one. You do not need a paragraph explaining your financial philosophy. You need one clean line and a link.

The line that works nearly every time: **"your presence is the gift."** Pair that with a clean link to your wedding website and you have the least awkward version of the ask that exists. You are telling guests, plainly, that showing up is enough. Anyone who wants to do more will find the fund on your site. Anyone who can't, doesn't feel judged.

A few wording notes that keep it smooth:

- Keep the money talk off the physical invitation itself. The invite says "your presence is the gift" and points to your website. The website holds the details.
- On the website, a short, warm sentence beats a formal one. Something like "we're saving for our honeymoon and would be grateful for any contribution, but truly, just come celebrate with us."
- Never put a dollar amount or a "suggested contribution" on the invitation. That's the fastest way to make someone feel billed.

If you want more phrasing help, our [etiquette guides](/blog/category/etiquette) walk through invite and website language for touchy situations like this one.

## 3. offer small tiers so no one feels pressured

The single biggest source of cash-gift anxiety is not knowing what's normal. A guest who has no reference point will either overthink it or avoid it. Solve that by building the answer into your registry with tiers.

List clear contribution levels: **$25, $50, $100.** That's it. When those numbers are visible, a guest instantly understands the range, and nobody feels like they have to overspend. Someone on a tight budget clicks $25 and feels fine. Someone flush clicks $100 and feels generous. You've quietly removed the guesswork.

A few tips on tiering:

1. Start your lowest tier low enough that a student or a plus-one can participate without stress. $25 is a friendly floor.
2. Don't make the top tier huge. A $500 "level" reads as a hint, not an option.
3. Attach tiers to the real things from tip one. "$25 covers breakfast," "$50 covers a museum," "$100 covers a night." Now the amount and the meaning travel together.

Tiers turn a vague ask into a menu. Menus are comfortable. Blank fields are not.

## 4. keep a real registry mix alongside the fund

Here's the move most couples skip. Keep a handful of physical items on the registry, even if you mostly want cash. List **5 to 8 physical items at low price points** next to your cash fund.

Why bother if you don't need the stuff? Because it changes how the cash fund reads. When the fund sits alone, it looks like the ask. When it sits beside a few real gifts, the cash fund feels like one option, not the demand. Guests who are uncomfortable giving money now have an out, and the ones who prefer cash still have their easy path. Everybody gets to choose, and choice is what kills the awkwardness.

Good low-price items to sprinkle in:

- Nice everyday things you'd genuinely use (good knives, a cast iron pan, quality sheets)
- A couple of "fun" picks that show personality
- Things under $50 so a guest who wants a physical gift isn't forced into a big spend

You're not building a full traditional registry. You're building enough of one that the cash fund feels like a natural part of a set of options.

## 5. pick your platform before anyone contributes (fees vary a lot)

This is the step that quietly costs couples money, because platform fees vary from 0% to 2.5% per transaction and almost nobody checks before guests start giving.

The spread is real. **Honeyfund is free. Zola takes 2.5%.** On a $5,000 cash haul, that 2.5% is $125 that never reaches you, gone to processing. That's a hotel night you named in tip one, evaporated because of a default setting.

Before you send a single invite, do this:

1. Compare the platforms you're considering on their cash-fund fee, not their sticker features.
2. Read whether the fee is charged to you or passed to the guest (some let the guest cover it, which changes the math).
3. Check payout speed and whether there's a separate credit-card processing fee stacked on top.
4. Lock your choice in before contributions start, because moving platforms mid-registry is a mess and confuses guests who already gave.

Cheaper isn't automatically better. A free platform with a clunky interface can cost you in guests who give up at checkout. But you should choose with the fee in front of you, not discover it on your payout statement.

### watch for these red flags

A few things quietly sabotage an otherwise fine cash registry. Watch for:

- **Any dollar figure on the printed invitation.** The moment a suggested amount hits the paper, it reads as a bill. Keep numbers on the website only.
- **A cash fund standing completely alone** with zero physical items. It looks like a demand instead of a choice.
- **Guilt-forward wording.** "We'd prefer cash" or "no boxed gifts please" can land as bossy. "your presence is the gift" does the same job warmly.
- **Fees you didn't check.** That 2.5% is easy to miss until it's already been skimmed off every transaction.
- **Multiple payment methods scattered everywhere** (venmo here, a fund there, cashapp in a group text). One clean link keeps it simple and keeps guests from wondering which one is "right."

That last point about unchecked fees is the same trap that hides in vendor contracts, where a 2% or 3% line item slips past you until the final invoice. It pays to read the fine print on both.

## put it together before you hit send

None of this requires a delicate conversation or a long explanation. It's a few small setup choices that make the ask feel like a normal, generous option instead of a request nobody knows how to answer.

Save this before you send anything out:

1. **Name real things.** Fund a hotel night, a dinner, a flight upgrade. Let guests give $50 to something tangible.
2. **Let the invite carry it.** "your presence is the gift" plus a clean link to your wedding website is the quietest version of the ask.
3. **Offer small tiers.** List $25, $50, $100 so no one feels like they have to overspend.
4. **Mix in 5 to 8 physical items** at low price points so the cash fund feels like a choice, not the demand.
5. **Pick your platform on fees first.** Honeyfund is free, Zola takes 2.5%, and that spread is real money. Choose before guests start contributing.

The same eye for hidden costs that saves you $125 on a registry fee will save you a lot more across your vendor contracts. Before you sign anything, [get started](/get-started) and scan your own vendor quotes for the fees nobody flagged. The quiet line items are where the money goes.
