Picture the morning of your wedding. Your dress is half-zipped, your maid of honor is holding a curling iron, and your phone lights up for the ninth time in an hour. The florist is at the wrong loading dock. The DJ wants to confirm the grand entrance song again. Your uncle is texting to ask where he parks. Multiply that by the roughly 150 vendor and guest messages that hit before you've even had breakfast, and you can see the problem. Some of those texts should never reach you at all.

That's the whole point of this list. Not every line item is worth cutting. DIY costs more when it goes wrong, and it goes wrong most often in the exact places where you can least afford a redo. Below are six wedding tasks worth paying a pro for, with the real numbers behind each one, so you can decide where the risk lives instead of discovering it on the invoice.

None of this is about spending more. It's about knowing where a small save upfront turns into a big cost later.

## 1. Day-of coordination

A day-of coordinator averages around **$1,500**, and what you're really buying is a firewall. They field the 150+ vendor texts so you don't. They know the timeline down to the minute, they chase the late florist, they tell the caterer when to start plating, and they handle the parking uncle.

Couples cut this line first because it feels optional. You already made the plan, so why pay someone to run it? Because the person who made the plan (you) is also the person getting married. You physically cannot be zipping your dress and directing traffic at the same time. A coordinator is the difference between being a guest at your own wedding and being the unpaid operations manager of it.

If your budget is tight, ask about a "month-of" package, which is usually a lighter version of the same service. But do not assume your venue coordinator covers this. Venue staff manage the building, not your vendors or your timeline. Those are two different jobs, and confusing them is one of the most common and expensive assumptions couples make.

## 2. Contract review

Here's the math that surprises people most. A contract attorney charges **$150 to $300** to read your vendor agreements and catch the clauses that cost **$3k+** to undo.

That's not a hypothetical. Wedding contracts are full of language that looks routine until it isn't:

1. **Cancellation and postponement terms.** What happens to your deposit if the date moves? Some contracts keep 100% of it.
2. **Force majeure clauses.** Vague wording here decides who eats the cost when something outside everyone's control happens.
3. **Exclusivity and vendor restrictions.** Some venues bar outside caterers or charge a fee to bring your own.
4. **Overtime and hidden fees.** Per-hour rates that kick in automatically, plus "service" or "administrative" charges you never agreed to out loud.
5. **Image and liability rights.** Who owns your photos, and who's covered if a guest gets hurt.

Spending $150 to $300 to avoid a $3,000 problem is one of the highest-return decisions in wedding planning. If a full attorney review isn't in the cards, at least read every contract twice and flag anything you don't understand before you sign. Our [contracts guide](/blog/category/contracts) walks through the clauses that trip couples up most.

## 3. Floral design

DIY florals wilt under event lighting. The bouquet that looked perfect at 9am in your cool kitchen is a different flower by the time it's under warm reception lights at 7pm.

Pros typically charge **10 to 15% of your total budget**, and it shows. That number covers more than the stems. It covers conditioning the flowers so they last the day, knowing which blooms survive heat and which collapse, transport, setup, and the design eye that makes an arch look intentional instead of assembled. Flowers are deceptively technical. Buckets of grocery-store stems seem cheap until you realize you also need someone to strip, cut, arrange, and keep them alive across a 12-hour day while you're otherwise occupied.

### Where a hybrid can work

If full floral design is out of reach, this is one area where a smart middle ground exists. You can hire a pro for the pieces that must hold up on camera and in the heat (bouquets, boutonnieres, ceremony focal points) and DIY the lower-stakes touches like loose greenery on tables. Just be honest with yourself about how many hours of setup you're signing up for the day before, when you should be resting.

## 4. Second shooter

A second shooter is an add-on that runs **$300 to $800**, and it exists for one reason: your main photographer can only point the camera in one direction.

During the ceremony, that's a real constraint. While your lead photographer shoots the altar (your faces, the vows, the ring exchange), the second shooter covers the aisle, the guests reacting, your partner's expression the moment you walk in. Without a second body in the room, you get one side of every moment and lose the other forever. There is no reshoot on a wedding.

A second shooter also covers the moment your main photographer physically can't. When you're getting ready in one room and your partner is in another, one photographer means one of those rooms goes undocumented. For $300 to $800, you double your coverage during the parts of the day that happen exactly once.

## 5. Hair and makeup trial

Skipping a hair and makeup trial saves **$150 to $250** upfront. It also makes your wedding day the first time the look is tested on camera, in your dress, in daylight, with real photos being taken.

That's a gamble with terrible odds. A trial is where you find out that the foundation flashes white under flash photography, that the updo won't hold past the ceremony, or that the lip color reads completely differently in person than in the inspo photo. Discovering any of that at 8am on the wedding day, with no time to fix it, is how the "savings" evaporate.

Book the trial. Take photos with your phone flash on. Sit in it for a few hours to see how it wears. The $150 to $250 you'd save by skipping it is the cheapest insurance in the entire budget, because the alternative isn't just an aesthetic risk, it's the risk of hating how you look in every single photo you'll keep forever.

## 6. Cake delivery

This is the sneaky one. Pros charge **$75 to $200** to deliver and set up your cake. A DIY transport collapse averages **$800+** to fix, if it can be fixed at all.

A tiered cake is structurally fragile. It has about a 45-minute runway before something shifts, and that's under good conditions. Add a warm car, a bumpy road, and a sharp turn, and you have frosting on the floor of a sedan with no backup and no time. Bakers deliver in climate-controlled vehicles, they assemble tiers on-site, and they know how to fix a smudge before it becomes a catastrophe. Paying $75 to $200 to skip the white-knuckle drive is an easy yes.

## Red flags to watch for in your quotes

As you collect quotes, these are the warning signs that a "deal" is hiding a cost:

1. **A venue coordinator being sold as day-of coordination.** They're not the same. Ask exactly which vendors they manage and which they don't.
2. **Delivery and setup listed as "included" without a line item.** Get it in writing, especially for florals and cake.
3. **No trial mentioned in a hair and makeup package.** Confirm whether it's included, separate, or assumed.
4. **A single photographer for a two-location day.** Ask directly whether a second shooter is available and what it adds.
5. **A contract you're pressured to sign fast.** Any vendor rushing your signature is a reason to slow down, not speed up.
6. **Vague cancellation language.** If you can't tell what happens to your deposit when plans change, that's the clause to question first.

## The quick version

Here's what to hold onto when you're mid-spreadsheet deciding where to cut:

- **Day-of coordination ($1,500 avg):** someone else fields 150+ vendor texts while you get dressed.
- **Contract review ($150 to $300):** catches clauses that cost $3k+ to undo.
- **Floral design (10 to 15% of budget):** DIY florals wilt under event lighting.
- **Second shooter ($300 to $800):** covers the aisle while your photographer shoots the altar.
- **Hair and makeup trial ($150 to $250):** skip it and the wedding day is the first test, on camera.
- **Cake delivery ($75 to $200):** a DIY transport collapse averages $800+ to fix.

None of this means you have to spend more. Plenty of tasks are genuinely fine to DIY. It's about knowing exactly where the risk lives so you can decide, not discover it on the invoice.
