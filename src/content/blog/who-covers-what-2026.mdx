the venue quote said $22,000, all in. the couple signed it, felt good about it, and started planning the rest of the wedding around that number. three months later they were staring at a spreadsheet that had quietly grown by almost $10,000, and not one of those new lines was a surprise upgrade or a splurge. it was rehearsal dinner. it was cash tips. it was shuttles for guests who couldn't drive after an open bar. every single one of those costs used to belong to somebody's parents. now it belonged to them.

the old playbook said the bride's family covers the ceremony, the groom's family covers the rehearsal dinner, and everyone splits the rest. that's not how it works anymore. most couples walk into planning season and discover seven line items sitting entirely in their own lap. none of it shows up in the first venue quote. all of it shows up in the final budget.

here's the good news: knowing these seven before you book means you can build them in rather than scramble for them later. below is each one, with the real ranges, so you can put a number next to it today instead of finding out in month three.

## the seven costs that quietly moved to your side of the table

Let's go one by one. Read these as a checklist, not a horror story. Every item has a range you can plan around.

1. Rehearsal dinner: $1,500-$4,000
2. Vendor tips: $500-$1,200 in cash on the day
3. Day-of transport (guest shuttles): $800-$2,500
4. Both wedding bands: budget 3-5% of total spend per band
5. Welcome bags for out-of-town guests: $15-$35 each
6. Hair and makeup for the whole bridal party: $900-$2,000
7. Honeymoon fund: an average of $4,400 self-funded

That list adds up to real money on top of the reception. Now here's why each one lands where it does.

### 1. rehearsal dinner ($1,500-$4,000)

Traditionally the groom's family paid for this. It was the one night that had a clear owner. That expectation has largely faded, and now most couples budget $1,500-$4,000 themselves.

The spread comes down to headcount and format. A plated dinner for 40 at a restaurant sits near the top of the range. A backyard taco spread or a pizza-and-drinks night for the wedding party and immediate family can land near the bottom. If you want to keep this line small, cap the invite list to people who actually attend the rehearsal, not every out-of-town guest who happens to be in early.

### 2. vendor tips ($500-$1,200, cash, on the day)

This is the one that catches almost everyone. Tips run 15-20% per vendor, and for a typical team that's $500-$1,200 extra. It's cash, and it's due the day of, which means it has to come out of savings and into labeled envelopes before you leave the house.

Think about who's on your team: photographer, videographer, DJ or band, hair stylist, makeup artist, catering staff, and delivery drivers. Not every vendor expects a tip (some build gratuity into the contract), but enough of them do that you should assume the full range applies. Check each contract for the word "gratuity" before you decide it's already covered.

### 3. day-of transport ($800-$2,500)

Shuttles and car service for guests run $800-$2,500, and couples pay 100% of it now. This one shows up when your ceremony and reception are in different places, when parking is tight, or when you have an open bar and a duty of care to get people home.

The math is driven by vehicle size and hours. A single shuttle running loops for four hours is cheaper than two coaches on standby all night. If your venue is remote, this stops being optional and becomes a safety line item, so price it early.

### 4. both wedding bands

The tradition where one side buys and one side receives has given way to a simpler reality: each partner buys the other's ring. Budget 3-5% of total wedding spend per band.

On a $30,000 wedding, that's roughly $900 to $1,500 per band, or $1,800 to $3,000 for the pair. Metals and stones swing this a lot, so treat the percentage as a starting frame rather than a hard cap. The point is that two rings, not one, belong in your budget.

### 5. welcome bags ($15-$35 per bag)

Out-of-town guests have come to expect them, and at $15-$35 per bag the cost adds up fast on 30-plus rooms. Thirty bags at the low end is $450. Thirty bags at the high end is $1,050. Now imagine 50 rooms.

You don't have to skip them, but you can be smart. Bag per room, not per person, cuts the count roughly in half for couples. Fill them with a couple of useful things (water, a local snack, a hangover kit, a printed schedule) rather than a dozen trinkets that inflate the per-bag cost.

### 6. bridal party hair and makeup ($900-$2,000)

Covering hair and makeup for the whole party is now the norm rather than a generous extra. At $150-$250 per person that's $900-$2,000 for a party of six or so.

If this line feels heavy, decide up front what you're covering. Some couples pay for their own look plus a base service for each attendant, and let anyone who wants add-ons (extensions, lashes, an airbrush upgrade) pay for those themselves. Say it clearly and early so nobody's guessing.

### 7. honeymoon fund (average $4,400)

Parents rarely pitch in here. The average couple self-funds $4,400 after paying for the venue. It's the cost that arrives last and gets planned least, which is exactly why so many couples put it on a card.

Build it as its own line from day one. A honeymoon registry can offset part of it, but plan as if it won't, so a slow registry doesn't become debt.

## the number that actually matters

Add the midpoints and these seven items can easily clear $10,000 on their own. That's not a splurge budget. That's the "everyone assumed someone else had it" budget. Which is exactly the trap: none of it shows up in the first venue quote, and all of it shows up in the final one.

The fix is boring and effective. Put a placeholder number next to all seven before you sign anything, even a rough one. A budget that includes these lines at their low end is more honest than a beautiful venue quote that pretends they don't exist. ## red flags to watch for before you sign

A few warning signs tell you a cost is about to jump the fence onto your side of the table:

- **A quote that only names the venue.** If the proposal covers room rental and catering but says nothing about staff gratuity, transport, or setup, those costs still exist. They're just yours.
- **"Gratuity not included" in the fine print.** This is the phrase that turns a 15-20% tip into a surprise. Search every contract for it.
- **A destination or remote venue with no shuttle mention.** No shuttle line usually means guests will need one and you'll be booking it late, at a premium.
- **A parent who says "we'll help with the wedding" without naming a line.** Warm, but not a budget. Ask which specific item, and get the number. Vague generosity has a way of evaporating around invoice time.
- **A bridal party glam count you haven't confirmed.** If you assume six and five want add-ons, your $900 estimate becomes $2,000 fast. Confirm services per person.

Catch these early and none of them are disasters. Catch them in month three and they're the reason the spreadsheet grew by $10,000 while you weren't looking.

## before you book anything

Here's the short version to save:

1. Write all seven lines into your budget now, at least at their low end.
2. Read every contract for the word "gratuity" so tips don't ambush you.
3. Decide what you're covering for the bridal party and say it out loud.
4. Price shuttles and rehearsal dinner as soon as your headcount and venue are set.
5. Give the honeymoon its own line and treat any registry money as a bonus, not the plan.
6. Confirm exactly what any parent is covering, by line item, in a number.
