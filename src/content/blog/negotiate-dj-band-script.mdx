you get the PDF back on a tuesday afternoon. eight hours of DJ coverage, ceremony sound, uplighting, "premium" package. the number at the bottom is $3,400. under it, in smaller type: setup/breakdown fee $350. travel (estimated) $275. overtime billed at standard rate. gratuity not included.

that is four separate places where the number can move, and most couples say yes to all of them without a single question.

music vendors are one of the most negotiable line items in a wedding budget, and most couples never ask. the quote you get first is rarely the floor. DJs and bands build in padding because they expect a conversation, and when the conversation never happens, they keep the padding. that is not a scam. it is just how pricing works when one side has done this three hundred times and the other side has done it once.

below are six lines worth having ready before any music vendor call. not vibes, not "be confident." the actual sentences, and what to do with the answer you get.

## why music is the line item that actually moves

photography is priced on the photographer's time and their editing hours, which are fixed and real. catering is priced on food cost and headcount, which are also fixed and real. music sits differently. a DJ's cost is their gear (already owned), their time (variable), and their travel (variable). the package price is more of a positioning decision than a math problem.

which means the add-ons stacked around the base rate are where the flexibility lives:

1. **setup and breakdown fees** ($200-400) are often waived when you ask directly, because the DJ was showing up early regardless.
2. **overtime rates** can be capped in the contract before you sign, when nobody is stressed and the reception is not currently running long.
3. **travel** is almost always listed as an estimate, meaning it is open to a fixed number.
4. **gratuity for DJs runs 10-15%**, separate from the quoted total, so knowing that before you book changes what you budget.

none of those four are the headline number. all four of them are money.

## the six lines, word for word

### 01. the anchor

> "our budget for music is $X, what can you do at that number?"

this one goes first, before they walk you through packages. it flips who is naming the price.

when a vendor presents three tiers and asks which one you want, you are choosing between numbers they picked. when you name your number first, they are building toward your number. a good DJ will come back with a version of their service that fits, usually by trimming something you did not need (an extra hour of cocktail coverage, a lighting package for a room with great windows).

say the real number, not a lowball. if your ceiling is $3,000, say $3,000. if you say $1,800 hoping to land at $3,000, a vendor whose floor is $2,400 will politely end the call and you will never know how close you were.

### 02. the overtime ask

> "can we cap overtime at $150/hr instead of your standard rate?"

overtime is the single most common way a music quote grows after you have already signed. the reception is going beautifully, your uncle requests one more song, someone says "can they stay another hour," and that hour gets billed at whatever the contract says. you agree to it at 10:40pm with a drink in your hand and no leverage whatsoever.

cap it in writing now. $150/hr is a reasonable ask for a DJ. bands cost more to keep on site because you are paying multiple musicians, so expect a higher number there, but the principle holds: get a stated ceiling, in the contract, before you sign.

and get the trigger defined too. does overtime start at the contracted end time, or after a 15-minute grace period? does breakdown time count? ask.

### 03. the setup waiver

> "setup and breakdown fees add $200-400, can those come off?"

this is the most frequently waived fee in the entire category, and it is the one couples are most reluctant to question because it sounds operational. it sounds like a cost, not a charge.

your DJ is arriving early to set up whether or not there is a line item for it. asking for it to come off is not asking them to do free labor, it is asking them to price the job as one number instead of two. plenty of vendors will say yes immediately. some will meet you halfway, dropping a $350 fee to $150. either outcome beats not asking.

if they hold firm, ask what the fee covers specifically. sometimes it is a genuine second crew member or a venue that charges for early load-in, and that is a fair answer. sometimes the answer gets vague, which tells you something else.

### 04. the travel cap

> "your travel line is open-ended, can we fix it at $X total?"

"travel: estimated" is an invoice writing a blank check to itself. mileage changes, gas prices change, and if the vendor books a hotel the night before, you may be looking at a number that has grown by a few hundred dollars by the time the final invoice arrives.

fix it. propose a flat figure, get it written in, and make clear it is inclusive of mileage, lodging, and any second-vehicle charge for gear. if your venue is genuinely remote and a hotel is unavoidable, agree on a lodging cap rather than reimbursing whatever they book.

### 05. the compare

> "we have a quote $500 lower, can you meet that or close?"

this works, but only if it is true. name the real gap, not a made-up one, because a DJ who has been working your market for a decade knows what the other DJs charge and will hear a fake number instantly.

the phrase "or close" is doing real work in that sentence. it gives the vendor a way to respond without capitulating fully, which makes a yes much more likely. a $300 move on a $500 gap is still $300.

this is also the line that requires homework. you need at least two or three real quotes in front of you, broken into line items, so you can see whether the $500 gap is a genuine price difference or one vendor bundling something the other charges for separately. [tracking quotes side by side](/get-started) is what turns "this one feels expensive" into "your setup fee is $200 higher than the next quote."

### 06. tip clarity

> "is gratuity included or separate? standard for DJs is 10-15%."

not a negotiation, a budgeting question, and skipping it is how couples end up $400 short in the final week.

gratuity for DJs runs 10-15%, separate from the quoted total. on a $3,400 package, that is $340 to $510 you need to have set aside. some vendors build a service charge into the contract, and if they do, you should know whether that charge goes to the person actually working your wedding or to the company. ask plainly. nobody is offended by this question.

for bands, tipping conventions differ and often work out to a per-musician amount rather than a percentage. ask what is customary for their setup specifically, and get the answer before you sign so it lands in your budget instead of surprising you.

## red flags on a music vendor call

most DJs and bands will handle these six questions like professionals, because they have heard all of them before. the reaction tells you as much as the answer.

**watch for:**

- **"we can talk about the contract after the deposit."** no. every number you negotiated lives in the contract. a verbal yes on an overtime cap is worth nothing at 11pm on your wedding night.
- **refusal to name a specific overtime rate.** "we'll work it out day-of" is a rate. it is just one you have not seen yet.
- **a travel line that stays "estimated" after you ask twice.** at that point it is a choice, not an oversight.
- **package language with no inventory.** "premium lighting" should be a count of fixtures. "full sound" should be a description of what is covered, ceremony and cocktail hour included or not.
- **no named performer in the contract.** multi-DJ companies sometimes assign talent later. if you fell in love with a specific person's mixing, their name belongs in the agreement, along with what happens if they are unavailable.
- **pressure to sign that day for a discount.** a real date hold is a real thing. a discount that expires in four hours is a tactic.
- **vague cancellation and postponement terms.** you want to know the refund schedule and what happens if you move the date, in writing.

one more thing to check rather than a red flag exactly: whether the quote includes the ceremony. a shocking number of "full day" music packages cover the reception only, and ceremony sound gets added later at $200-400. confirm early.

## how to actually run the call

1. **know your number before you dial.** name it out loud with the anchor line, and make it the real one.
2. **ask them to break the quote into line items,** not a single package price. you cannot negotiate a number you cannot see inside.
3. **work the four flexible pieces in order:** setup and breakdown, overtime cap, travel, then the comparison ask.
4. **confirm gratuity is separate** and add 10-15% to your working budget immediately.
5. **get every agreed change in the written contract** before any deposit moves.
6. **collect at least two more quotes** and compare them line by line, not bottom line to bottom line.

the goal here is not to grind a vendor down. the goal is to pay a fair number for exactly what you want, with no surprise line items showing up in month eleven of planning. a DJ who negotiates cleanly and writes it all down is usually a DJ who runs a clean reception too.
