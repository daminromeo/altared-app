say you land on a Sunday night, drop the suitcase in the hallway, and don't open it until Wednesday. by Thursday there's a pile of gift cards on the counter, two vendor envelopes you meant to hand out at the end of the night, and a group text asking if you got the air fryer. nobody is chasing you for any of it. that's exactly why it slips.

you're back, you're tan, and somewhere under that suitcase is a pile of gift cards, vendor envelopes, and a list of names you need to write by hand. the first two weeks after the honeymoon are quieter than planning, but they're not nothing. this is the admin nobody warns you about, and it has an order that makes it much faster.

here's the short version, then the detail:

1. write the gift log before anything else
2. send thank-you notes within two weeks, naming the actual gift
3. tip any vendor you missed, with a short thank-you by name
4. leave specific reviews while the details are sharp
5. start the name change in the right order: social security, dmv, bank

## start with the gift log, even though it's listed last

the gift log is the one people always skip, and then spend a week reconstructing from photos and texts. someone ends up zooming in on a blurry shot of the gift table trying to read a card, or scrolling back through a registry dashboard that doesn't match what actually arrived.

do it first, because every other task on this list leans on it. the thank-you notes need it. the "did we ever acknowledge your aunt's check" question needs it. and if anything arrived damaged or duplicated, you want the record before the return windows close.

write it down now while the cards are still somewhere findable. you want three columns, nothing fancier:

1. who gave it (full name, and note the household if it was a couple or a family)
2. what it was (be literal: "linen set," not "home goods")
3. where it came from (shipped, gift table, mailed after, given at the shower)

that third column matters more than it looks. gifts that arrive after the wedding are the ones that go unacknowledged, because they land in a week when nobody is thinking about the wedding anymore.

keep the log somewhere both of you can see it, one shared list rather than two versions. the same goes for the money side of the close-out: final vendor payments, tips you still owe, deposits coming back. we built [Altared](/get-started) to hold your vendors, quotes, payments and to-dos in one place through the final close-out, and this is the part of the timeline where that pays off.

## thank-you notes: within two weeks, and name the gift

thank-you notes go out within two weeks, and they land better when you name the actual gift. that's the whole trick. a note that says "thank you so much for celebrating with us and for the generous gift" reads like a form letter, because functionally it is one.

a note that says "the linen set is already on our bed" reads like you opened it.

### how to get through them without losing a weekend

- split the list by who knows whom. you write your side, your partner writes theirs, and you each sign both.
- work in batches of ten. notes get worse after that, and people can tell.
- for cash or a check, name what it's going toward instead of the amount. "we're putting it toward the apartment" is warm. restating the number is not.
- for guests who traveled, thank them for the travel as well as the gift. it's often the bigger ask.

if you missed the two-week mark, send it anyway. a late note is still a note. an unsent one is a thing your mother-in-law will mention in four years.

## vendor tips and thank-yous go now, while you remember who did what

vendor tips go now, while you still remember who did what. this is the task with the shortest useful memory. two weeks out, you remember the coordinator's name. two months out, you remember "the woman with the clipboard."

work the list in two passes.

**first pass: did anyone get missed?** envelopes handed off on the day have a way of not making it to the right hands, especially if you delegated distribution. check with whoever carried the envelopes, and check your contract for anyone whose gratuity was already built in so you're not double-paying. tipping norms vary by role and by region, so ask your planner or check your contracts rather than guessing.

**second pass: send each a short thank-you by name.** not a group email. one message each, two or three sentences, with one specific thing they did. vendors keep these. some of them put them on their websites. and if you ever need a referral, a reference, or a rush favor from that photographer again, this is the message that makes it easy for them to say yes.

if you're still sorting out what was owed to whom, our [contracts](/blog/category/contracts) posts cover reading the fine print on gratuity and final-balance language.

## reviews: specific beats enthusiastic

reviews go while the details are sharp. a review that says "AMAZING, 10/10, could not recommend more" helps nobody, including the vendor. it doesn't tell the next couple what this person is actually good at.

write the sentence only you could write. "she kept the timeline without us noticing" tells a stranger exactly what they're buying. so does "he reorganized the whole ceremony setup in the rain in under ten minutes" or "she answered every email within a day for eleven months."

### where to put them

post to the platforms the vendor actually asks you to use. most will tell you if you ask, and most will have one that matters most for their business. if you have photos you're allowed to share, attach one. a review with an image gets read.

**watch for:** don't post a negative review while you're still owed something, whether that's a final gallery, a refund, or a deposit return. finish the transaction first. if there's a real dispute, go back to the contract language and handle it in writing before it becomes public. and if a vendor offers you something in exchange for a five-star review, that's a red flag about how they handle feedback generally.

## the name change has a real order

if one of you is changing your name, the sequence is not optional. social security first, dmv second, bank after, because each step feeds the next. the dmv wants your updated social security record. your bank wants your updated id. do it out of order and you'll make the same trip twice.

a few things to have ready before you start:

1. **certified copies of the marriage license.** not the decorative certificate from the ceremony. order more than one. requirements and fees vary by county, so check yours.
2. **your new signature, picked and practiced.** you'll use it a lot in one week.
3. **a running list of everything else that has your old name.** passport, payroll, insurance, lease or mortgage, utilities, voter registration, frequent flyer accounts, your email signature.

on the passport specifically: if you have travel booked, check the name-match rules before you change anything, because your ticket has to match your document. the order is the same for anyone changing a name, and the timing is what varies by office, so build in more runway than you think you need.

## the close-out list

do these in this order and the whole thing takes a couple of evenings instead of a couple of months:

1. **gift log** before the cards get lost. who, what, how it arrived.
2. **thank-you notes** within two weeks, naming the actual gift.
3. **vendor tips** to anyone who got missed, plus a short thank-you by name.
4. **reviews** that say one specific true thing.
5. **name change** in order: social security, dmv, bank, then everything else.

then one last pass: confirm every vendor balance is actually at zero, save your contracts and final invoices somewhere you'll find them in a year, and close out your [budget](/blog/category/budgeting) so you know what the wedding really cost. that number is useful later, and it disappears if you don't write it down.

save this for the morning after you land, and send it to the friend who just got married.
