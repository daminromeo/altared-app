Picture the third night of a five-day beach wedding. The rehearsal dinner went great. Then a group of guests books a catamaran day trip, assumes the couple is covering it because "everything else has been covered," and shows up expecting a tab that never got discussed. The bride finds out at breakfast. Nobody is technically wrong. Everybody is a little annoyed. That is the actual number one source of destination wedding drama, and it is not the venue or the guest list. It is everyone assuming someone else is covering something.

The fix is boring and it works: decide the money rules before you book, not mid-trip. Below are the six that trip couples up most. Most couples get at least two wrong, usually welcome bags and import tax. Read all six, then check your own quotes against them.

## rule 1: guest travel is on your guests, always

This is the one people know but still fumble in execution. Guests pay their own flights and hotels. Always. Your job is to make it easy and affordable, not to pay for it.

Here is the part couples miss: you book the room block, they fill it. A room block is a batch of rooms the hotel holds for your group, usually at a negotiated rate, sometimes with a perk or two. You negotiate it. Your guests each book and pay for their own room inside it. You are not on the hook for empty rooms unless you signed an attrition clause that says otherwise, which is exactly the kind of contract detail worth reading twice.

What you can do to soften the cost for people:

1. Negotiate the room block early, when the hotel has the most inventory to give.
2. Give guests a booking deadline so the block does not expire and prices do not jump.
3. Share flight windows and a couple of realiztic airline options in your invite communication.
4. Be honest in your save-the-date about the ballpark total cost of attending.

That last one matters more than a fancy invitation. People will forgive a lot if they were not surprised.

## rule 2: welcome bags are nice, and completely optional

Welcome bags feel mandatory once you see them all over your feed. They are not. Couples cover these when they do them, and the honest budget is $25-60 a bag. Multiply that by a 40-person guest list and you are looking at $1,000 to $2,400 for something guests will genuinely enjoy for about eleven minutes.

Skip them and nobody notices. Seriously. If you do want them, spend the money where it earns its keep: a printed weekend schedule, local snacks, sunscreen, a rehydration packet, and a small map with your recommendations. Skip the branded stuff nobody packs home.

Treat welcome bags as the first thing you cut if the budget tightens, not the last. They are a want dressed up as a need.

## rule 3: vendor tips run 15-20%, often in cash and local currency

This is the line item that quietly detonates budgets. Tips for local vendors run 15-20% of total vendor cost. On a $15,000 vendor spend, that is $2,250 to $3,000 you need on hand, and it is frequently expected in cash and in the local currency.

Local coordinators, photographers, catering staff, and drivers may not take a card for gratuity, and they may not want a foreign currency they then have to exchange at a loss. So you plan for it:

- Ask each vendor, in writing, whether gratuity is already included in the contract. Some destination packages fold in a service charge, and tipping on top is double-paying.
- Confirm the expected tip percentage for that specific country, because norms vary.
- Withdraw local currency in advance rather than scrambling at a resort ATM with a bad rate and a daily limit.
- Label envelopes by vendor before the weekend so you are not doing math at the reception.

If you want a deeper breakdown of how gratuity should be handled, our [vendor tips guide](/blog/category/vendor-tips) walks through it.

## rule 4: the couple pays for one group meal and one activity, then guests split

Group activities are where the catamaran situation happens. The clean rule: the rehearsal dinner and one group excursion land on the couple. Everything beyond that, guests split.

So you host the rehearsal dinner and, say, one welcome-day beach outing or group dinner. That is your hosting obligation, and it is generous. The optional snorkeling trip, the spa afternoon, the extra bar night, those are guest-funded. This is normal and expected at a destination wedding, where guests have already committed to a multi-day trip.

The only thing you owe here is clarity. Put it in writing in your guest communication:

1. State exactly which events you are hosting.
2. State which activities are optional and self-funded.
3. Give price ranges for the optional stuff so people can budget.
4. Do not let a well-meaning guest "organize something for everyone" without confirming who pays.

Ambiguity is the enemy. A one-line note ("welcome dinner is on us, Thursday's boat trip is optional at roughly $80/person") prevents the entire breakfast-table conversation.

## rule 5: legal filing abroad is yours, not the planner's

Marrying in another country comes with paperwork, and that paperwork costs money. Legal filing abroad runs $300-1,200 depending on where you marry. The couple pays this, not the planner, and not the venue.

Costs swing based on the country's requirements: some places want documents translated and apostilled, some require a minimum residency period before the ceremony, some charge for a certified officiant or a civil registration fee. Every one of those is a line item.

### how to not get surprised by legal fees

- Research the exact legal requirements for your specific country and region, not the country in general. Rules can vary by locality.
- Budget at the high end of the $300-1,200 range until you have confirmed numbers.
- Ask whether your on-paper legal marriage will happen at home before you travel. Many couples do the courthouse part locally and treat the destination ceremony as symbolic, which sidesteps a lot of foreign filing entirely.
- Confirm document deadlines. Apostilles and translations take time you may not have.

If your planner's quote does not mention legal fees at all, that is not because they are free. It is because they are your responsibility and were left off the invoice.

## rule 6: shipping your dress or decor can trigger import tax

Here is the one almost nobody sees coming. If you ship your dress or decor internationally, customs duties can hit 20-40% of the declared value.

Declare a $2,000 dress and a box of decor, and you could face a customs bill of $400 to $800 before you have hung a single string light. This applies to florals, signage, favors, and anything else you send ahead rather than carry.

Ways to keep this from wrecking your budget:

1. Carry your dress with you as a garment bag rather than shipping it, whenever the airline allows.
2. Source decor and florals locally through your coordinator instead of importing them.
3. If you must ship, understand the destination country's duty rules and declared-value thresholds before you send anything.
4. Factor a customs line into your budget from day one, not after the box is already in transit.

Local sourcing usually wins twice: no customs duty, and no risk of your decor getting stuck in a warehouse the week of your wedding.

## the red flags to watch for in your quotes

Most of these six rules go wrong because the cost was invisible in the paperwork, not because someone was being cheap. Watch for:

- A vendor quote that never mentions gratuity. Assume 15-20% is coming and ask if it is included.
- A room block contract with an attrition clause that puts you on the hook for unbooked rooms.
- A "package price" that quietly excludes legal filing fees.
- A planner who says decor is "handled" without clarifying whether it is sourced locally or shipped (and who pays customs).
- Group activities getting organized informally with no one confirming who is paying.
- Welcome bags creeping from a nice-to-have into a four-figure line item.

The through-line: the drama is never the big obvious number. It is the small assumed one.
