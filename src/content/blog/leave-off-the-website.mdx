say you finish building your wedding website on a Sunday night, send the link to your group chat, and feel great about it. by Wednesday, your aunt has forwarded it to three cousins who are not invited, a vendor you are still negotiating with has opened it twice, and someone from your old job has read your story page start to finish. nothing went wrong, exactly. the link just did what links do.

your wedding website is a public page. anyone with the link can read it, and that list includes vendors, uninvited relatives, and people you forgot you knew. password protection helps a little, but passwords get shared along with the url, and most couples hand out the same one to every guest. the safest approach is to assume everything on that site is readable by everyone, then decide what you still want up there.

a few things are better kept off it entirely. here are five, and what to do instead with each.

## 1. your budget

this one feels obvious until you notice how many ways a number leaks onto a public page. a "our story" timeline that mentions what you saved. a FAQ answering why you chose a smaller guest list. a thank-you note to parents that names a contribution. a per-head figure in a shared planning doc you accidentally linked.

guests read it, vendors find it too.

the vendor piece matters more than people expect. if a florist or a photographer can see what you have allocated before they send a proposal, you have handed over the one piece of leverage you had. quotes have a way of arriving right at the number you named. keep the number off every public page, and give vendors a scope instead: guest count, date, hours of coverage, what you actually want. let them price the work, then compare.

your budget is never a detail guests need. it does not help them pick a gift, it does not help them book a flight, and it does not help them dress. it only invites commentary.

### where the number should live instead

keep it somewhere private and structured, where you can compare quotes side by side rather than in a text thread. that is the entire reason we built quote tracking into Altared. you can [add your vendor quotes free](/get-started) and keep the planning side just as tidy, without any of it being crawlable.

## 2. the full venue address

venue city and name is enough on a public url. that is genuinely all a guest needs at the website stage. they are figuring out whether to book a flight, how far the hotel block is, and roughly what the drive looks like. "the barn at x, hudson valley, ny" answers all of that.

the exact street address belongs in the physical invite, not a url that gets forwarded. there are practical reasons:

1. **crashers and drop-ins.** rare, but not imaginary, and the fix costs you nothing.
2. **security and day-of logistics.** some venues specifically ask couples not to publish the address, especially private estates and residences.
3. **wrong-location arrivals.** if your ceremony and reception are in different places, a single published address is the fastest way to send half your guests to the wrong gate.
4. **vendor confusion.** load-in addresses are often different from guest entrances. publishing one creates a second source of truth.

send the exact address in the invite only, or in a details email that goes out to confirmed rsvps a few weeks before. if you want something on the site, a "travel" page with the city, the airport, and the hotel block does the job.

## 3. family drama

no story context, no "as you may know."

the urge here is understandable. if your parents are divorced, if a parent has passed, if someone is not invited and you are worried about how that reads, it feels responsible to explain. a public page is the worst possible venue for that explanation. you are writing to one or two people while hundreds of others read over their shoulder, and the people you are writing to will read it as a public statement, because it is one.

a one-liner like "we eloped" is plenty. short, factual, closed. the same principle applies to a parent who has passed (a simple "in loving memory of" line, if you want one) or a complicated host situation (list hosts by name, skip the relationship labels).

anything that needs context needs a phone call, not a paragraph. and anything you would not want quoted back to you at the rehearsal dinner should not be typed into a page with a share button.

## 4. dress details

your partner, their family, and guests all visit. your website is one of the only planning documents your partner will actually look at, usually because someone asked them a question they could not answer.

so the silhouette, the designer, the alterations saga, the second look for the reception: none of it goes on the site. not in the story page, not in a cute "what to expect" section, not in a photo from the shopping trip. save the silhouette for the day itself.

the version of this rule people forget is the registry link that points to a bridal retailer, or a honeymoon page with a photo of a dress you have not told anyone about. check what your links actually show when someone clicks them.

guests do need dress code guidance, which is a different thing. "black tie optional," "garden formal, grass-friendly shoes," "it will be cold after sunset." that is useful. what you are wearing is not.

## 5. cash requests framed as a disclaimer

no "no gifts, but if you must..." language.

this phrasing is everywhere and it almost always lands worse than silence. it tells guests there is a correct answer, that you have anticipated them getting it wrong, and that you have thought about this more than they have. the longer the disclaimer, the more it reads as a request.

link a fund quietly under the registry tab. a honeymoon fund, a house fund, a charity, whatever it is: list it alongside any physical items with no preamble. guests who want to give cash will find it. guests who want to buy a thing will buy a thing. nobody needs a paragraph explaining your reasoning.

the same restraint applies to "adults only," "we are not doing plus ones," and "please do not post photos." say the rule once, plainly, in the FAQ. do not justify it.

## watch for these

a few things that quietly undo all of the above:

- **a password you share in the same message as the link.** that is not privacy, that is a speed bump.
- **auto-generated story pages.** some website builders prefill a "how we met" section with the details you entered at signup. read what is actually published.
- **search indexing left on.** most builders have a toggle to hide the site from search engines. find it and use it.
- **photo metadata and backgrounds.** a venue sign in the corner of a photo publishes the address you just left off.
- **a registry link that previews your full shipping address.** check how it looks logged out.
- **comments or guestbook features** that let anyone with the link post publicly under your name.

## what the website is actually for

the website's one job is to answer where, when, and how to rsvp. everything else is extra, and extra is where etiquette gets complicated.

a tight site has: date, city and venue name, timing, dress code, travel and hotel info, a short FAQ, and a working rsvp. that is a complete wedding website. it is also, conveniently, the fastest one to build and the easiest to update when something changes.

### the short version

1. keep the budget off every public page, and share scope with vendors instead of a number.
2. publish venue city and name only; the exact address goes in the invite.
3. no story context, no "as you may know." a one-liner is plenty.
4. save the silhouette for the day itself, and check what your links preview.
5. link a cash fund quietly under the registry tab, with no disclaimer attached.
6. turn off search indexing and read your own site logged out before you send the link.
