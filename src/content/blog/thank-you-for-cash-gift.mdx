say it's three weeks after the wedding and you're at the kitchen table with a stack of cards, a pen that's already drying out, and a spreadsheet of who gave what. the gravy boat notes write themselves. the cash gifts do not. you can't say "i love it" and you don't want to say "thanks for the money." so you skip them, and then it's week nine, and the stack has become a thing you feel guilty about every time you walk past it.

cash gifts are the most common and somehow the hardest to write a thank you for. here's a formula that works every time: six lines, one card, and the person on the other end feels genuinely seen. that's the whole job.

## the six-line formula

each line does one piece of work. together they cover every kind of giver, from your aunt who wrote a check at the reception to your coworker who venmo'd you from the parking lot.

1. **name the gift** without saying the amount.
2. **name the use.** say what you're putting it toward.
3. **thank the act** of giving, not the dollar figure.
4. **add a memory.** drop in one real moment from the day.
5. **close warmly,** with something specific to that person.
6. **send it,** and send it sooner than you think you need to.

that's it. you're not writing an essay. you're writing six sentences that prove you noticed who was in the room.

### 01. name the gift

say "your generous gift" not "the money" or "your check." naming the instrument is what makes cash thank yous feel like receipts. "thank you for the check" reads like a bank confirmation. "thank you for your generous gift" reads like a person wrote it.

never write the amount. not in the first line, not anywhere. the number is between you and your spreadsheet. writing it back at someone turns a gift into a transaction and makes the gap between what different guests gave visible in a way nobody wants.

### 02. name the use

"we're putting it toward our first home" lands better than nothing. this is the line people skip and it's the one that does the heaviest lifting, because it turns an abstract gift into a thing that exists in your life.

you don't need a line item. a direction is enough: the honeymoon, the down payment, the new apartment, the savings account you've been building for a while. and if you genuinely don't know yet, say that honestly. "we're setting it aside while we figure out our next step, and it takes real pressure off" is a true sentence and a kind one.

### 03. thank the act

"your thoughtfulness means more than i can say" covers any amount. this is the line that makes one formula work for every giver. your grandmother and your boss can receive the same sentence and both feel correctly thanked, because you're thanking the choosing, not the sum.

it's also the line that protects you from a comparison trap. if you write effusively to the big gifts and briefly to the small ones, people notice. thank the act and the warmth stays level across the whole stack.

### 04. add a memory

one line about the day: "seeing you dance all night made it real." this is the proof-of-life line. it's what separates a note you wrote from a note you copied.

it doesn't have to be big. they drove four hours. they were the first one at the bar. they hugged your mom for a long time. they wore the shoes they complained about all week. if you can't remember anything, look at your photos before you sit down with the cards, or ask your partner, because one of you will remember.

### 05. close warmly

"we can't wait to celebrate with you again soon" keeps it personal. anchor it to something real if you can: the trip you've been talking about, dinner at their place, the baby coming in spring. a closing line that could apply to anyone is a closing line that sounds like a template, and people can feel the difference even if they couldn't name it.

### 06. send by when

within three months of the wedding. two weeks is better. aim for two weeks out, three months is the outer limit most people quietly notice.

the honest reason to move fast isn't etiquette, it's memory. at two weeks you still remember who danced and who cried and who fixed your bustle in the bathroom. at three months you're working from a spreadsheet and the notes start to read like one.

## watch for these

a few things that quietly undo an otherwise good note:

- **writing the amount.** even phrased kindly ("thank you for your incredibly generous gift of..."). don't.
- **the group card.** if four people each gave separately, they each get a card, even if they came together and sat at the same table.
- **copy-paste that didn't get updated.** wrong name, wrong memory, wrong spouse. read each one back before it goes in the envelope.
- **thanking the gift and forgetting the person.** if the note would make sense sent to a stranger, it needs one more line.
- **waiting until you have a "good" card.** a plain note sent at two weeks beats beautiful stationery sent at month four.
- **silence on a gift you're not sure about.** if a venmo came in with no note and you can't place it, ask around. a late thank you is better than none.

## how to get through fifty of them

the stack is the real obstacle, not the wording. a few things that help:

1. **batch by relationship.** all the college friends, then all the parents' friends, then all the coworkers. the tone stays consistent and your brain stops switching gears.
2. **split the list by who knows the person better.** you take yours, your partner takes theirs, you both sign everything.
3. **do ten at a time.** a short session you finish beats a long one you abandon.
4. **keep your guest and gift list in one place** so you're not cross-referencing three screens. if your planning is already organized, this part is twenty minutes a night instead of a saturday.
5. **address and stamp the envelopes first.** then the only thing between you and done is the writing.
