Your ring has been on your finger for about nine minutes. Someone has already sent you a venue account on Instagram. Your group chat is naming your hashtag. An aunt you see twice a year has texted "SO happy for you!!! have you picked a date???"

The moment after the yes, there are a hundred people telling you to start pinning and booking immediately. That is the exact wrong instinct, not because enthusiasm is bad, but because the first few decisions you make stack on top of each other. Make them out of order and you end up redoing them. Book a venue before you know your guest count and you either pay for space you do not need or you cut people you actually wanted there.

Here is the actual order that makes the rest of the planning make sense. Six decisions, in sequence. Most of them cost you nothing but a conversation.

## 1. tell your family before you post

The post can wait a day. Maybe three.

There is a specific kind of hurt that comes from a parent finding out their kid got engaged because a cousin tagged them in a carousel. It is small, and it is also the thing that gets brought up two years later at Thanksgiving. The announcement order that avoids it:

1. Parents (both sides, ideally within the same day so nobody feels like the second call)
2. Siblings and anyone who functions like a sibling
3. Grandparents and anyone who does not use social media and will hear it secondhand otherwise
4. Your closest friends, the ones who will be standing up there
5. Everyone else, which is when you post

If one of you has divorced parents or a complicated family situation, decide together who calls whom before you start dialing. Do not improvise this at 11pm on a high. And if you have a parent who will be contributing financially, this call is not the budget call. Let them be happy first. The money conversation gets its own moment.

## 2. align on budget with everyone who is contributing, before any vendor call

This is the one people skip, and it is the one that causes the most damage later.

Before you email a single venue, you need a real number. Not a vibe. Not "we're thinking something modest." A number, and an understanding of where it is coming from. That means a direct conversation with every person who has hinted they might contribute.

### the questions to actually ask

- How much are you contributing, as a dollar figure?
- Is it a lump sum, or are you planning to pay specific vendors directly?
- When is it available? (Money that arrives in March does not help you pay a September deposit.)
- Does it come with expectations? (Guest list additions, a church, a specific caterer, a say in the date.)

That last one is uncomfortable and you have to ask it anyway. Contributed money frequently comes attached to invitations. A parent giving you a number while also assuming their forty friends from the neighborhood are coming is not a gift of money, it is a gift of money with a guest count attached, and you need to know that now rather than when you are trying to fit 180 people into a room rated for 140.

Write the total down. Then write down what you two can add from savings and what you can realistically save per month between now and the date. That sum is your budget. Everything after this decision is spending against it, which is why you want it tracked in one place from day one instead of scattered across three notes apps and a text thread. ## 3. lock in a rough guest count

Here is the thing nobody tells you clearly enough: guest count is the most powerful number in your entire wedding.

That single number controls your venue options, your catering cost, and your invitation spend. It also quietly controls your rentals, your bar, your favors, your transportation, and the size of your dance floor. Change it by thirty people and you have changed your wedding, not adjusted it.

You do not need a finalized list right now. You need a range, and you need it before you tour venues. Build it like this:

1. Each of you writes a "non-negotiable" list separately. People you would be genuinely sad not to have there.
2. Combine them. That is your floor.
3. Add the lists from anyone contributing money (see decision two, this is why you asked).
4. Add the "probably should" tier: coworkers, extended family, plus-ones for single friends.
5. Assume roughly 10 to 20 percent will decline, but do not plan your venue capacity around declines. Plan around the invited number.

Now you have a range, something like 95 to 130. Carry that range into every venue tour. A room that holds 120 seated is not a maybe for a 130 guest list, it is a no.

If the number that comes out of this exercise does not fit the budget from decision two, this is the moment to resolve it. Not later. One of the two has to move, and it is almost always easier to adjust guest count than to find more money.

## 4. book the venue

Only now. With a budget and a headcount range in hand, you are a buyer who can actually make a decision on a tour instead of a person collecting pretty PDFs.

Popular dates fill 12 to 18 months out. Saturdays in peak season at in-demand venues go first and they go early. If you are planning for 2026 and you have your eye on a September Saturday, you are already in the window where you should be touring, not browsing.

The venue sets every other date on your calendar. Your photographer, your caterer, your band, your hair and makeup, your rentals, your save-the-dates, all of it keys off the date, and you do not have a date until you have a venue. This is why venue-before-everything is not a preference, it is a dependency.

### what to pull apart before you sign

- What is actually included? Tables, chairs, linens, setup, teardown, a coordinator, parking?
- Is there a required caterer or a vendor list you have to use? Exclusive caterers can swing your per-person cost significantly.
- What is the real capacity with your ceremony setup, your dance floor, and your band? Not the theoretical maximum.
- What is the deposit, the payment schedule, and the cancellation policy?
- Is there a service charge or admin fee on top of the quoted rate, and is gratuity separate?

### watch for these

**A quoted "rental fee" that is not the real number.** Service charges of 20 to 24 percent and separate gratuity lines turn a comfortable quote into an uncomfortable one. Ask for an itemized estimate at your guest count before you decide anything.

**Pressure to sign on the tour.** "We have another couple looking at this date" is sometimes true and always used as a closing line. Ask them to hold it for 48 hours in writing. A venue that will not give you two days is telling you how the next 14 months will go.

**Vague inclusions.** If the contract says "setup assistance" without defining it, define it in writing before you sign. More on this in [contracts](/blog/category/contracts).

**Capacity that only works on paper.** Ask to see a floor plan at your number, with the dance floor and the bar where they will actually be.

## 5. pull your marriage license requirements

Once you have a date and a location, look up the marriage license requirements for your county. Not your state generally, your county, because the details vary.

Fees run $25 to $115. Some states require a waiting period of up to three days between when you apply and when the license becomes valid. Some licenses expire after 30 or 60 days, which means applying too early is also a problem. Some counties require both of you to appear in person, some require an appointment weeks out, some want certified copies of documents you do not currently have.

Do not leave this for the week before. A $25 to $115 line item is not what will hurt you. The three day waiting period you discovered on a Thursday is.

Put the application window on your calendar the same day you book your venue. Note the fee, the ID requirements, whether you need an appointment, and whether the office takes cards or only cash.

## 6. collect at least three quotes per vendor category before you sign anything

Three. Photography, catering, florals, DJ or band, hair and makeup, rentals, officiant. Every category, three quotes, every time.

This is not about finding the cheapest option. It is about knowing what the market rate actually is in your area at your guest count on your date. A single quote tells you nothing. Three quotes tell you whether the one you love is priced normally, priced high for a good reason, or priced high for no reason.

Three quotes also give you language. "We're comparing a few photographers in this range" is a completely reasonable thing to say, and it occasionally produces a better package without anyone having to haggle.

Ask every vendor for the same things so the quotes are comparable: hours of coverage, what is delivered and when, travel fees, overtime rate, deposit and payment schedule, cancellation and rescheduling terms. A $3,400 photographer with eight hours and a second shooter is not more expensive than a $3,000 photographer with six hours and no second shooter.

Keep all of it in one place. Scattered quotes are how couples lose track of what they have actually committed to. See more on [hidden costs](/blog/category/hidden-costs) that tend to show up between the quote and the final invoice.

## the short version

1. Tell family before you post. Parents, then siblings, then friends.
2. Align on budget with everyone contributing, before any vendor call. Ask for dollar figures, timing, and expectations.
3. Lock in a rough guest count range. It controls venue size, catering cost, and invite budget.
4. Book the venue. Popular dates fill 12 to 18 months out and the venue sets every other date.
5. Pull your county's marriage license requirements. $25 to $115, and check your waiting period.
6. Collect at least three quotes per vendor category before signing a single contract.
