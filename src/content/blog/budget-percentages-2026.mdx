A couple sat down with three catering quotes that all looked roughly the same on the first page. $85 a head, $92 a head, $88 a head. They picked the middle one because it felt safe. Then the final invoice landed and it was almost 30% higher than the number they had circled, because the quote was food only. Service charge, bar, cake cutting, and vendor meals were all hiding in the fine print, and by the time they saw them, the contract was signed.

That is the exact problem these five percentages solve. Not the ones floating around Pinterest that assume every quote is clean and every line is final. These are the numbers that kept real couples on budget in 2026, and the reason they work is that they account for the fees that actually show up, not the ones the pretty infographics leave out.

Run these five against your total before you book a single vendor, and your whole quote stack starts telling a different story.

## The five numbers, up front

Here they are in order, so you can screenshot this part and move on if that is all you came for:

1. **Venue: cap it at 30%.** Go higher and everything else breaks.
2. **Catering: 35%.** That ceiling has to cover food, bar, service charge, and cake, not just food.
3. **Photography: 10-12%.** Enough for a primary shooter and a second shooter.
4. **Florals: 8% max.** Anything over runs to $10k fast.
5. **Tip reserve: hold back 5% from day one.** $150-200 per vendor is the standard.

Now the part that matters, which is why each one is set where it is and how to check your own quotes against it.

## Venue: 30% is the anchor, not a suggestion

Your venue is the first big number most couples commit to, and it quietly sets the ceiling for everything after it. That is why 30% is the cap and not a target you should be excited to beat by spending more.

Here is the math on a $50,000 wedding. Thirty percent is $15,000 for the venue. That leaves $35,000 for catering, photography, florals, your tip reserve, and the two dozen smaller lines nobody lists on a mood board (attire, stationery, transportation, rentals, an officiant, the marriage license). When the venue creeps to 40%, you are not stealing from one category. You are stealing from all of them at once.

### What to watch for

Venue quotes love to look lean by unbundling. Watch for these getting added back later:

- Ceremony fee charged separately from the reception space
- Required security or day-of staffing minimums
- Cleaning or "damage" fees held as a deposit
- Mandatory in-house or preferred-vendor upcharges

If a venue is right at 30% before those extras, it is probably over budget once they land. Ask for the all-in number in writing before you fall in love with it.

## Catering: 35% is a ceiling, and the quote is just the beginning

This is the line that catches the most people, and it is worth slowing down on. The number a caterer hands you is almost always food only. The 35% ceiling has to absorb everything that clusters around the plate:

- **Service charge**, typically 18-25% of the food and beverage subtotal
- **Bar package**, which can be a flat per-person rate or a consumption tab
- **Cake cutting fee**, charged per slice at some venues
- **Vendor meals**, because your photographer, coordinator, and DJ all need to eat

All of that lives inside the 35% or it busts it. Run the numbers on that same $50,000 wedding: your catering ceiling is $17,500. If the food quote is $12,000, a 22% service charge alone adds $2,640. Add a bar package, cake cutting, and vendor meals, and you are pressing right up against the wall you set.

### How to compare catering quotes fairly

When you have two or three quotes, do not compare the per-head number. Compare the loaded number:

1. Start with the food subtotal.
2. Add the service charge (ask for the exact percentage, not "gratuity is included").
3. Add the bar package or an estimated consumption tab.
4. Add cake cutting and vendor meals.
5. Divide by your guest count for a real per-person figure.

The quote that looked cheapest on page one is often the most expensive once it is loaded. That is the whole point of doing this before you sign, not after. More on reading the fine print in [our guide to hidden costs](/blog/category/hidden-costs).

## Photography: 10-12% buys the coverage you actually want

Photography sits at 10-12%, and that range is deliberate. On a $50,000 wedding that is $5,000 to $6,000, which is enough for a primary shooter plus a second shooter for most of the day. The second shooter is the part couples try to cut first and regret most, because one person cannot be in two places when you are getting ready in separate rooms or when the ceremony and cocktail hour overlap.

Below 10%, you are usually looking at a single shooter, a shorter coverage window, or a package with no edited files included. None of those are wrong if they match what you want. They just are not the same product, so do not compare their prices as if they are.

### What to watch for

- **Coverage hours.** Eight hours versus ten hours is a real price difference dressed up as a small one.
- **Second shooter listed as an add-on**, not included.
- **Print or album rights** sold separately from the digital files.
- **Travel fees** for anything outside a set radius.

## Florals: 8% is the ceiling, and it climbs faster than anything

Florals get an 8% max for a specific reason: this is the category that runs to $10k fast when it is left open. On a $50,000 wedding, 8% is $4,000. That covers personal flowers (bouquets, boutonnieres), ceremony pieces, and reception centerpieces at a reasonable scale.

The jump happens when you add hanging installations, an arch fully covered in blooms, elevated centerpieces on every table, and premium or out-of-season stems. Each one sounds like a small upgrade in the moment. Stacked together, they double the quote without anyone deciding to double the quote.

### How to stay under the ceiling

- Pick two or three statement moments (the arch, the head table, the entrance) and keep everything else simple.
- Ask your florist which blooms are in season for your date. Out-of-season stems are the quiet budget killer.
- Reuse ceremony arrangements at the reception. Your florist can move them during cocktail hour.
- Get the quote itemized by piece so you can see exactly what pushes you over 8%.

## The tip reserve: 5% held back from day one

This is the one that surprises almost everyone, and it is the one that saves the day. Hold back 5% of your total as a tip reserve, from day one, before you sign anything.

The standard is $150-200 per vendor, and that adds up faster than couples expect once you actually count who gets tipped: your photographer, second shooter, coordinator, caterer (often built into the service charge, so check), florist, and DJ. That is a real list of people. On a $50,000 wedding, 5% is $2,500 set aside specifically so tips do not come out of a line that is already spoken for.

Here is the rule that makes this work: **if it is not in the budget before you sign, it comes out of something else.** Couples who skip the reserve end up pulling tip money from catering or attire in the final two weeks, which is the worst possible time to be short.

### What to watch for

- **Service charge is not a tip.** Ask your caterer directly whether staff gratuity is included in that 18-25% or whether it is expected on top.
- **Contracted gratuity** that is already baked in, so you do not double-tip.
- Vendors you forgot to count. Hair, makeup, and transportation drivers often expect a tip too.

## Putting it together on a real budget

Here is all five percentages against a $50,000 total, so you can see the whole picture at once:

1. Venue (30%): $15,000
2. Catering (35%): $17,500
3. Photography (10-12%): $5,000 to $6,000
4. Florals (8%): $4,000
5. Tip reserve (5%): $2,500

That is roughly 88% of your budget accounted for by five decisions, which leaves the remaining 12% for attire, stationery, music (if your DJ is not inside another line), rentals, transportation, and the small stuff that always shows up. If any one of these five blows past its percentage, you now know exactly which of the other lines is about to absorb the hit. That is the entire value of running the numbers first.

## Watch for these across every quote

Regardless of the category, the same tricks show up on every quote stack:

- **"Starting at" pricing** that assumes a smaller guest count than yours
- **Gratuity or service charge described vaguely** so you cannot tell if a tip is included
- **Add-ons presented as standard** (second shooter, floral installs, bar upgrades)
- **Deposits framed as the full cost**, with the balance and fees left off the first page

None of these mean a vendor is dishonest. They mean the first page of a quote is a sales document, not a final invoice, and your job is to load every quote to its real number before you compare.
