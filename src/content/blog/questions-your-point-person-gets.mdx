say it's 2:40 on your wedding day. you're in a room upstairs with your hair half-finished, and downstairs the photographer has just pulled into a lot with no obvious entrance, the florist is circling the block with a van full of centerpieces, and your aunt is asking whether she's in the family photos or not. three people want an answer. all three of them are going to ask the same person, and that person is going to come looking for you.

your day-of point person is going to get questions. from vendors, from family, from the photographer who just arrived and doesn't know where to park. the ones who fumble are the ones who weren't given answers in advance.

that's the whole problem, and it's fixable in about twenty minutes the week before. not by hiring anyone, not by building a forty-tab spreadsheet. by writing down five answers on one sheet of paper and handing it to the person holding the clipboard.

## why a point person needs answers, not authority

a point person (a coordinator, a cousin, your most organized friend, your maid of honor) can only be useful to the degree they know things. "ask the bride" is not an answer, it's a delay, and it's a delay that gets passed to you at the exact moment you can't take it.

the questions they'll get are predictable. that's the good news. vendors ask the same things at every wedding because those are the things that affect whether they can do their job. family ask the same things because they want to be in the right place at the right time. none of them need to be figured out on the day if you write down the answer now.

so: five questions worth answering in advance. when does it start, where do vendors load in, who still needs to be paid, who are the must-have photo groups, and what happens if the weather turns.

## the five questions, and what to tell them

### 01. the timeline: "when does it start?"

this one sounds obvious and it is the one people get wrong most often, because "the wedding" has at least three start times and everybody means a different one. guests hear a start time on the invitation. vendors need a different one. the shuttle driver needs a third.

give a short, specific answer in the same format every time: "doors 3:30, ceremony 4:00." two numbers, no explanation needed. your point person can repeat that sentence to a guest, a driver, a DJ and a grandparent without checking anything.

on the sheet, go one layer deeper. write the arrival time for each vendor, hair and makeup finish time, first look time if you're doing one, the ceremony start, and the time the reception space has to be cleared. if your venue has a hard end time, write it in large letters. that's the number that decides whether the last song happens.

### 02. vendor entry: "where do we load in?"

vendors arriving at a venue they've never worked before will lose fifteen minutes to parking, and those fifteen minutes come out of setup, not out of your timeline buffer. the fix is one line: "side entrance, code 4412."

include the physical directions (which door, which lot, whether there's a service elevator), the gate or door code, the name of the venue staff member who can open things, and whether there are loading restrictions or a time window. if the caterer has to be out of the kitchen by a certain hour, that belongs here too.

### 03. final payment: "are we still owed a check?"

this is the question that causes the most awkward conversation on a wedding day, because the person asking is often standing in front of someone who has no idea. a vendor asking "are we still owed a check?" needs to hear one of two sentences: "paid in full. receipt here," or "yes, envelope with your name on it, point person has it."

so before the day:

1. go contract by contract and write down what's been paid and what's outstanding for each vendor.
2. note how each remaining balance is being paid (cash, check, card, transfer) and when it's due, before or after service.
3. put physical envelopes in order, labeled with vendor name and amount, and hand them to one person.
4. write down who is tipping whom, and how much, so nobody has to guess at midnight.
5. keep the receipts or confirmations accessible, on your phone and printed, so "paid in full" can be proven in ten seconds.

this is also the beat where a lot of couples realize they've lost track of which deposit went where. if that's you, worth reading up on [contracts](/blog/category/contracts) before you build the sheet, because the payment schedule is usually written there in plain language.

### 04. family photos: "who's in the photo groups?"

family photos run long for one reason: nobody knows who's supposed to be standing there. the photographer calls a group, half of it is on the patio, and twelve minutes evaporate.

hand them the printed shot list. not a list on your phone, not a list in a shared doc. paper, in the hand of the person who can yell names across a lawn. the list should name actual people, not categories. "bride's immediate family" means nothing when there are stepparents involved. names do.

group the list in the order the photographer wants to shoot it, usually largest group first so people get released. mark the two or three groupings that genuinely matter to you, the ones where a missed shot would actually hurt. those are your must-have photo groups, and your point person should know which ones they're not allowed to let slide.

### 05. plan b: "what if it rains?"

the question isn't really about rain. it's about who decides and when. the answer your point person gives should be short: "ceremony moves inside."

underneath that, write down the trigger (what weather, decided by what time), the person who makes the call (you, your partner, the venue, the coordinator), and what physically changes: which room, who moves the chairs, whether the ceremony musicians need a different setup, whether guests need to be told and how. if there's a cost attached to flipping the space, you should already know it from your venue contract, so note where that's written down.

## red flags to watch for

- your point person has never seen the venue and has no map, no address, no contact name for venue staff.
- the only copy of the timeline lives in a group chat, which means it lives nowhere once phone batteries start dying.
- you're planning to answer questions yourself "if anything comes up." you won't be reachable. that's not a failure of discipline, it's a dress and a hundred guests.
- nobody except you knows where the vendor envelopes are.
- the shot list exists but hasn't been shared with the photographer in advance, so two different versions are in play.
- a vendor's arrival time appears in an email thread but not in the timeline you printed.

## put it on one sheet

give your point person a single printed sheet with the answers before you leave for the venue. one page, big type, nothing decorative. a second copy for the maid of honor or best man isn't overkill.

then, briefly:

1. write the two-number version of the start time: doors, then ceremony.
2. write the load-in door and the code, plus venue staff contact.
3. list every vendor with paid, outstanding, and tip, and hand over labeled envelopes.
4. print the family photo list with real names, ordered largest group first.
5. write the weather trigger, the decision-maker, and the exact room you move to.
6. hand the sheet over before you leave for the venue, not when you get there.
