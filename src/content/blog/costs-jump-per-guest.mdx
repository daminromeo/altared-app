Your mom calls on a Tuesday. "We should really invite the Hendersons, and their two kids are basically adults now, and honestly it would be strange not to ask your cousin's new fiancé." That is eight people in one phone call, and by the end of the week it is ten. The guest-count conversation almost always happens like this: casually, warmly, over the phone, before anyone has opened a spreadsheet.

Here is the problem. Every time you say "let's just add ten more," the budget hears something very different. Ten guests is not one line item. It is five of them, and they all move at once. None of this is hidden, exactly. It is just that the decision gets made before the numbers get run.

So let's run them. Below are the five costs that jump with every ten guests you add, with the dollar figures spelled out so you can see what "just ten more" actually looks like at the bottom of the invoice.

## 1. Catering scales with every single head

Catering is the most obvious one, and also the one people underestimate the most, because they think of the per-plate number and forget how fast it multiplies.

Per-head cost typically runs $85 to $175 depending on your menu, your market, and whether you are doing plated, buffet, or stations. Multiply that by ten and every batch of new guests adds $850 to $1,750 to your food bill. That is before the service charge, which we will get to, and before any per-person upgrades like a second entrée choice or a late-night snack.

The reason this feels sneaky is that catering is usually priced and discussed early, when your guest list is still a rough estimate. You lock in a per-head rate, feel good about it, and then the list creeps. Every creep is invisible until the final headcount is due, usually two to three weeks out, when you are suddenly writing a much bigger check than the one you budgeted for.

If your list is still moving, price your catering as a range, not a fixed number. Assume it lands at the high end of your guest estimate, not the low end. Lists almost never shrink.

## 2. Florals jump in table-sized chunks

Florals do not scale smoothly the way catering does. They scale in steps, and the step is a whole table.

Ten more guests usually means one or two new tables, and each centerpiece runs $300 to $600. So a single "let's just add ten more" can quietly add $300 to $600 in centerpieces alone, and if those ten guests push you over into two new tables, you are looking at double that.

### Why tables are the hidden multiplier

Most reception tables seat eight to ten people. That means your florist, your rental order, and your catering setup are all built around the number of tables, not the number of guests. Adding four people might cost you nothing extra in florals if they fit at existing tables. Adding six might force an entirely new table, which drags along its own centerpiece, linens, chargers, and place settings.

This is why the same "ten guests" can cost wildly different amounts depending on where your current count sits. If you are at a clean 100 (ten tables of ten), adding ten means one full new table and everything on it. If you are at 94, you may absorb some of those guests before the table math tips over.

The practical move: ask your florist to break out the per-centerpiece cost separately from the rest of the floral package. That way you know exactly what one more table costs before you commit to it.

## 3. Stationery adds up per invite, per menu, per place card

Stationery feels small. It is paper. How much can paper be? More than you think, because every guest touches multiple printed pieces.

Ten more seats adds $80 to $200 to your print costs, and that covers the whole chain of paper each guest generates:

1. **The invitation** and its inner envelope, response card, and details insert
2. **The menu card** at their place setting
3. **The place card or escort card** telling them where to sit
4. **The favor tag or program**, if you are doing them
5. **The seating chart**, which has to be reprinted if your table layout changes

Each of these is priced per unit, and the per-unit cost is highest at low quantities. If you have already sent your order to the printer and then add guests, you are placing a second, smaller order, and small reprints almost always cost more per piece than the original run. Some stationers charge a minimum order fee for reprints on top of the per-piece cost, which is where the $200 end of the range comes from.

## 4. The bar tab climbs on consumption pricing

If you are running a consumption bar, meaning you pay for what your guests actually drink rather than a flat per-person package, then every guest is a variable, not a fixed cost.

Consumption bars charge per pour, and ten guests can add $200 to $400 to the final tab depending on how much they drink and what they order. A crowd of enthusiastic cocktail drinkers costs more than a table of one-glass-of-wine relatives, which is exactly why consumption pricing is so hard to predict in advance.

### Consumption vs. flat rate

The trade-off looks like this. A flat per-person bar package gives you certainty: you know the number before the wedding. A consumption bar can save you money if your crowd drinks lightly, but it can also blow past your estimate if they do not. The more guests you add, the more that uncertainty compounds. Ten light drinkers might cost you $200. Ten heavy pours at a late reception might cost you $400 or more, and you will not know which until the final bar total lands days after the wedding.

If your list is still growing and you are on a consumption bar, budget for the top of the range. It is far better to be pleasantly surprised than to get a bar invoice that is double what you planned.

## 5. The service charge compounds on all of it

Here is the one that quietly makes every other number bigger: the service charge.

Most caterers and venues apply an 18 to 24% service charge on top of your catering (and often your bar) total. It is not a tip, and it is usually not optional. And because it is a percentage, it compounds every single one of the increases above.

Walk through the math. Say you add ten guests at the middle of the catering range, roughly $130 per head. That is $1,300 in food. Apply a 21% service charge and you are actually paying about $1,573 for those ten people, just on catering. Stack the $300 to $600 in florals, the $80 to $200 in stationery, and the $200 to $400 bar tab on top, and "let's just add ten more" can land anywhere from $1,400 to well over $2,500 all in.

That is the whole point. The service charge is applied last, so it feels like an afterthought, but it is a multiplier on the biggest line item you have. Adding guests does not just add costs. It compounds them.

## Red flags to watch for

A few things should make you slow down before you say yes to another ten:

- **A quote that lists a per-head rate but buries the service charge.** Always ask what the service charge percentage is and what it applies to. If it hits both catering and bar, your real per-person number is much higher than the menu price.
- **A florist who quotes a total package without breaking out per-centerpiece cost.** You cannot make a smart guest-count decision if you do not know what one more table costs.
- **A consumption bar with no estimated range.** A good bar vendor can tell you roughly what a crowd your size tends to spend. If they will not give you a ballpark, budget for the high end yourself.
- **Reprint minimums on stationery.** Ask upfront what a small reprint costs. Adding six guests after the order is placed can trigger a fee that dwarfs the paper itself.
- **"We can always add them later."** You can, but every vendor prices late additions higher. The cheapest guest to add is the one you never added.

## What to do before you add one more name

The fix is simple, and it is the same advice from the caption that probably brought you here: run the math first, while your list is still moving. Once you know the true per-head number, the guest-count conversation gets a lot easier, because "let's just add ten more" becomes "let's just add $1,573 more," and that is a decision instead of a reflex.

Here is your quick checklist:

1. **Get your true per-head number**, catering plus service charge, not just the menu rate.
2. **Ask your florist for the per-centerpiece cost** and know where your table math tips over.
3. **Confirm your stationery reprint policy** before you send the first order.
4. **Budget your bar at the top of its range** if you are on consumption pricing.
5. **Apply the 18 to 24% service charge to everything** it touches, then decide.
6. **Do this before the phone call**, not after, so you are negotiating from real numbers.
