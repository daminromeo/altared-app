A couple sits down with a spreadsheet and a favor they love: small jars of local honey, $5 each. 120 guests. $600. Done, they think, and move on to the seating chart.

Six weeks later the real number is $2,880. Nothing went wrong. Nobody upgraded. The honey still cost $5. What happened is that five other lines showed up after the first one, each of them small enough to feel like a rounding error and big enough, together, to quadruple the total.

the favor is never the expensive part. it's the box, the ribbon, the custom tag, the tote, the hotel drop-off fee, and the 15% buffer order you didn't plan for. by the time all six lines hit, most couples are spending $20-35 per guest on something they originally quoted at $5.

This isn't an argument for skipping favors. Plenty of couples do them and are glad they did. It's an argument for knowing where the number actually comes from, so you can decide what's worth it and what to cut before the invoice arrives instead of after.

## the six lines that stack up

Here's the full breakdown, in the order they usually hit your inbox:

1. **The favor itself.** Most couples budget $3-6 per guest. Actual spend lands $8-14.
2. **Packaging.** Boxes, ribbon, tissue, and tags add $2-5 per guest on top.
3. **The welcome bag.** The tote alone runs $4-9 each before a single item goes in.
4. **Custom tags.** Printed custom tags or cards cost $1-3 per guest, and minimum orders apply.
5. **Shipping and drop-off.** Hotel delivery or venue drop-off fees run $1-4 per bag.
6. **The buffer.** Order 10-15% extra for breakage and late RSVPs, or reorder fees hit harder.

Add the low end of every line and you're at roughly $16 per guest. Add the high end and you're past $35. For 120 guests, that's the difference between a $1,900 line item and a $4,200 one, on a category most couples mentally file under "small."

## line 1: the favor costs more than the sample

The gap between the $3-6 you budgeted and the $8-14 you spend usually isn't a change of heart. It's three predictable things.

**Minimums push you up a tier.** The $4 candle you priced has a 200-unit minimum. You need 120. You either buy 200 (and your per-guest cost is now $6.67 for the ones you actually use) or you switch to a smaller-batch maker who charges $9 each.

**Personalization is a separate SKU.** The base price is the base product. Etching, printing, engraving, or a custom label is almost always an add-on, frequently $1.50-3 per unit, sometimes with its own setup fee.

**You upgrade once you hold one.** The sample arrives and it feels cheap in your hand. Nobody wants to hand 120 people something that feels cheap. So you move up a tier, which is a legitimate choice, but it's the moment $5 becomes $11 and it usually happens after the budget's already written.

### how to hold the line here

Price the version you'd actually be proud to hand someone, not the cheapest listing that matches the vibe. Ask every vendor two questions in the first email: what's the minimum order, and what does personalization add per unit. Those two answers tell you your real per-guest number before you've committed to anything.

## line 2 and 4: packaging and tags, the invisible doubler

Packaging is where the math gets genuinely funny. Boxes, ribbon, tissue, and tags add $2-5 per guest on top of the favor. On a $5 favor, that's a 40-100% increase to hold the thing you're giving away.

And it compounds. A rigid box is $1.80. Satin ribbon at 18 inches per box is $0.40. Tissue is $0.25. A kraft tag is $0.30. Twine is $0.10. None of those numbers scare anyone individually. Together they're $2.85, and you haven't printed anything yet.

Custom tags are their own line because they behave differently. Printed custom tags or cards cost $1-3 per guest, and minimum orders apply, which is the part that bites. Most printers won't run 130 tags. They'll run 250 or 500. If your minimum is 250 at $0.90 each, you've spent $225 for 130 usable tags, which is $1.73 per guest, not $0.90.

### the swaps that actually save money

- Skip the box, use a ribbon and a tag. Saves $1.50-2 per guest with almost no visual loss on a table.
- Use one design printed on a larger sheet and cut it down yourself. Most print shops charge by the sheet, not the tag.
- Put the personalization on the tag, not the product. A generic candle with a custom tag reads as custom and costs half as much as an engraved one.
- Let the escort card be the tag. One printed item doing two jobs is the single best packaging trick there is.

## line 3: welcome bags are a second budget wearing a trench coat

Here's the thing couples consistently miss: the welcome bag is not part of the favor budget. It's a parallel budget with the same six-line structure.

The tote alone runs $4-9 each before a single item goes in. Canvas with a printed monogram lands at the top of that range. And then you fill it, because an empty tote is worse than no tote. Water, a snack, something local, a printed itinerary card. Even a restrained bag is $12-18 filled.

If you're doing both favors and welcome bags, you're running two per-guest lines, and the welcome bag one usually only applies to out-of-town guests. That's the one piece of good news here. If 40 of your 120 guests are traveling, you're buying 40 bags plus a buffer, not 120.

A real example: 40 bags at $6 for the tote is $240. Fill them at $10 each and that's $400. Hotel delivery at $3 per bag is $120. Buffer of 15% is another $115. Total: $875 for a detail most guests will leave in the hotel room. Worth it for some couples. Absolutely not worth it for others. The point is deciding on purpose.

## line 5: drop-off fees nobody quotes you

Hotel delivery or venue drop-off fees run $1-4 per bag. This is real, it's common, and it almost never appears in an early conversation because it comes from the hotel, not from you or your favor vendor.

Hotels charge it because a staff member has to receive, store, sort, and deliver bags to specific rooms. Some charge per bag. Some charge a flat handling fee plus per bag. Some waive it if you've blocked enough rooms. Venues sometimes charge a similar fee to receive and store shipments before the wedding date.

Ask three questions when you book your room block, not the week of:

1. Is there a per-bag delivery or handling fee, and what is it?
2. Can we drop bags at the front desk for guests to pick up instead (often cheaper or free)?
3. How early can shipments arrive, and is there a storage fee?

That last one catches people. If your favors ship to the venue ten days out and the venue only accepts deliveries 48 hours out, you're paying for storage somewhere, or paying to reroute a pallet.

## line 6: the buffer you will need

Order 10-15% extra for breakage and late RSVPs, or reorder fees hit harder.

Things break in transit. Guests you counted as no show up as yes. You discover the morning of that eleven tags got smudged. If you ordered exactly 120, you're now paying a rush fee plus expedited shipping plus a second setup charge on a print job, which can easily cost more than the original buffer would have.

On 120 guests, a 12% buffer is about 14 extra units. At $10 each, that's $140 you planned for instead of a $300 emergency reorder. Build it in as a line, not a hope.

## red flags to watch for

- **A quote that says "starting at."** That's a base product with no personalization, no packaging, and no shipping. It is not a price.
- **No minimum order listed anywhere.** It exists. Ask before you fall in love.
- **Setup or plate fees on printed items.** Common on tags, cards, and anything foiled. Get it in writing per design, because a second design means a second setup fee.
- **Shipping calculated at checkout only.** Favors are heavy and bulky. A $600 order can carry $150 of freight you won't see until the final screen.
- **A hotel that says "we'll take care of it."** Get the per-bag number in the room block agreement. "We'll take care of it" is not a rate.
- **Assembly time treated as free.** 120 boxes with ribbon and tags is a real four to six hour job. It's not a dollar cost, but it's the night before your wedding.

## before you order anything

- Write all six lines into your budget as separate items: favor, packaging, tote, tags, shipping/drop-off, buffer.
- Ask every vendor for minimum order quantity and the per-unit cost of personalization in your first email.
- Get the hotel's per-bag delivery fee in writing when you book the room block, not the week of.
- Cut the box before you cut the favor. Packaging is the easiest $2-5 per guest to reclaim.
- Make one printed item do two jobs (tag plus escort card) instead of printing two.
- Build in the 10-15% buffer as a planned line, because reorder fees hit harder.
- Count welcome bags for traveling guests only, and price the tote and the contents separately.

The goal isn't a cheaper wedding. It's knowing that your favors cost $22 per guest before you've committed to them, so you can say yes on purpose or spend that money somewhere guests will actually remember.
