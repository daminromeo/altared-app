Two couples sit down with the same $18,000 budget. One is planning for 60 guests. The other is planning for 20. By the time they both book their venue, caterer, florist, and cake, the second couple has more than $11,000 still sitting in the account, and it is not because they cut corners. It is because a micro wedding is not a compromise. It is a budget decision that shows up in six very real line items.

Most of the "should we do a micro wedding" advice online talks in vibes. Intimate. Cozy. More meaningful. All true, and all useless when you are staring at a spreadsheet trying to figure out what size actually makes sense for your number. So let's skip the vibes and run the numbers. Here is exactly where the money moves when you go from 60 guests to 20, line by line, with the dollar figures attached.

## the six line items where guest count shows up

Your guest count is not one decision. It is a multiplier that touches almost every quote you collect. These are the six places it hits hardest:

1. the venue rental
2. the per-head catering bill
3. the floral order
4. the paper suite
5. the cake tier
6. the tip envelope stack at the end of the night

Add those up and switching from 60 guests to 20 saves roughly $2,500 on venue, $6,000 on food, $2,000 on florals, $600 on stationery, and $700 on cake. That is over $11,000 before you factor in fewer vendor hours and smaller gratuity totals. The savings are not hypothetical. Here is how each number gets built.

### 01. venue cost

A room that seats 150 costs more to rent than a room that seats 25, full stop. Micro venues (a restaurant back room, a small garden, a gallery, an Airbnb with a permit) average around $2,000. Full banquet halls and event spaces built for a crowd run $6,000 to $10,000 once you add the mandatory minimums.

That is a $2,500-plus gap on the low end, and it comes with a hidden bonus: smaller venues usually skip the food-and-beverage minimums that big halls bury in the contract. A hall that "only" charges $6,000 to rent may also require you to spend $10,000 on catering through their preferred vendor. A 20-person space rarely does.

### 02. per-head food

This is the biggest number on the list, and it is the one couples underestimate most. Catering is priced per head, so it scales directly with your guest list. Take 20 guests at $150 a head. That is $3,000. A comparable 60-person dinner at the same $150 plate is $9,000. Cutting 40 guests saves $6,000 on food alone.

And $150 a head is a mid-range plated dinner, not a luxury one. If your caterer quotes $200 or $250 per person, the gap gets wider fast. This is why the guest list is the single most powerful lever in your entire budget. Trimming names is not petty. It is the highest-leverage financial move you can make.

### 03. floral budget

Florals scale with tables. Fewer tables means fewer centerpieces, and centerpieces are where floral quotes quietly balloon. Going micro typically cuts $1,500 to $3,000 off centerpieces alone, call it $2,000 in the middle.

You still get your bouquet, your boutonnieres, maybe a statement piece behind the couple. You just are not paying to fill fifteen tables with fresh arrangements that get thrown out at midnight. Fewer tables is fewer of the most repeated, most expensive item on the florist's invoice.

### 04. paper suite

Stationery is one of those costs that feels small per unit and gets big in bulk. Printing, addressing, and mailing 150 invitation suites runs about $800 by the time you add save-the-dates, RSVP cards, envelopes, and postage. Twenty invites cuts that same stationery spend to under $200.

That is a $600 swing on paper. It is also less time addressing envelopes, less postage, and fewer RSVP cards you have to chase down. Small guest list, small paper trail.

### 05. cake tier

Cake is priced by the slice, so it follows the same logic as catering. A two-tier cake for 20 runs $300 to $500. A cake big enough to serve a full 60- to 150-person crowd runs closer to $1,200. Call it a $700 difference.

You are not sacrificing the cake moment. You are just not paying for three extra tiers of structural buttercream that exist only to feed people you barely see. A smaller cake can actually be nicer per bite, because the baker is spending the same effort on fewer servings.

### 06. vendor tips

Here is the line most couples forget entirely, and it runs in both directions. Fewer guests can mean fewer vendors, and gratuity adds up per person. But a micro wedding does not automatically mean a small tip stack. Tipping 20 vendors instead of 10 adds $500 to $1,000 that most couples miss.

So the tip envelope is a savings line only if your micro wedding actually uses fewer vendors. If you keep the full team (planner, photographer, videographer, DJ, two servers, a bartender, a hair-and-makeup duo, a day-of coordinator) the guest count dropping does not shrink the envelope stack. More on that trap below.

## where couples still overspend on micro weddings

This is the part nobody puts on the pretty slideshow. A micro wedding saves money by default, but it is very easy to hand those savings right back. Watch for these:

- **Upgrading every single detail because the guest count feels small.** This is the number one leak. You think, "it's only 20 people, so I can splurge on the $400 plate, the imported blooms, the four-tier cake." Do that across every line and you have recreated a 60-person budget for a 20-person event. The savings only exist if you let them stay saved.
- **Forgetting that a shorter vendor day still comes with standard tipping rates per person.** A four-hour micro wedding does not get a discount on gratuity. Your photographer is still tipped at their standard rate, not a prorated one. Budget the full tip.
- **Per-person minimums hiding in the venue contract.** Some smaller-looking venues still enforce a food-and-beverage floor. If the minimum assumes 50 guests and you have 20, you are paying for 30 empty seats. Read the [contract](/blog/category/contracts) line before you sign.
- **Assuming fewer guests means fewer vendors.** It usually does not. Twenty guests still want good photos, good food, and good music, so the vendor list barely changes. Your savings come from the per-head and per-table items, not from cutting the team.
- **Chair, rental, and delivery fees that do not scale down.** A delivery fee is a delivery fee whether it is for 20 chairs or 200. Do not expect every line to shrink proportionally.

The rule of thumb: a micro wedding shifts your money from quantity to quality. That is the whole point. But if you upgrade quality *and* keep quantity-level spending everywhere, you have missed the trade.

## how to actually keep the $11,000

The savings are sitting in your quotes right now. You just have to see them side by side to believe them. The fastest way to do that is to price your real guest count against a bigger one and watch the six line items move.

Here is a clean way to run it before you lock your list:

1. Get quotes with the price *per head* and *per table* spelled out, not just a lump sum.
2. Model two guest counts (your target number and one 40 people higher) using those per-unit prices.
3. Subtract. The difference across venue, food, florals, paper, cake, and tips is your real micro-wedding savings.
4. Decide which of those savings you keep and which one splurge you spend it on. One upgrade, not six.

## the short version

Save this before you set your guest list. Going from 60 guests to 20 moves real money:

- **Venue:** micro venues average $2,000 vs. $6,000 to $10,000 for full halls, roughly $2,500 saved.
- **Food:** 20 guests at $150 a head saves $6,000 vs. a 60-person dinner.
- **Florals:** fewer tables means $1,500 to $3,000 less on centerpieces, about $2,000 saved.
- **Paper:** 20 invites vs. 150 cuts stationery from $800 to under $200, a $600 swing.
- **Cake:** a two-tier for 20 runs $300 to $500 vs. $1,200, about $700 saved.
- **Tips:** fewer vendors saves, but tipping 20 vendors instead of 10 adds $500 to $1,000 if you keep the full team, so budget the full rate.
