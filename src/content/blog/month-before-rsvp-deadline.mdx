say your invitations went out six weeks ago, your RSVP deadline is the 15th, and right now your reply count is sitting at roughly half the list. you check the mailbox, you refresh the online form, you tell yourself people always reply late. that is technically true and also not a plan.

the month before your RSVP deadline isn't just waiting around. it's four weeks of specific moves in a specific order, and skipping one means scrambling later. here is the whole sequence, with what each step is actually protecting you from.

## why order matters more than effort

you'll do all six of these things eventually. the problem is doing them out of order. if you chase silent guests before you've confirmed every invitation actually arrived, you're nudging people who never got mailed anything. if you set your cutoff before you've asked your caterer when they need the final headcount, you've picked a date out of thin air that may be days off from the one that matters.

sequence turns a month of low-grade anxiety into four short tasks. that's the whole trick.

## 4 weeks out: check the mailing

confirm every invite went out. resend returns.

this sounds obvious until you actually audit it. pull up your guest list and go name by name, not household by household. you're looking for three categories of problem:

1. **invitations that were never addressed.** the ones that got set aside because you couldn't find an apartment number, and then the pile moved.
2. **invitations that came back.** returned mail is the single most common reason a guest "ignored" you. reissue those immediately with a corrected address.
3. **invitations sent to an outdated address.** no return envelope, no reply, no signal. if a guest has moved in the last year or two, assume the address you have is stale and confirm it directly.

if you're sending digital RSVPs, the equivalent audit is checking bounces and spam. a wedding invitation from an unfamiliar domain is exactly the kind of email that quietly lands in a junk folder.

### do this step even if you're confident

the cost of checking is twenty minutes. the cost of not checking is finding out in week three that eleven people never heard from you, and now you're compressing a six-week reply window into eight days.

## 3 weeks out: count replies

list who answered and who hasn't yet.

make two columns. answered, not answered. that's it. the useful part is not the number of yeses, it's the clean, named list of silence.

keep it somewhere you can update quickly, because you'll touch it three more times in the next three weeks. a shared doc, a spreadsheet, or a planning tool, whatever you'll actually open. if you're keeping your [budget](/blog/category/budgeting) and vendor notes in one place already, keep the guest count there too so your headcount and your money live next to each other.

### what to log alongside the name

- how you invited them (mail, email, text)
- whether you've already confirmed the address
- who in your family or wedding party is the natural person to follow up

that third column saves you later. some guests will respond to a text from you and some will only respond to their cousin.

## 2 weeks out: ask catering

call your caterer and get the exact date they need your final headcount.

not "about two weeks before." not what you remember from the tasting. the exact date, in writing, from the person who will actually be cooking. some caterers need the number earlier than your invitation deadline implies. some will let you adjust upward but not downward after a certain point.

while you're on the phone, ask these too:

1. what date do you need the final headcount?
2. can the number go up after that date, and by how much?
3. can it go down, and what happens to the cost if it does?
4. do you need a separate count for kids, vendor meals, or dietary restrictions?
5. how do you want to receive it (email, portal, form)?

then do the same quick check with anyone else whose job depends on the number: rentals, bar, cake, transportation, favors. your caterer's date is usually the earliest and strictest, so it becomes the anchor for everything else. if any of this is new territory, our [contracts](/blog/category/contracts) posts cover what to pin down in writing.

### while you have a minute, draft the nudge

this is step four and it belongs here, in the same sitting, because it takes four minutes and future you will not be in the mood.

draft the reminder text now: *"rsvp is due the 15th. coming?"*

that's the whole message. short, warm, no apology, no paragraph about how much it would mean to you. a guest who hasn't replied is not withholding, they're busy and the card is under a stack of mail. make the ask take five seconds to answer.

write one version for text, one for email, and leave a blank for the name. done.

## 1 week out: send the nudges

send it to everyone still silent, not just close family.

this is the step people half-do. they text their siblings and their college roommates and skip the uncle they feel weird bothering, or the coworker they're not sure they should have invited in the first place. those are exactly the people who will show up unannounced or decline the day before.

send it to the full silent list. same message, same day, no editorializing. if someone finds the reminder rude, that is information, and it is cheaper to learn it now than at the door.

### watch for these red flags

- **a caterer who won't give you a firm headcount date.** vague answers here mean the real deadline is whatever is convenient for them later. get it in writing.
- **plus-ones replying for people you never invited.** address this the day it happens, not on deadline day.
- **a big silent block from one side of the family.** that's usually a mailing problem, not a rudeness problem. check the addresses before you check the attitude.
- **guests who reply "probably" or "we'll try."** that is a no for headcount purposes until they say yes in writing.
- **your own urge to extend the deadline.** extending the deadline teaches everyone the deadline isn't real, and it compresses every downstream task.

## deadline day: set the cutoff

close the list, update your budget number, and send vendors whatever count you have.

all three parts of that matter. closing the list means you stop counting, even though three more replies will trickle in. updating your budget means your money reflects reality instead of your original estimate. sending vendors the number means the people who need it have it, on time, from you.

whatever count you have is the right count. a headcount sent on the agreed date, slightly imperfect, is worth far more to your caterer than a perfect number sent four days late. late additions get handled as exceptions. a late headcount gets handled as a problem.

## the sequence, saved

1. **4 weeks out:** confirm every invite went out, resend returns.
2. **3 weeks out:** list who answered and who hasn't yet.
3. **2 weeks out:** get the exact date your caterer needs the final headcount.
4. **same sitting:** draft the nudge text now so you're not writing it stressed later.
5. **1 week out:** send it to everyone still silent, not just close family.
6. **deadline day:** close the list, update your budget number, send vendors the count you have.
