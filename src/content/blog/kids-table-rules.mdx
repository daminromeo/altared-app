say it's eleven days out and you're finally sitting down with the seating chart. the adults fall into place fast, because you've been thinking about those table combinations for months. then you hit the nine children on your guest list and you make a snap call: one round table, stick it near the back, done. it takes you four minutes.

those four minutes are the reason a seven year old spends your first dance crying into a napkin three feet from a subwoofer, and the reason your caterer's final headcount email has a line item you never actually approved.

the kids table is an easy seating decision to leave until the last week, and it's the one that can quietly derail the whole reception flow. here are the four things that actually matter, in the order you should handle them.

## rule 01: group by age range, not by family

the instinct is to seat cousins together because they're related. related is not the same as compatible. a table with a three year old, a six year old and a fourteen year old has no shared activity, no shared conversation and no shared attention span, and the oldest kid ends up doing unpaid childcare at your wedding.

seat kids 4-12 together. that's a wide band, but it's a band where a table of kids will mostly entertain each other: they'll color, they'll compare desserts, they'll get up and dance as a group when the floor opens.

### under 4s stay with parents, always

this one isn't a preference, it's a safety and sanity rule. under 4s stay with parents, always. toddlers need someone cutting food, managing a cup, and doing the bathroom run. if you seat a two year old at a kids table, you have not created a kids table, you've created a situation where one parent hovers behind a chair all night and doesn't eat.

### what about teenagers

thirteen and up usually do better at an adult table, ideally with a parent or a cool aunt and a seat with a view of the dance floor. they'll be mortified by a kids table and they're old enough to find it insulting. ask the parents if you're unsure. it takes one text.

## rule 02: name one adult, in writing

a kids table with no designated grown-up is a table that gets managed by whoever happens to notice the spill. that's usually your mom, your maid of honor, or you.

assign one grown-up to sit nearby. not at the kids table, nearby, close enough to see it and respond without being stuck in a tiny chair all night. then tell them before the day, in writing.

in writing matters because a hallway conversation at the rehearsal dinner is not a plan. send a text or an email that includes:

1. the fact that you're asking them specifically, and that you'd love a yes or a no (a no is fine, you just need to know)
2. which kids are at the table and roughly how old they are
3. where the table sits in the room, and where they're sitting
4. who each child's parent is and where that parent is seated, so they can hand off fast
5. what "covered" means for the night, so expectations match

you can also hire this out. some couples bring in a sitter or a kids' attendant for the reception hours, and some venues have a quiet side room that works as a wind-down space. ask your venue what they allow and what it costs, and get the all-in number before you assume it's affordable.

## rule 03: ask the venue where the table lands relative to the speakers

this is the question almost nobody asks, and it's the one that saves you the most grief.

ask the venue: "where is the kids table relative to the speakers?" wedding sound systems get loud in a way that's genuinely painful for small ears. a table that looked fine on the floor plan can end up directly in front of a stack of speakers once the DJ or band loads in and decides where to set up.

while you're asking, cover the rest of the geography:

- how far is the kids table from the nearest bathroom
- is it in a service path where staff are carrying hot plates
- can the parents actually see it from their seats
- where exactly do the speakers go, and does that change between dinner and dancing
- is there a corner that's slightly quieter where the table could live instead

get the answers from the person who runs events there, not from the floor plan PDF. floor plans show tables. they do not show where sound actually hits. if your venue has a walkthrough slot, bring the seating chart with you and point at the spot.

## rule 04: confirm a plain, simple kids menu before the final headcount is due

children's palates at a formal dinner are not a mystery. tell the caterer "plain pasta, no sauce" and you have solved most of the table. butter on the side, sauce on the side, nothing composed, nothing with a surprise ingredient in the middle of it.

the timing matters more than the menu. confirm a plain simple kids menu with your caterer before the final headcount is due, because that's the deadline when the numbers lock and changes start costing you. you want the kids meals counted, priced and written down before anyone is rushing.

kids meal pricing depends on the venue and catering package, so it's worth confirming that line in your contract too. ask whether a kids meal is a reduced-price plate or a swap at full price, whether under 4s are charged at all, and whether the kids count goes toward any food and beverage minimum. those three answers can move your catering total meaningfully, and they are all answerable in one email. more on what else hides in catering paperwork over in [hidden costs](/blog/category/hidden-costs).

## red flags to watch for

- **"we'll just figure out the kids meals on the day."** you will not. the kitchen prepares what's on the count sheet, and day-of substitutions either don't happen or get billed.
- **a caterer who won't put the kids meal price in the contract.** if it's a real price, it can be written down. if it's verbal, it's not a price, it's a vibe.
- **a floor plan with the kids table and the DJ booth on the same wall.** flag it before the final layout gets approved.
- **a named adult who hasn't actually confirmed.** silence is not a yes. follow up until you get the word back.
- **a parent who assumes their under-4 has a seat at the kids table.** clarify early and kindly, so nobody discovers it at the escort card display.
- **a kids headcount that doesn't match your RSVP list.** count the children by name, every time. "about eight or nine" is how you end up paying for a plate nobody eats.

## the short version

1. seat kids 4-12 together, and keep under 4s with their parents.
2. assign one grown-up to sit nearby, and tell them before the day, in writing.
3. ask the venue where the kids table lands relative to the speakers, and move it if the answer is bad.
4. confirm the plain kids menu and the per-child price with your caterer before the final headcount deadline, and get that line into the contract.

none of this takes long. it takes one email to the caterer, one question to the venue, one text to an adult you trust, and a slightly slower pass at the seating chart. do it when you're building the chart, not in the last week.
