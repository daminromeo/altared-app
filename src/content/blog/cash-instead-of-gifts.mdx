A couple I'll call Maya and Dev sent out 120 invitations with a small card tucked inside that read "monetary gifts appreciated." They got a house full of blenders, three matching photo frames, and roughly a third of the cash they'd hoped for. The problem wasn't their guests. Their guests were generous. The problem was the ask: it was in the wrong place, worded in a way that made people wince, and routed through a platform that skimmed a cut off every dollar that did come in.

Asking for cash instead of gifts is completely normal now. But most couples word it wrong, put it in the wrong place, or lose hundreds of dollars to platform fees before a single dollar reaches them. The good news is that fixing this is not complicated. It comes down to five rules you can set up in an afternoon, before your invites go out.

## Rule 1: Never say "cash"

The word "cash" does something strange to people. It reads as transactional, a little cold, and it makes a warm gesture feel like a fee. So don't use it.

Instead, name the fund something specific: "honeymoon fund," "home down payment," "adventure fund," or "first-home fund." When couples name the fund something concrete instead of just asking for cash, guests give around 30% more. The reason is simple. "Cash" is an abstraction, but "two nights in Kyoto" or "the closing costs on our first place" is a story your guests want to be part of.

The specificity matters more than you'd think. A honeymoon fund that lists actual experiences (a snorkeling trip, a nice dinner, one splurge night at a hotel) gives your guests something to picture. They are not handing you money. They are buying you a memory, and that feels good to do.

### A quick wording test

Read your fund description out loud. If it sounds like an invoice, rewrite it. If it sounds like you're inviting someone into a plan, you've got it right.

## Rule 2: Keep it off the invitation

This is the one that trips up the most couples, and it's the one Maya and Dev got wrong. Never put the fund in the invitation itself. No insert card. No line at the bottom. No "in lieu of gifts" note nestled next to the RSVP.

Traditional etiquette still holds here for a reason: the invitation is about inviting people, not about what they should bring. Registry information, including a cash fund, lives on your wedding website. Guests know to look there. If they don't, the people close to you (your parents, your wedding party) can pass the word along.

Wording the fund only on your website does two things at once. It keeps the invitation gracious, and it gives you space to explain the fund properly, with tiers and a short, warm note, instead of cramming it onto a card in six words that come off blunt.

## Rule 3: Watch the fees

Here is where couples quietly lose real money. Most cash registry platforms and payment apps take a processing cut, and on a wedding-sized total, that adds up fast.

The rough breakdown:

1. **PayPal** takes about 2.9% per transaction.
2. **Venmo business** takes about 1.9%.
3. **Zelle** takes zero.

Run the math on a realistic number. Say your guests contribute $8,000 to your honeymoon fund. At PayPal's 2.9%, that's $232 gone to processing. At Venmo's business rate of 1.9%, it's $152. Through Zelle, you keep the full $8,000. That $232 is a night at the hotel you were fundraising for in the first place.

A note on the "friends and family" question: peer-to-peer payment apps sometimes let personal transfers go through fee-free, but the moment a transaction gets flagged as a goods-or-services payment, the fee kicks in, and you don't control that flag. Zelle sidesteps the whole issue because it moves money bank-to-bank with no processing layer.

### When a paid platform is still worth it

Some couples happily pay a small percentage for a polished honeymoon-registry platform because it handles the tiers, the thank-you tracking, and the pretty photos automatically. That's a fair trade if the convenience saves you hours. Just go in knowing the number. Read the fee disclosure before you commit, and price out what that percentage costs on your expected total. ## Rule 4: Set the amounts for them

The most common piece of guest feedback about cash funds is that people don't know how much to give. A blank "contribute any amount" box creates decision paralysis, and paralysis usually means people give less, or default to a physical gift instead.

Fix it with tiers. Offer clear giving amounts so guests do not have to guess:

- **$50** toward a nice dinner on the trip
- **$100** toward a night's stay
- **$250** toward a bigger experience or a chunk of the down payment

Guests give more when you remove the guesswork. Attaching each amount to something specific makes it even easier, because now the $100 tier isn't just a number, it's "a night in the city we're honeymooning in." You can still leave a custom-amount option open for people who want to give more or less. The tiers are a suggestion, not a gate.

The goal is to make giving feel easy and intentional, not awkward. Tiers do exactly that. They turn "how much is appropriate?" into a two-second choice.

## Rule 5: Still keep a small physical registry

Do not go all-cash. Keep 8 to 10 physical gifts listed alongside your fund.

There's a practical reason and a generational one. The practical reason: some guests genuinely will not send cash, no matter how warmly you word it. They want to hand you something they picked out. The generational one: older guests statistically skip cash funds. Your grandmother may find a Zelle request confusing or impersonal, and she would much rather buy you the nice towels or the Dutch oven.

Eight to ten items is the sweet spot. Mix a few affordable pieces (kitchen basics, a good set of glasses) with one or two aspirational items for the guests who like to go big. This gives everyone a comfortable way to participate, whether they're most at home tapping a button or walking through a store.

## Watch for these red flags

Before your invites go out, run through this list. These are the mistakes that quietly cost couples money and goodwill:

- **A fund labeled "cash gifts" on your website.** Rename it something specific. You're leaving roughly 30% of generosity on the table with that one word.
- **Any registry line printed on the invitation.** If it's on the paper, take it off. Website only.
- **A default to PayPal without checking the fee.** That 2.9% is real money. Confirm your route before you share it.
- **A single open-ended "contribute" button.** Add tiers. Guesswork lowers giving.
- **A no-physical-registry policy.** You'll lose the guests who only give tangible gifts, which often includes the older relatives who tend to give generously.
- **A fund that reads like an ATM.** If your description has no warmth and no story, guests feel like they're paying a bill, not celebrating you.

## Putting it together before invites go out

None of this requires a planner or a spreadsheet marathon. Set the whole thing up in one sitting:

1. **Name your fund something specific.** Honeymoon, home, adventure. Never "cash."
2. **Put it only on your wedding website.** Keep the invitation clean.
3. **Route the money through Zelle** to avoid the 2.9% (PayPal) or 1.9% (Venmo business) cut, or price the fee before choosing a paid platform.
4. **Build three tiers**, like $50, $100, and $250, each tied to something real.
5. **List 8 to 10 physical gifts** so every guest, including the ones who skip cash funds, has an easy way to give.

Guests are generous. They want to help you start your life together. Your only job is to make giving feel easy and intentional, not awkward, and these five rules do exactly that. Set them up once, get the wording and the fees right, and the difference shows up as real dollars in your account instead of a stack of duplicate frames.
