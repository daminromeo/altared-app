Picture the Tuesday three weeks before your wedding. You've paid your deposits, signed every contract, and told yourself the budget is basically locked. Then the caterer emails about the final headcount. Your seamstress mentions a rush fee. Someone in the group chat asks how guests are getting from the ceremony to the reception. By Friday you've quietly added four figures to a number you thought was finished.

The quote you signed three months ago is not the number you'll pay. The last 30 days are where budgets actually break, and almost none of it is in any contract. It isn't vendors being dishonest. It's that the final stretch moves fast and the line items pile up so quietly you don't notice until you're adding up the damage after the honeymoon.

Here are the seven places it happens, roughly in the order they'll come for you, plus what each one actually costs.

## 1. Gratuity: 15-20% on top of every contract

This is the big one, and it's the one people forget most reliably because it never appears on a single invoice. Gratuity runs 15-20% across every vendor, and "every vendor" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Caterers, bartenders, hair and makeup, your coordinator, drivers, sometimes your photographer and DJ. It stacks.

Run the math on a mid-size wedding. If your catering bill is $12,000, an 18% tip is $2,160 for that one line alone. Add tips across hair, makeup, transport, and bar service and you can clear $3,000 in gratuity that lived nowhere in your planning spreadsheet.

A few notes that save you money and awkwardness:

1. Check whether a service charge is already gratuity. Some catering contracts bake in a 20-22% service charge that partly or fully covers staff. Ask directly: "does this service charge go to the team, or is a separate tip expected?"
2. Tip on the pre-tax subtotal, not the tax.
3. Owners of a business (the photographer who runs the studio) don't expect a tip the way hourly staff do. A kind review or referral counts.

Build a gratuity line into your budget the day you sign, not the week you pay. It's the difference between planning for $3,000 and getting surprised by it.

## 2. The final headcount: caterers reprice at the 14-day cutoff

Most catering contracts have a final headcount cutoff around 14 days out, and here's the part that stings: it almost always reprices up. You guessed 120 when you signed. RSVPs came in at 134. Plus-ones you didn't expect, kids you assumed weren't coming, the college roommate who confirmed late.

At a $95 per-plate rate, going from 120 to 134 guests is 14 extra plates, or $1,330, decided in a single afternoon at the two-week mark. And that per-head increase drags gratuity and rentals up with it, because now you need more chairs, more linens, more staff.

You usually cannot go down after the cutoff. If someone drops out at day 10, you've already paid for their seat. So the move is to be honest with your estimate early rather than optimistic. Track RSVPs against your contracted minimum weekly starting at 45 days out, and assume a handful of late yeses rather than late nos.

## 3. Add-on florals: 30-40% above the original quote

Florals are where "just one more thing" gets expensive. Last-minute stems run 30-40% more than your original proposal. You see the venue in person, decide the head table looks bare, want two more centerpieces, an arch upgrade, petals down the aisle. Every one of those is a fresh order at a fresh price.

Why the premium? Your florist quoted around a wholesale flower order placed weeks out. Adding blooms inside two weeks means rush sourcing, sometimes substitutions when a variety isn't available, and extra labor the night before. A $2,500 floral proposal that grows by 35% is $875 you didn't plan for, on flowers that wilt in 48 hours.

If you know you're the type to want more once you see the space, tell your florist now and ask for a "flex" line in the proposal. Locking the design early is almost always cheaper than loving it late.

## 4. Rush alterations: $200-500 in the final week

Your dress arrives, or your body changes, or you lost weight in the stress of the last month (it happens more than you'd think). Rush fittings in the final week add $200-500 to the bill on top of standard alterations, and standard alterations already run several hundred.

The trap is the timeline. Bridal alterations typically need multiple fittings across 6-8 weeks. Start late, or discover a fit problem at your final fitting, and every stitch becomes a rush stitch. A bustle redone under deadline, a hem taken up when there's no room to schedule a second appointment, all of it prices at the panic rate.

### how to dodge it

Book your first fitting the moment your dress arrives. Bring the exact shoes and undergarments you'll wear so nothing changes later. If you're between sizes or planning a body change, tell your seamstress up front so the plan accounts for it. The cheapest alteration is the one that isn't a rush.

## 5. The day-of team: a coordinator booked late runs $800-1,500

Somewhere around week three, the reality lands that no one is actually running the day. Not you. Not your mom. Not the venue coordinator, who works for the venue, not for you. So you go looking for a day-of coordinator, and a coordinator added late costs $800-1,500 with zero time to compare.

That price isn't the problem. A good coordinator is one of the highest-value people at your wedding. The problem is booking under pressure. When you have three weeks, you take whoever's available, at whatever they quote, without checking references or comparing two other options. You lose all your leverage precisely when you need it.

If you're reading this early, book coordination as a first-tier decision, not a last-minute patch. If you're already inside the window, still ask each candidate for two recent references and a clear timeline document, because a coordinator who can't produce those in a day probably can't run yours either. ## 6. Overtime hours: $150-300 per extra hour, in cash

The reception is glorious. Nobody wants it to end. So it doesn't, and your photographer and DJ keep working past their contracted stop time. Photographers and DJs bill $150-300 per overtime hour, usually in cash, on the night.

This is the sneakiest one because you're not thinking about money at 10:45pm. But two extra hours across a photographer and a DJ can be $600-1,200, decided emotionally, paid immediately, and impossible to compare in the moment.

Read the overtime clause in every timed contract before the day. Know the per-hour rate, decide in advance whether you're willing to extend, and if you might, bring cash so you're not scrambling. Better still, build the timeline so the party peaks inside your contracted hours and the last song lands on schedule.

## 7. Guest transport: shuttle minimums of $600-900

Around week two, someone raises the transport question. The venue is 20 minutes from the hotel block, guests are drinking, parking is limited. So you add a shuttle. And shuttle minimums most couples add at week two cost $600-900 they weren't tracking.

The minimum is the catch. Shuttle companies charge for a block of hours (often 4-5 hour minimums) whether you use them all or not, plus gratuity for the driver on top. A single round-trip you imagined at $300 becomes $750 once the minimum and tip are in.

If guest transport matters (and for a venue far from lodging, it does), price it while you still have room to compare two or three companies. Splitting a shuttle cost per guest, or arranging it as part of a hotel block deal, can soften the number a lot when you're not booking in a panic.

## the red flags to watch for

The final 30 days have a specific texture. Learn to recognize when a "small" ask is actually a budget event:

- Any sentence that starts with "it's just one more." One more centerpiece, one more hour, one more guest. Those are the line items that pile up quietly.
- A vendor who won't put a rush fee or overtime rate in writing before the day.
- A "service charge" with no clear answer on whether it's gratuity.
- A headcount you're rounding up "to be safe" the week before the cutoff. Safe for the caterer, expensive for you.
- Any major role (coordination, transport) you're solving with less than three weeks and only one quote.

None of these mean someone's cheating you. They mean the countdown is doing what countdowns do.

## your last-30-days checklist

Save this part. Before you book anything else, make sure you've accounted for:

1. Gratuity at 15-20% across every vendor, budgeted the day you sign.
2. A final headcount buffer, tracked weekly, before the 14-day catering cutoff.
3. A floral flex line so add-ons don't hit at 30-40% above quote.
4. Alterations booked early enough to avoid the $200-500 rush fee.
5. A coordinator chosen with time to compare, not the $800-1,500 panic hire.
6. Overtime rates ($150-300/hour) read in advance, with a plan and cash ready.
7. Guest transport priced against the $600-900 minimum while you can still shop it.

The quote you signed isn't the number you'll pay, but it can be a lot closer if you find these before they find you.
