say it's the morning of your wedding. you're in a hotel room with curlers in, someone's steaming a jacket in the bathroom, and your point person walks in and asks who's supposed to take the rented arbor back. you don't know. you knew three weeks ago. right now you know approximately nothing, because your brain has decided today is not a day for logistics.

that's the scene this checklist exists to prevent.

your day-of point person can only execute what you actually give them. whether that's a hired coordinator, your venue's event manager, or the one friend who color-codes her own grocery list, the quality of their day depends entirely on the quality of the handoff. and the handoff should happen seven days out, printed, in a folder, in their hands.

the morning of the wedding is not the moment to brief anyone on anything.

## why seven days, and why on paper

a week out is the sweet spot. final headcounts are in, the ceremony order has stopped changing, and your vendors have confirmed arrival windows. it's also still early enough that if your point person reads through and says "wait, who's getting the cake from the venue fridge?", there's time to figure it out without a panic text.

print it. one hard copy, not a shared link. venue wifi is unreliable, phones die, and the person running your day should not be scrolling a google doc with one thumb while holding a bouquet. a physical folder they can flip through, write on, and hand to someone else is worth more than the prettiest spreadsheet.

do a walkthrough together when you hand it over. fifteen minutes of talking it through out loud catches more gaps than an hour of them reading it alone.

## the 7 things

### 1. master timeline

print one hard copy, every vendor arrival time included. not just "ceremony 4pm" but when the florist pulls up, when hair and makeup needs to be done, when the band loads in, when the shuttle leaves the hotel, and when the photographer's coverage ends.

the arrival times matter more than the event times. your point person's job for the first half of the day is mostly opening doors for people and telling them where to put things. if a vendor shows up and nobody knows they were coming, that's a timeline problem, not a vendor problem.

### 2. vendor contacts

name, cell, and what they're there to do. the third part is the one people skip, and it's the one that saves you. "mobile bar, arriving 3pm, needs the side door and a table for the ice" tells your point person what to look for. a bare name and number tells them nothing when someone's standing at the loading dock asking questions.

include the person who will actually be on site, not just the company owner you signed with. those are often different humans. if you've been tracking all of this in [Altared](/get-started) as quotes came in, you can pull the whole list straight from your vendor records instead of digging through eight months of email.

### 3. vendor tips

pre-labeled envelopes, who gets each one, and when. do this at the kitchen table on a quiet night, not in the car on the way to the venue. write the vendor's name on the front of each envelope, seal them, and put them all in one labeled bag or box that goes in the folder or straight to your point person.

then write down the when. some people get handed theirs at load-out, some at the end of the reception, some before they leave mid-afternoon. your point person shouldn't have to guess, and you shouldn't be doing math in a gown.

### 4. seating chart

final guest count plus any dietary notes per table. your caterer needs the counts, but your point person needs the map, because they're the one a guest will find when they can't locate their name.

annotate it. mark the tables with the vegetarian plates, the gluten-free, the allergy that actually matters. mark the grandparent who needs the chair closest to the aisle and the two relatives who should not be seated together. your point person can quietly solve a seating problem if they know it exists in advance.

### 5. family roles

who walks where, who speaks, who needs a mic. processional order, recessional order, who's lighting a candle or pouring something or holding something. toast order with full names (your point person does not know which uncle is which, and shouldn't have to ask).

flag mic handoffs specifically. the mic is a surprisingly common place for a wedding to go briefly sideways: three toasters, one microphone, nobody holding it between speeches.

### 6. end-of-night

who takes the gifts, florals, and rented items home. write it as assignments with names attached, not as a vague category. the card box is the obvious one, but also:

1. gifts and the card box, with the named person taking them and where they're going
2. florals, including what the venue composts and what someone wants to keep
3. rentals, with the pickup time or the drop-off address and who's loading them
4. your personal items (shoes, signage, leftover favors, the guest book)
5. the top tier of the cake, if you're keeping it
6. anything borrowed that belongs to a specific person who will notice it's missing

assign a name to every line. "someone will grab it" is how a card box ends up in a locked venue overnight.

### 7. the script

exact words for: "photography is closed" and "please be seated." wording matters more than you think.

these are the two moments where a well-meaning person improvises and accidentally sounds rude to fifty guests. give your point person the sentence you want said. something warm and clear that moves people without making anyone feel scolded. the same goes for last call, for the shuttle departure, for asking guests to put phones away during the ceremony if that's your preference.

writing it down also protects your point person. they're delivering your words, not their own, which makes the awkward announcements much easier to make.

## watch for these

a few red flags that your handoff isn't going to hold up:

- **your point person has never seen the venue.** if they can't picture where the side door is, the timeline is abstract to them. a walkthrough or at minimum a photo tour helps.
- **you're the only phone number on the vendor list.** if every vendor was told to call you, your phone becomes the day's bottleneck. tell vendors in writing who to contact instead.
- **the timeline still has a "TBD" on it a week out.** TBD at seven days means unresolved at zero days, which means someone improvising.
- **you're asking a member of the wedding party to also run logistics.** someone standing at the altar can't be reachable at the loading dock. if that's your only option, give them a backup name for the ceremony hour.
- **nothing is printed.** a link is not a handoff.
- **your point person doesn't know they're the point person.** this happens more than you'd think. say it out loud, confirm it, and make sure vendors know the name too.

## the short version

- hand everything over seven days before, printed, in one folder
- master timeline with every vendor arrival time, one hard copy
- vendor contacts: name, cell, and what they're there to do
- pre-labeled tip envelopes, who gets each one and when
- final seating chart with dietary notes per table
- family roles: who walks where, who speaks, who needs a mic
- end-of-night assignments with a name on every item
- the script, written word for word
- walk through the folder together instead of mailing it and hoping
