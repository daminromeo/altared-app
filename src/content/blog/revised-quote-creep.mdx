A couple books a caterer in March off a clean, tidy quote: $110 a head, 100 guests, a number that fits the spreadsheet. In August, the signed contract comes back at nearly a third more. Nobody lied to them. The guest list crept to 118. A service charge appeared. Sales tax got calculated on top of that service charge. The photographer added a travel line that wasn't in the first draft. Same vendors, same wedding, different document.

Here's the thing most people don't find out until they're already emotionally committed: the quote you got and the contract you sign are two different documents. The first quote is a starting point, not a final number. Quotes are living documents, and most couples don't know to compare version one against the final contract line by line.

None of what follows is hidden on purpose. It's just how vendor pricing works, and it moves in predictable directions. Once you know the five places a quote tends to grow, you can read every revision like a pro instead of getting surprised at signing.

## 1. Guest count reprices everything per head

The single biggest driver of quote drift is your guest count, because so much of a wedding is priced per person. Catering reprices per head, often $85-200 more a person, depending on your menu and market. That's not a fee you can negotiate away. It's just math tied to a number that almost always goes up between your first estimate and your final RSVP count.

Think about how a guest list actually behaves. You start with a rough number. Then a parent adds cousins. Then plus-ones get confirmed. Then a few "maybes" become "yeses." Every one of those additions runs through the per-head rate.

### A quick example

Say you quoted 100 guests at $110 per person. That's $11,000 for catering in draft one. Your list settles at 118 guests. At the same rate, that's now $12,980. You didn't upgrade the menu or add a station. You added 18 chairs, and the quote grew by nearly $2,000 before any other line moves.

The lesson: lock a realistic guest range early, and ask your caterer what the per-head price is at the low end and the high end of that range. A quote built on 100 when you'll realistically host 120 isn't a real quote. It's a hopeful one.

## 2. The service charge shows up in revision two

This is the line that catches the most people off guard, because it often isn't on the first quote at all. Service charges (18-24%) show up in revision two once the subtotal is higher. It's added on the new subtotal, so it scales with every other increase.

A service charge is not a gratuity, and it's not tax. It's a percentage the vendor adds to cover operations, staffing, and overhead. What makes it feel sneaky is the timing and the base it's calculated on. When your subtotal grows because of guest count, the service charge grows too, because it's a percentage of that larger number.

Run it forward on the catering example above. Your subtotal is now $12,980. Add a 22% service charge and that's another $2,855.60. So a quote that started around $11,000 is now sitting near $15,835 before we've even touched tax, and we've only changed two lines.

Watch for a service charge that's listed as a range or as "to be determined." Pin down the exact percentage before you sign, and ask in writing whether it applies to the food and beverage subtotal only or to the whole invoice including rentals. Those are very different bills.

## 3. Overtime is the fee you agree to at 11pm

Your contract has an end time. What it doesn't have is a crowd that wants to leave on schedule. Add-on hours are where quotes grow after the fact, and they run real money: overtime runs $150-400 per vendor per hour past contract end.

The trap is that overtime is a per-vendor charge. If the reception runs one hour long and you've got a band, a photographer, and a coordinator all still on the clock, you're not paying one overtime fee. You're paying three.

### How the math stacks

1. Band overtime at $350 an hour
2. Photographer overtime at $250 an hour
3. Coordinator overtime at $150 an hour

That's $750 for a single extra hour, decided in the moment, when you're the least equipped to make a budget call. Multiply if the party really wants to keep going.

Before you sign, do three things. Confirm each vendor's exact hourly overtime rate in the contract. Ask what your realistic buffer is (does packing up count as billable time?). And decide in advance what your hard stop is, so a fun night doesn't quietly cost you $750 you never planned for. ## 4. Travel fees appear quietly before signing

This one is easy to miss precisely because it's small on the page and often absent early. Travel fees that weren't in draft one appear quietly before signing, showing up as either $0.67/mile or a flat hotel block.

Photographers, planners, bands, and officiants who serve a wide area frequently charge for the distance to your venue. Sometimes it's mileage. Sometimes it's a flat fee. Sometimes, for a destination or a late-ending event far from where the vendor is based, it's a hotel night you're expected to cover.

A mileage line at $0.67 a mile doesn't sound like much until you add up a round trip, sometimes for a rehearsal and the wedding day both, across several vendors. A flat hotel block for two or three vendors can add a few hundred dollars that simply weren't visible in your first estimate.

Ask every vendor whose base is more than a short drive from your venue: is travel included, and if not, how is it calculated? ## 5. Tax on tax inflates the final line

Save this one for last because it's the most counterintuitive. In many places, sales tax lands on top of the service charge, so you're taxed on a fee. Tax on tax. Sales tax applied after service charge inflates the final line.

Here's why it matters. Your service charge is treated as part of the taxable amount, so tax is calculated on the subtotal plus the service charge, not just the subtotal. You end up paying tax on money that was itself a fee, not a good or service you chose.

Back to our running example. Subtotal of $12,980, plus a 22% service charge of $2,855.60, gives a taxable base of $15,835.60. Apply an 8% sales tax to that whole figure and you add $1,266.85. If tax had only applied to the original subtotal, you'd have paid about $228 less in tax. The difference is entirely tax being charged on the service charge.

This is legal and standard in a lot of markets. The point isn't that anyone is cheating you. The point is that your true final number is meaningfully higher than the subtotal your first quote showed, and this stacking effect is why.

## Red flags to watch for in a revised quote

When a new revision hits your inbox, don't just look at the bottom line and gasp. Compare it against the previous version line by line. These are the signals worth pausing on:

- A service charge that appears for the first time in revision two, or one still written as a range instead of a fixed percentage
- Per-head pricing that doesn't match the guest count you actually gave them
- Overtime rates that are vague or missing entirely (silence here is expensive later)
- Travel or hotel lines that showed up quietly with no prior conversation
- A subtotal that looks identical but a final total that jumped, which usually means tax on tax or an added fee you skimmed past
- Any line labeled "TBD," "estimated," or "additional charges may apply" without a cap or a formula

If a vendor can't or won't explain how a number is calculated, that's your answer. Good vendors are happy to walk you through every line, because they'd rather you sign clear-eyed than dispute a bill later.

## The takeaway: track every version, not just the last one

None of these five increases means a vendor is being dishonest. It means quotes evolve, and the couples who stay on budget are the ones who read each revision like a document, not a formality. Here's the short version to act on:

1. Build your quote on a realistic guest range, and ask for pricing at both ends of it.
2. Pin the exact service charge percentage and what it applies to before signing.
3. Get every vendor's overtime rate in writing and set a hard stop for the night.
4. Confirm travel fees ($0.67/mile or a flat hotel block) in the quote, not after.
5. Expect sales tax to land on top of the service charge, and budget for that final line.
6. Compare version one against the final contract, line by line, every time.
