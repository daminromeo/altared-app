Picture the group chat three days before the wedding. Someone's aunt just found out the bridesmaid dresses weren't "covered." The groom's mom assumed the officiant tip was on the bride's parents. Two people are typing at once, and nobody has actually paid anyone yet.

None of this is because the rules changed. It's because nobody said them out loud early enough. Who pays for what at a wedding has a surprisingly clear answer for most line items. The trouble is that these expectations get quietly assumed and then loudly argued about the week of the wedding, usually over a text thread nobody wanted to be in.

Here are the 7 rules families still get wrong in 2026, with the actual dollar figures attached so you can check them against your own quotes before anyone writes a check.

## The couple's column: what falls on you two

Start with the items that are unambiguously yours. These are the ones people try to hand off, and they almost never move.

### 1. The venue deposit

The couple pays the venue deposit. Traditionally, and still in 2026. This is usually the first big check anyone writes, and it's the one that sets the tone for the whole budget. If a parent offers to cover it, wonderful, but the default assumption is that it comes from the couple.

Why this trips people up: the deposit often gets confused with "the parents are paying for the wedding." Even in situations where one family is contributing a lump sum, the venue deposit tends to be the piece the couple locks in themselves, because they're the ones signing the contract. Whoever signs is on the hook, which is worth remembering before you volunteer someone else's name.

### 5. The officiant fee

The couple pays the officiant. Budget $300 to $800, plus a tip if they traveled to you. This one gets missed because so many couples have a friend or family member officiate, which makes the whole category feel free. It usually isn't. Even a friend officiant typically involves a thank-you gift or a real tip, and a professional officiant is a flat fee that belongs to the couple.

If your officiant drove two hours each way or flew in, the tip is not optional in most people's etiquette. Add it to your officiant line, not your "maybe" pile.

### 7. Vendor tips

The couple tips, and it's $50 to $200 per vendor, not on the invoice. This is the single most-forgotten number in wedding budgeting, and it deserves its own beat because it's the one that quietly wrecks the final week.

Vendor gratuity never shows up in any quote. Your photographer's contract says one number. Your caterer's invoice says another. Neither includes the tip you're expected to hand over on the day. Multiply $50 to $200 across your photographer, second shooter, DJ or band, hair and makeup, catering staff, and delivery drivers, and you're looking at hundreds to over a thousand dollars that lives entirely outside your signed paperwork.

Here's how it stacks up on a fairly normal vendor list:

1. Photographer: $100 to $200
2. DJ or band: $50 to $200
3. Hair and makeup: 15 to 20 percent of the service
4. Catering staff: often built in as a service charge, but confirm, because a service charge is not always a tip
5. Delivery and setup crews: $10 to $20 each
6. Officiant: $50 to $100 if not already a flat professional fee

Read every contract for the phrase "service charge" and ask, in writing, whether it goes to the staff or to the company. If it goes to the company, you're still tipping on top. That one question can save you from double-paying or from stiffing a team that assumed you knew the difference.

## The groom's column: the parts that still surprise people

Two classic items sit with the groom or his family, and both get quietly reassigned every year.

### 2. The rehearsal dinner

The rehearsal dinner is the groom's family. It averages $1,200 to $4,000 total, depending on headcount and how fancy the room is. This is one of the oldest splits in the book, and it's still one of the most commonly fumbled, because the groom's side sometimes assumes the couple is "handling everything" now that couples pay for so much of their own weddings.

They're not wrong that the old model has loosened. But when nobody names the rehearsal dinner specifically, it lands on the couple by accident, on top of everything else they're already covering. Naming it early, calmly, means the groom's family can plan for a $1,200 taco spread or a $4,000 sit-down without it becoming a surprise the week of.

### 3. The bride's bouquet

The groom pays for the bride's bouquet. It's traditionally part of his florals budget. This one feels almost quaint until you see the invoice, because bridal bouquets are frequently the most expensive single arrangement of the day. It's an intimate, old-school gesture that's easy to forget exists as a line item.

You don't have to keep this tradition. Plenty of couples pool all their florals into one budget and split it however makes sense. The point isn't that you must do it the 1987 way. The point is that if you're assuming tradition, both people should be assuming the same tradition, so the florist's deposit doesn't spark a "wait, I thought you had that" conversation.

## The everybody-else column: guests and the wedding party

The last two rules aren't about the two families at all. They're about the people you invite, and they cause the most awkwardness because nobody wants to say them out loud.

### 4. Bridesmaid dresses

Each bridesmaid pays her own dress. The average runs $150 to $300. This is standard, and it's still what most bridesmaids expect, but it's also where feelings get complicated fast. Someone in your party may be stretched thin, and someone else may assume you're covering it because you chose the dress.

The kind move is to be clear up front. Tell your party what the dress runs before they say yes. If you want to cover it for someone, or cover it for everyone, that's a generous choice, but it should be a choice you make and announce, not a gap someone discovers at the fitting.

### 6. Guest travel

Guests pay their own flights and hotels. Always. This feels obvious written down, and yet destination weddings and out-of-town venues blur it constantly. A guest who has to fly and book two hotel nights may quietly expect a room block discount or a shuttle, and that's reasonable to provide. What isn't standard is the couple footing anyone's airfare.

Where couples get into trouble is over-promising. "Don't worry about it, we've got you" said warmly at an engagement party can turn into a real expectation later. Be generous where you can, with blocks and shuttles and welcome details, and be honest that the travel itself is on each guest.

## Red flags to watch for before anyone pays

A few warning signs that the money conversation is about to go sideways:

- Someone says "we'll figure it out later." Later is the week of the wedding, and later is when it explodes.
- A contract includes a "service charge" and nobody has confirmed whether it's actually a tip. Assume it isn't until you're told otherwise.
- A parent is "already volunteering opinions on the budget" but hasn't been asked to contribute a specific dollar amount to a specific line. Opinions and contributions are different currencies.
- Anyone assumes a friend officiant means zero cost. It rarely does.
- Your florals, catering, and photography quotes are being compared without adding the invisible tip layer on top. Your real total is always higher than the sum of the quotes.

Any one of these is your cue to have the calm version of the conversation now, once, in advance, instead of the loud version at the rehearsal.

## Have the conversation once, calmly

None of these rules are new. They're just the ones that get assumed instead of said. Knowing them before anyone writes a check means the whole thing happens once, quietly, and never comes up again. Here's the short version to send to the family group chat:

1. Venue deposit: the couple pays.
2. Rehearsal dinner: the groom's family, roughly $1,200 to $4,000 total.
3. Bride's bouquet: the groom, from his florals budget.
4. Bridesmaid dresses: each bridesmaid, $150 to $300 each.
5. Officiant fee: the couple, $300 to $800 plus a tip if they traveled.
6. Guest travel: guests pay their own flights and hotels, always.
7. Vendor tips: the couple, $50 to $200 per vendor, never on the invoice.

The one that will save you the most stress is the last one, because it's the number that hides in plain sight.
