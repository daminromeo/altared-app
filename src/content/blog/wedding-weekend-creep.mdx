A couple books a $40,000 wedding. They celebrate. They tell everyone the number. Then, six weeks before the date, the welcome dinner quote lands, the rehearsal dinner deposit is due, the venue mentions a brunch minimum, and the hotel emails about "unfilled room liability." Suddenly the real spend is closer to $52,000, and nobody moved the wedding budget at all. The wedding was one line item. The weekend was six more.

That gap is not bad luck, and it is not overspending in the impulsive sense. It is a documentation problem. The quote for the wedding and the quote for the weekend are two very different documents, and most couples only ever ask to see the first one. Here is what quietly doubles the budget before you notice, with the numbers written down so nothing surprises you at signing.

## The wedding is one line item. the weekend is six more.

Think of the weekend as its own mini-event calendar. A modern wedding is rarely a single afternoon anymore. It is often a Friday welcome dinner, a rehearsal, the ceremony and reception, a Sunday brunch, and all the logistics wrapped around out-of-town guests staying two or three nights. Each of those touchpoints has its own vendors, its own per-head math, and its own contract.

The six that do the most damage, roughly in the order they hit your inbox:

1. Welcome dinner
2. Rehearsal dinner
3. Day-after brunch
4. Hotel block attrition
5. Welcome bags
6. Vendor overtime

None of these are surprises if you see them written down before you sign. Let's price each one.

## Welcome dinner and rehearsal dinner: the two dinners that grow

### Welcome dinner ($85-150 per head, and the list always grows)

The welcome dinner is the friendliest budget trap because it starts small and honest. You picture 30 close people, a relaxed vibe, drinks and a family-style spread. Then the math starts moving. At $85-150 per head, 30 guests is $2,550 to $4,500. Reasonable.

But the welcome dinner list always grows. Once you are inviting the wedding party plus partners, then the parents' close friends, then the cousins who flew in and would feel excluded, you are at 60 people fast. Sixty guests at that same range is $5,100 to $9,000. Same menu, same venue, double the invoice, and it happened one polite addition at a time.

The fix is to set the welcome dinner headcount as a hard number before you send a single invite, and to price both your target count and your realistic ceiling. If $150 per head at 60 people is a dealbreaker, you want to know that in month two, not in the final week.

### Rehearsal dinner (10-15% of your total budget on its own)

This is the one that shocks people, because it is expressed as a percentage rather than a per-head number. The rehearsal dinner runs 10-15% of the total wedding budget on average. On a $40,000 wedding, that is $4,000 to $6,000 for one dinner the night before.

Two things make this creep. First, the rehearsal dinner is often paid by a family member who plans it semi-independently, so it never gets folded into the main budget spreadsheet. Second, the guest list overlaps heavily with the welcome dinner, which means you can accidentally cater nearly the same group of people twice in two days. If your weekend has both a welcome dinner and a rehearsal dinner, decide early whether they are genuinely different events or the same event wearing two names. Combining them is one of the cleanest ways to protect the budget without cutting a single guest.

## Brunch, bags, and the hotel block: the three that hide in the fine print

### Day-after brunch (you budget $20, venues charge $55-90)

The Sunday brunch is where expectation and reality drift furthest apart. Couples budget $20 a head, picturing pastries, coffee, and a fruit tray. Venues charge $55-90 a head, because the moment it happens on their property with their staff, it becomes catered service with a food-and-beverage minimum, service charge, and gratuity stacked on top.

Run the numbers on 50 guests. Your mental budget: $1,000. The venue's quote: $2,750 to $4,500. That is a $1,750 to $3,500 gap on a single meal you almost forgot to plan. If a hosted brunch matters to you, the move is either to price it at the venue's real number from the start or to move it off-site to a restaurant, a park, or a suite, where you control the format instead of accepting theirs.

### Welcome bags ($25-60 per room once delivery is added)

Welcome bags feel like a $15 craft project. The tote, a water bottle, some local snacks, a printed itinerary. Then reality: welcome bags hit $25-60 per room once delivery is added. Hotels frequently charge a per-bag delivery or handling fee (often $3 to $7 each) to get the bags to guest rooms or the front desk, and that fee is easy to miss until the final hotel invoice.

At 40 rooms, welcome bags at $25-60 per room is $1,000 to $2,400. Ask the hotel about their delivery or handling fee in writing before you assemble a single bag, and consider a self-serve table at check-in to skip the per-bag charge entirely.

### Hotel block attrition (you get billed for rooms guests don't fill)

This is the quietest and most expensive one, because it can charge you for nothing at all. Hotel block attrition clauses bill you for unfilled rooms. When you reserve a block, many contracts require you to fill a minimum percentage (commonly 80-90%). Book 40 rooms with a 90% attrition clause, fill only 28, and you can be liable for the difference on the remaining rooms you guaranteed.

At even $200 a night for a two-night stay, a shortfall of eight rooms can mean roughly $3,200 owed for beds nobody slept in. Before you sign a block, read the attrition clause line by line, ask for a lower guarantee or a courtesy block (no financial commitment), and understand the release date when unbooked rooms return to the hotel with no penalty. This one belongs on any [contracts](/blog/category/contracts) checklist you use.

## Vendor overtime: the multiplier under everything

Here is the cost that touches every event above at once. Every added event adds hours to your vendors' schedules, at $150-300 per vendor. Your photographer covering the welcome dinner, your coordinator running the rehearsal, your musicians playing the brunch, your videographer catching the send-off. Each stretched timeline is billable.

Stack a few of them. Photographer overtime at the welcome dinner ($300), coordinator hours for the rehearsal ($250), a musician for brunch ($200), and videographer overtime for a late reception exit ($300). That is $1,050 in overtime alone, spread so thin across four events that no single line item ever raised an alarm.

The fix is a written weekend timeline you share with every vendor before signing, so their quotes reflect the real schedule instead of just the reception. Ask each vendor directly: what does an extra hour cost, and which events are you assuming you cover?

## Red flags to watch for before you sign

If you notice any of these, slow down and ask for the number in writing:

- A venue quote that lists "brunch available" without a per-head price. That blank is where the $55-90 lives.
- A hotel block agreement that mentions "attrition" or "performance guarantee" but does not spell out the percentage or the release date.
- A welcome dinner or rehearsal dinner headcount described as "flexible" or "we'll figure it out." Flexible means it grows.
- A vendor contract that covers "the wedding" without naming which weekend events are included and which are billed as overtime.
- Welcome bag pricing that ignores the hotel's delivery or handling fee.
- Two separate dinners on back-to-back nights for essentially the same guest list. That is one event being paid for twice.

The pattern is the same every time. The cost is real and predictable, but it lives in a document you were never handed. Ask for that document.

## Put it all on one page before you book

Run the low end of these six against a mid-size weekend and you are already looking at several thousand dollars beyond "the wedding number." Run the high end and you have genuinely doubled a chunk of your budget. The difference between a couple who feels blindsided and one who feels in control is not spending less. It is seeing the full weekend written down before signing anything.

Here is your quick checklist:

1. Set a hard headcount for the welcome dinner and price both your target and your ceiling.
2. Fold the rehearsal dinner (10-15% of total) into the main budget, or merge it with the welcome dinner.
3. Price the day-after brunch at the venue's real $55-90 per head, or move it off-site.
4. Read the hotel block attrition clause and ask for a courtesy block or a lower guarantee.
5. Confirm welcome bag delivery fees in writing before assembling anything.
6. Share a full weekend timeline with every vendor so their quotes include (or clearly exclude) overtime.
