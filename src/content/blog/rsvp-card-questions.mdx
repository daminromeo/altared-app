say you send your invitation suite to print on a Monday, feeling very accomplished, and six weeks later you are sitting on the floor with a stack of returned cards that say nothing but a checked box and the word "Mike." Mike who? Mike who is bringing a guest, or Mike who is not? Does Mike want the chicken? Is Mike the one with the shellfish thing?

the rsvp card is doing a lot more work than just yes or no. if you only ask whether someone is coming, you will spend the next two months texting guests for their meal choice, spelling of their name for place cards, and whether the DJ should skip the electric slide. four questions fixes most of that.

here they are, in the order they should appear on the card.

## 1. full name, written out

ask guests to write it out, not just check yes, so seating charts and place cards match exactly.

this sounds fussy until you are building a seating chart from a pile of cards signed "the Johnsons" and you have three Johnson households on your list. a blank line that says **full name(s)** does three jobs at once:

1. it tells you exactly who is attending, which matters when you invited a couple and only one is coming
2. it gives you the spelling your guest actually uses, which is what goes on the place card and the escort card
3. it catches plus-ones you did not know about, because people will write a second name in that space whether you invited them to or not (better to find out now)

### a small wording fix

instead of a line labeled "name," try "names of those attending, please print." the word *print* is doing real work here. cursive on a small card at the end of a long day is how "Katharine" becomes "Katherine" on a place card she has to sit behind all night.

## 2. meal choice

list every option the caterer gave you, exactly as they gave it to you.

do not paraphrase. do not shorten "herb-roasted chicken with seasonal vegetables" to "chicken" and then try to match it back to the catering contract later. your caterer is going to ask for counts by their menu names, and the easiest version of that conversation is the one where your card and their contract use identical language.

a few things to decide before this line goes to print:

1. are you offering a vegetarian or vegan option by default, or only on request? if it is on the menu, put it on the card
2. are kids' meals a separate selection? if so, label them clearly so adults do not accidentally select one
3. do you need one box per person, not per card? two guests on one card means two meal selections, and a single checkbox will cost you a follow-up text

### why the deadline matters more than the design

caterer headcounts are usually due two to three weeks before the date, so your rsvp deadline should land well before that window. not on it. before it. you need buffer for the stragglers, the cards that get lost in the mail, and the week you spend chasing the ten people who were always going to be chased.

work backward: find the headcount due date in your catering contract, then set your rsvp deadline far enough ahead that you can still call, text and confirm the holdouts without calling your caterer in a panic. if your contract does not say when the final count is due, ask. that date drives your entire stationery timeline.

## 3. dietary needs

write: "please note any allergy or restriction so we can confirm with our caterer."

that phrasing matters. it is specific, it signals that you are going to act on it, and it tells guests the information goes somewhere real instead of into a void. people with serious allergies are used to being an inconvenience, and a line that says *we will confirm with our caterer* gets you honest, usable answers.

keep it as an open line, not a checkbox list. you cannot predict every restriction, and a guest who writes "severe tree nut allergy, including oils" has just handed your caterer exactly what the kitchen needs. a checked box labeled "allergies" has handed them nothing.

when the cards come back, keep these in one place as you go. a note in your planning spreadsheet or app the day a card arrives takes fifteen seconds. reconstructing it from a shoebox three weeks out does not.

## 4. song request

one optional line: "a song that gets you on the floor."

this is the fun one, and it is genuinely fun to read, but it is also practical. it hands your DJ a real list before the night starts. instead of a dance floor that takes forty minutes to fill while your DJ reads the room, they walk in already knowing what this specific group of people responds to.

mark it optional so nobody feels obligated. and when you pass the list to your DJ, pass the do-not-play list at the same time. if the electric slide is a hard no for you, say so in writing before the reception, not from the dance floor at 9pm.

## watch for these before you send to print

the rsvp card is one of the few wedding items that is genuinely expensive to fix after the fact, because fixing it means reprinting and remailing. a few red flags worth catching at the proof stage:

- **no response deadline printed on the card.** "kindly reply by ____" with a blank is a card that comes back blank
- **your rsvp deadline is the same week your caterer's headcount is due.** you have left yourself zero room to chase
- **one meal checkbox for a card addressed to two people.** this generates follow-up texts at scale
- **menu wording that does not match your catering contract.** you will be translating between two documents at the worst possible moment
- **no return address or postage on the reply envelope.** a surprising number of otherwise perfect suites go out without it
- **a card with no room to write.** if the design is beautiful but the name line is an inch long, guests will abbreviate, and you will be back to guessing

### one more: decide where the answers live

four good questions only help if the answers end up somewhere you can sort them. pick the destination before the first card arrives, whether that is a spreadsheet tab, your planning app, or a shared doc with your partner. the system matters less than the fact that there is one. if you are still assembling the rest of your vendor paperwork, [keeping every number in one place](/get-started) makes the headcount conversation much shorter.

## the short version

before your suite goes to print, confirm the card asks for:

1. full name(s), printed, one line per attending guest
2. meal choice, worded exactly as your caterer wrote it, one selection per person
3. allergies or restrictions, as an open line, phrased so guests know you will confirm with the kitchen
4. one optional song request for your DJ

then check two dates against each other: your rsvp deadline and your caterer's final headcount date. the first should land well before the second.

ask on the card, not in a follow-up text. save this for when you sit down to design your suite, and send it to the friend who is somewhere in a spreadsheet right now. more on sequencing your stationery and vendor deadlines over in [planning](/blog/category/planning).
