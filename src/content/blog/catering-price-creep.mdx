You get the proposal back and it looks doable. $95 a head, 120 guests, a menu that made you a little emotional in the tasting. You do the multiplication in your head, feel good about it, and move on to the seating chart.

Then, months later, the final invoice lands and it is a different number entirely. Not because anyone lied to you. Because the pretty proposal and the real total live in two separate documents, and most couples only see the gap at final payment.

Your catering quote and your final invoice are almost never the same number. Here are the six things that quietly raise your catering price per head, and exactly where to look for them before you sign.

## 1. The service charge is 18-24%, and it is not the gratuity

This is the one that catches people first. The service charge runs 18-24% on top of your per-head rate, and it applies to almost everything: the food, the bar, the rentals, sometimes even the cake-cutting fee.

Here is the part that trips couples up. The service charge is not the gratuity. It covers the caterer's overhead (event coordination, insurance, back-of-house labor), and in most contracts your servers still expect a tip on top of it. Read the line carefully. If the contract does not explicitly say "gratuity included," assume it is not.

Run the math on a real number. Say your food, bar, and rentals total $150 per head across 120 guests. That is $18,000. A 22% service charge adds $3,960 before you have added a single tip. On the proposal, that percentage is a small line at the bottom. On the invoice, it is a four-figure add.

**Watch for:** service charge applied to the pre-tax subtotal versus the post-tax total. Applied after tax, it costs you more. Ask which order they run it in and get the answer in writing.

## 2. The bar tier can add $20-45 per guest

Most catering proposals quote a base bar package, usually beer and wine. It reads clean and keeps the headline number low. But upgrading from beer/wine to a full bar adds $20-45 per guest, and that range is wide for a reason.

What moves you toward the top of it:

1. **Premium and top-shelf liquor** instead of well brands
2. **A signature cocktail or two**, which often means specialty ingredients and extra prep labor
3. **A longer bar window**, because you are paying for both product and the bartenders staffing it
4. **Champagne toast or a welcome drink**, quoted separately from the bar package itself

On 120 guests, moving from beer/wine to full bar at the midpoint of that range (roughly $32 per guest) is about $3,840. And remember the service charge from point one stacks on top of that. The bar is often where the quote-to-invoice gap widens the fastest.

If your crowd is not a big-drinking crowd, this is a genuine place to save. Beer, wine, and one signature cocktail covers most weddings and photographs just as well as a full bar.

## 3. Cocktail hour appetizers are quoted separately

You pictured passed bites during cocktail hour: little skewers, something on a spoon, the bacon-wrapped thing everyone fights over. Fair. But passed apps are almost always quoted separately from your dinner menu, usually $8-18 per person, per course.

Read that phrase slowly: **per person, per course.** Each type of passed appetizer is often counted as its own "course." So a cocktail hour with four passed items is not one $12 charge. It can be four of them.

Do the math on a modest spread. Four passed apps at $12 per person, per course, across 120 guests:

- 4 items × $12 = $48 per person
- $48 × 120 guests = $5,760
- Plus your 18-24% service charge on top

That is a real line that many couples never accounted for because the dinner menu was the number stuck in their head. It does not mean skip cocktail hour. It means know that "passed apps" is a multiplier, not a flat fee, and pick your favorite three or four instead of saying yes to everything on the list.

## 4. Guest count minimums lock your floor early

This is the sneaky one, because it is not a fee at all. It is math you agreed to on signing day.

Most catering contracts lock your floor at 80% of the estimate you give them when you sign. So if you tell them 150 guests in month one, your contract minimum is 120. Even if only 105 people RSVP yes, you are paying for 120.

The rsvp math you do in month one follows you all the way to the final bill. And couples routinely over-estimate at signing, because early on your list is aspirational. You have not yet cut the coworkers, the plus-ones for single friends, or your mom's book club.

**Watch for:** the exact percentage in your contract (some lock at 85% or 90%, which is even tighter) and the deadline for adjusting your count. A slightly conservative estimate at signing protects you. You can almost always add guests later, but you can rarely subtract below the floor.

## 5. Rentals are rarely included, at $12-30 per head

Here is a fact that surprises a lot of first-time couples: the plates, glasses, forks, napkins, and tablecloths are frequently not the caterer's, and not in the base quote. China, linens, and chargers are rarely included, and they run $12-30 per head extra.

What lands you at the high end:

1. **Upgraded china and glassware** instead of house-standard
2. **Chargers** (the decorative plate under the plate, which is purely aesthetic)
3. **Specialty linens**, textured runners, or floor-length drops
4. **Multiple glass types** per setting (water, wine, champagne all count separately)

At $20 per head across 120 guests, that is $2,400 you might not have penciled in. And rentals often come with their own delivery, setup, and pickup fees, plus a damage or replacement clause if a glass breaks.

Ask one direct question: "Does your quote include all tableware and linens, or do those come through an outside rental company?" If it is an outside company, get that quote too. It belongs in your per-head number, not off to the side.

## 6. Staffing ratio scales with your service style

The style of dinner you choose changes your labor bill, and labor bills by the hour.

A plated dinner needs 1 server per 8-10 guests. A buffet or family-style meal typically needs fewer servers, but more of other roles. Bartenders, captains, chefs, and kitchen staff are usually separate line items, and each one bills by the hour, often with a minimum number of hours per person.

For 120 guests, a plated dinner at a 1:10 ratio is 12 servers. Add bartenders and kitchen staff and you can be at 15-plus people on the clock, each billing a multi-hour minimum. Push your reception from four hours to six and every one of those hourly rates extends with it.

**Watch for:** overtime clauses. If your reception runs long, staff hours often bill at 1.5x past a certain point, and it is easy to blow past the window when the dance floor is full. Know your cutoff and know the overtime rate before the night of.

## None of this is shady, it is just two documents

Here is the honest framing. None of these six things is a scam. Caterers are not hiding the ball on purpose. It is just that the proposal is built to be attractive and the invoice is built to be complete, and the gap between them is where couples get surprised.

The whole game is reading the second document before you sign the first. When you know the service charge is coming, the bar tier is a range, the passed apps multiply, the guest floor locks early, the rentals are extra, and staffing scales with service style, none of it can ambush you at final payment.

Save this for when you are reviewing quotes. Here is the short version to keep:

1. **Service charge (18-24%)** on top of your rate, and it is not the gratuity
2. **Bar tier** upgrade to full bar adds $20-45 per guest
3. **Cocktail hour apps** at $8-18 per person, per course (each item can be its own course)
4. **Guest count minimum** locks at ~80% of your signing estimate, so estimate conservatively
5. **Rentals** for china, linens, and chargers at $12-30 per head, often through an outside company
6. **Staffing ratio** of 1 server per 8-10 guests for a plated dinner, billed hourly, watch overtime
