Two florist quotes, both for the same arch, the same bridal bouquet, the same eight centerpieces. One came in at $2,400. The other came in at $2,600. Easy call, right?

Except the $2,400 quote listed "delivery" as a single line with no number attached, and the $2,600 quote had already baked in pickup and drop-off. When the first florist sent the final invoice, delivery was $300 out and $300 back. The "cheaper" option cost $600 more.

the quote your vendor sends is almost never the number on the final invoice. delivery going both ways, per-mile charges past their radius, a hotel night you did not know you were covering, rush fees for a tight venue window, and a gratuity expectation layered on top of all of it... none of that makes it into the pretty proposal.

this is not about vendors being difficult. these costs are standard in the industry. floral designers really do have to send a van and a crew back to your venue at 11pm to break down rentals. photographers really do burn gas and daylight driving two hours each way. the problem is that couples compare quotes without knowing to look for them, and then the real total shows up at signing.

Here are the five that catch almost every couple at least once.

## 1. Round trip: delivery is two charges, not one

Florists and rental companies bill pickup and drop-off separately, often $150-400 each way. That is the single most common surprise in this category, and it is also the easiest to miss, because "delivery" reads like one word and one event.

It is not one event. Someone loads a van, drives to your venue, unloads, styles, and leaves. Then, usually after your last dance, someone comes back, breaks everything down, reloads, and drives it all home. For rentals in particular (tables, chairs, linens, glassware, arbors, lounge furniture), the return trip is often the harder one because it happens late at night, on overtime, with a crew that has to be paid.

### What that math actually looks like

Take a mid-range setup: your florist charges $250 each way, and your rental company charges $200 each way. That is $900 in transportation before a single stem or chair shows up in the subtotal. On a $30,000 wedding, that is 3% of your entire budget in van trips.

Ask this, in writing:

1. Is delivery quoted one way or round trip?
2. Does the breakdown and pickup happen the same night or the next morning, and does that change the price?
3. If it is next morning, does my venue charge me for an extra access window? (This is where two vendors' fees can quietly trigger a third.)
4. Is there a separate labor or crew charge on top of the delivery fee?
5. Does the fee change if my ceremony and reception are in two locations?

That last one matters more than couples expect. A ceremony-to-reception flip usually means an extra move, and an extra move usually means an extra charge.

## 2. Mileage: the radius is the whole game

Photographers and DJs often charge $0.67-1.00 per mile past a 30-50 mile radius. Almost every vendor has a "included travel" zone, and almost no proposal says where the edge of that zone is.

Run the numbers before you fall in love with a vendor two counties over. Say your photographer includes 40 miles and your venue is 95 miles from their studio. That is 55 miles past the radius, each way, so 110 billable miles. At $0.85 per mile, that is $93.50. Manageable. Now add a second shooter who bills separately from a different city, and an engagement session at a third location, and you are looking at three or four of those charges instead of one.

### The questions that actually surface it

- Where does the radius start? Their studio address, their home, or the nearest major city? These can be 20 miles apart.
- Is the mileage measured one way or round trip?
- Does each team member bill travel separately?
- Does travel apply to non-wedding-day events, like the engagement shoot or a rehearsal-night DJ setup?

If a vendor cannot tell you the mileage rate and the radius in a single sentence, that is not a scam, it is just a quote that is not finished yet. Ask them to finish it.

## 3. The hotel night you did not know you were covering

Vendors over 60-90 miles out often require a hotel stay, $150-300, which you cover.

This one feels unfair until you think about it from the other side. A photographer who shoots until 11pm and then faces a two-hour drive home is a safety problem. A DJ with an 8am load-in the next morning is not driving back and forth twice. So the contract says the couple provides or reimburses lodging. Standard. Reasonable. Also, frequently, a total surprise at signing.

Watch for how it is worded, because "lodging provided by client" can mean very different things:

1. **Reimbursed at cost** with a receipt, capped at a stated amount.
2. **Flat lodging fee** added to your invoice, whether or not they book anything.
3. **Client books the room** in your block, which sounds cheap until you realize your hotel block rate is $220 a night on a Saturday in October.
4. **Per-person rooms** for multi-person teams. Two shooters plus an assistant can mean two or three rooms.

If you have a destination-ish venue (a vineyard 90 minutes out, a lake house, a family farm), assume lodging applies to at least your photo team, your DJ or band, and your planner, and budget for it early. Three rooms at $250 is $750, and that number should live in your spreadsheet from week one, not appear in month nine. ## 4. Setup windows and rush fees

A narrow venue access window can mean rush fees, typically $75-200, to compress load-in.

Here is how it happens. Your venue hosts a brunch event that ends at 1pm, and your ceremony starts at 4:30. That leaves your florist, rental company, and lighting tech roughly two hours to do what they normally do in four. So they bring extra hands, pay them, and pass it along as a rush or expedited setup fee.

This fee is unusual because it is not really your vendor's decision. It is your venue's timeline creating a cost that lands on your vendor's invoice. Which means you can sometimes negotiate it away by fixing the timeline instead of arguing about the fee.

### How to head it off

- Get your venue's exact load-in and load-out times in writing before you sign any setup-heavy vendor.
- Send those exact times to every vendor who touches the room, and ask directly: "Does this window trigger a rush fee for you?"
- Ask your venue what an earlier access window costs. Sometimes an extra hour of venue time is cheaper than three vendors' rush fees.
- Ask whether a next-morning pickup is allowed, which can remove late-night overtime from your rental bill.

## 5. Gratuity on top of travel costs

Some contracts expect a gratuity on top of travel costs, ask before you budget. this one catches almost everyone.

The issue is not whether to tip. Most couples plan to. The issue is what the tip is calculated on. If your contract says "20% gratuity on total invoice," and your total invoice includes $900 of round-trip delivery, $93.50 of mileage, $750 of hotel rooms, and a $150 rush fee, you are tipping roughly $378 on transportation and logistics.

Ask two questions:

1. Is gratuity included, expected, or optional?
2. Is it calculated on services only, or on the full invoice including travel and delivery?

Plenty of vendors will happily calculate on services only if you ask. Almost none will volunteer it. For more on what is customary versus contractual, see our [etiquette posts](/blog/category/etiquette).

## Red flags to watch for in a quote

None of these mean a vendor is bad. They mean the quote is incomplete, and you should not compare it to anything until it is not.

- **"Travel: TBD"** or a delivery line with no dollar figure. TBD becomes whatever it becomes, after you have signed.
- **No radius stated anywhere.** If there is a mileage rate but no starting point, the rate is unusable.
- **"Travel expenses billed at cost"** with no cap. Ask for a not-to-exceed number.
- **A package price that is dramatically lower than every other bid.** Sometimes it is a newer vendor. Sometimes travel and delivery are simply unbundled and coming later.
- **Gratuity language buried in the terms section** rather than the pricing section.
- **Lodging described as "provided by client"** with no cap, no room count, and no nights specified.
- **A vendor who gets irritated** when you ask for a fully-loaded total. Everyone else will answer this in one email.

## The one-line fix

Before you sign anything, send this: "Can you send me an all-in total that includes round-trip delivery, mileage, lodging if applicable, any setup or rush fees, and gratuity if it is expected?"

That single sentence turns four quotes you cannot compare into four quotes you can.

## Quick recap

1. Delivery is usually two charges, $150-400 each way, not one.
2. Get the mileage rate and the radius, $0.67-1.00 per mile past 30-50 miles, and confirm whether it is one way or round trip.
3. Assume lodging ($150-300 a night) applies to any vendor more than 60-90 miles out, and ask how many rooms.
4. Get your venue's load-in window in writing, then ask each vendor if it triggers a rush fee of $75-200.
5. Ask whether gratuity is calculated on services only or on the full invoice including travel.
6. Compare all-in totals, never package prices.
