You found the restaurant. the private room is gorgeous, the menu is $65 a head, and you did the math on your napkin: 25 guests, a couple bottles of wine you're bringing yourself, done. you tell your future mother-in-law it's handled.

then the invoice shows up, and it's almost double.

this is the rehearsal dinner story that plays out constantly, because the rehearsal dinner budget is almost always an afterthought. couples spend a year obsessing over the reception and treat the night before like an easy dinner reservation. it isn't. private dining runs on the same fee structure as a wedding venue, just smaller and quieter, which means the same line items are waiting for you. they just get added after the initial proposal, which is exactly when couples stop checking.

none of these are hidden because venues are trying to trick you. they're standard line items that live below the headline price. here are the five that catch people, with the dollar ranges you should expect, and how to see the real number before you sign anything.

## 1. the room minimum

before a restaurant will hold a private room on a saturday night, most of them want a commitment. that commitment is a food-and-drink minimum, and it's usually the biggest surprise on the list. private dining rooms require $1,500 to $5,000 in food and drink spend before they'll hold the date.

here's the part that trips people up: the minimum is a floor, not a fee. if your food and drink total lands above it, you don't pay extra. but if your dinner comes in under, you pay the difference anyway, for food nobody ate.

say the room minimum is $3,000 and you've got 25 guests at $65 a head. that's $1,625 in food. even with a couple hundred in drinks, you're well short of $3,000. so you either pay $1,000-plus for nothing, or you start "spending up" to hit the number, adding a cheese board, a second course, bottles of wine you didn't plan on. either way the room minimum quietly sets your real budget, not the menu price.

### how to check it

ask two questions before you fall in love with the space: what's the food and drink minimum for this room on my date, and does it include or exclude the service charge and tax? sometimes the minimum is measured on the pre-tax, pre-service subtotal, which changes your math a lot.

## 2. the service charge that isn't the tip

this is the one that genuinely upsets people, and it's worth understanding exactly.

most private dining comes with an automatic service charge of 18 to 24% added on top of your food and drink total. reasonable, you think, that's the tip handled. except most venues say it's not the tip. the service charge covers the cost of running the private event: staffing, coordination, setup. so your server still expects one.

read that again, because it's the most common source of double-charging at rehearsal dinners. on a $3,000 tab, a 20% service charge is $600. if you then tip your server another 18 to 20% out of politeness (or because the captain hands you a slip at the end of the night), you've added another $500-plus you never budgeted.

### what to actually do

ask the venue, in writing, one direct question: does the service charge include gratuity for the service staff, or is a tip expected on top? get the answer in the contract language, not from a verbal "oh don't worry about it." if the service charge is gratuity, you're done. if it isn't, decide your tip number now and put it in your budget so it isn't a $500 ambush at the end of the night.

## 3. the corkage fee that erases your savings

you bought a case of wine you love. maybe it was a deal, maybe it's sentimental, maybe the restaurant's wine list is marked up 300%. bringing your own feels like the smart, money-saving move.

then you learn about corkage. bringing your own wine to save money costs $15 to $40 per bottle just to open it. that's the fee for the glassware, the pour, the service of wine you supplied.

do the math on a real case. 12 bottles at a $30 corkage fee is $360. if you brought that wine because it saved you, say, $25 a bottle versus the venue list, you saved $300 and paid $360 to bring it. the corkage fee erases the savings on the case of wine you brought, and then some.

corkage isn't automatically a bad deal. it can be great if you're bringing genuinely nice bottles you already own or a special vintage you can't get on the list. it's a bad deal when you bought cheap wine specifically to dodge the markup. run the numbers both ways:

1. total cost of your own wine plus corkage per bottle times the number of bottles
2. total cost of ordering comparable bottles off the venue list, plus service charge on those bottles
3. whichever is lower, that's your answer

sometimes bringing your own still wins. often it's a wash. you won't know until you ask the corkage number, and a lot of couples never ask.

## 4. guest creep

on paper, a rehearsal dinner is small. rehearsal dinners average 20 to 30 guests. in practice, couples routinely end up at 40 or more.

it happens gently. the wedding party, obviously. then their plus-ones. then out-of-town relatives who traveled and would feel snubbed if they weren't invited. then the officiant. then a few close friends who are "basically family." nobody adds ten people at once. they get added two at a time over three weeks, and your head count drifts.

the problem is that head count is your budget's biggest multiplier, because rehearsal dinners run $75 to $150 per head. going from 25 guests to 42 at $110 a head is a jump of nearly $1,900. that single quiet drift can be the most expensive thing that happens to your rehearsal dinner budget, and it never shows up as a "fee." it just shows up as a bigger number of people times a per-plate cost you already agreed to.

### how to hold the line

set the guest count before you book, put it in writing, and treat additions like real budget decisions. every "can we just add..." is $75 to $150. if you decide the answer is yes, great, but decide it on purpose with the real per-head number in front of you. for more on keeping numbers honest as your event grows, our [budgeting guides](/blog/category/budgeting) walk through how to build in a buffer instead of getting surprised by one.

## 5. the AV extras nobody quotes

the last one shows up on setup day, when it's far too late to shop around. you want a microphone so the best man's toast isn't shouted over 40 people, and a screen or tv to run a slideshow of you two. reasonable, sweet, standard.

a mic for toasts and a screen for photos add $200 to $600, and almost nobody quotes it upfront. it lives in a separate "audio visual" line that isn't part of the food-and-drink proposal, so it never appears in the number you've been comparing. some venues rent their own gear, some bring in a third party, and the price swings hard depending on which.

$400 in AV charges nobody mentioned until setup day is a real and common line item. if toasts and a slideshow matter to you, ask about AV before you sign: what's available, what it costs, and whether you can bring your own bluetooth speaker or use the tv that's already mounted on the wall.

## red flags to watch for

when you're comparing private dining rooms, a few things should make you slow down and ask more questions:

- a proposal that lists the per-head menu price but says nothing about the room minimum
- a service charge line with no note about whether it includes gratuity
- "we'll figure out AV closer to the date" (translation: an unquoted charge is coming)
- a venue that quotes head count off your first guess and never confirms a final number in writing
- corkage described vaguely as "market rate" instead of a specific dollar figure per bottle

none of these mean the venue is dishonest. they mean the full cost isn't on the page yet, and the page you sign is the one that counts.

## the fix: compare the full quote, not the headline price

every one of these costs is knowable before you book. the reason they surprise people is that couples compare the wrong number. they compare the $65 menu price at one place against the $70 menu price at another, when the real comparison is the total: room minimum, service charge, corkage, per-head count, and AV, all of it side by side.

do that and the "cheaper" restaurant often turns out to be the pricier one once its $4,000 room minimum and $30 corkage are on the table. the fix is comparing the full quote, not the headline price, before you sign.

before you book your rehearsal dinner, confirm these five things in writing:

1. **room minimum**: the exact dollar floor for your room on your date, and whether it's pre or post tax and service
2. **service charge**: the percentage (18-24%) and whether it includes gratuity or a tip is expected on top
3. **corkage**: the per-bottle fee ($15-$40), then run your own-wine math against the venue list
4. **per-head cost and final guest count**: lock the number ($75-$150 per head adds up fast at 40 guests)
5. **AV**: mic and screen pricing ($200-$600) confirmed before setup day, not after

save this for your rehearsal dinner research, add your own quotes, and track every line so nothing gets added after the proposal. you can build the whole side-by-side comparison free when you [get started](/get-started). the goal isn't to spend less on a night you'll love. it's to know the real number before you sign, so the only surprise that night is how good the toast turns out to be.
