You get the caterer's proposal back and it's beautiful. Clean columns, a per-plate number that fits your spreadsheet, a subtotal you can live with. You sign. Three months later the final invoice lands and it's $1,400 higher than the proposal, and nothing on it is technically wrong. The extra is sales tax, a service charge, and one hot meal per staff member that was written into the contract on page four.

This is the gap that quietly wrecks wedding budgets. The number on your quote and the number on your final invoice are almost never the same. And it isn't because vendors are hiding things. It's that the budget template everyone hands you was built to look good, not to be complete. The couples who come in under budget are the ones who knew to look for these six line items before they signed.

Here they are, one at a time, with the dollar figures you should actually pencil in.

## 1. The tip budget you forgot to fund

Tipping is the cost that feels optional right up until the wedding day, when you're handing out envelopes and realize you never set the money aside for them. Vendors expect $50 to $200 each, per person, in cash. That range moves depending on the vendor and how they price, but the important word is "cash." You can't put a tip on the card you're already carrying a balance on, and you can't skip it without feeling terrible at 11pm.

Run the math on a normal vendor lineup:

1. Hair and makeup team
2. Photographer and second shooter
3. DJ or band members
4. Catering and banquet staff
5. Delivery drivers (florals, cake, rentals)
6. Coordinator or day-of planner

That's easily six to twelve people. At the middle of the $50 to $200 range, you're looking at several hundred to over a thousand dollars in cash you need to physically have on hand. Some contracts fold gratuity into a service charge (more on that under tax), so read closely before you double-tip. But assume tips are coming, build a line for them now, and pull the cash out a few days early so you're not at an ATM in your wedding shoes.

## 2. Alterations that arrive after the dress

The gown price is the gown price. Alterations are separate, and they are not small. Expect an average of $500 to $1,000, and know that it's not included in any gown price you're quoted.

Here's why it stacks up. A wedding dress usually needs a hem, a bustle, side adjustments, and sometimes strap or bodice work. Add a fitting or two, add rush fees if your timeline is tight, and the total climbs. If you fall in love with a sample gown or something heavily beaded, alterations can run higher than the average because beadwork has to be removed and reset by hand.

The fix is simple: when you see a dress price, mentally add $500 to $1,000 before you decide whether it fits your budget. A $1,800 gown is really a $2,300 to $2,800 gown once it fits your body. Ask the salon for their alterations estimate in writing before you fall for anything, because "we don't do alterations in house, you'll need a seamstress" is a very common answer that leaves you sourcing (and paying) separately.

## 3. Postage that sneaks past $200

Paper feels like a rounding error until you're standing at the post office. Run a 100-guest list: 100 guests times $2-plus per invite adds up to $200 before stamps.

That $2-plus isn't the mailing cost, it's the cost of the invitation suite itself per household (invite, RSVP card, details card, envelopes). Then postage is its own separate charge on top. And here's the trap nobody warns you about: square envelopes, thick cardstock, wax seals, and anything bulky get hit with non-machinable surcharges, so you pay more than a standard stamp per piece. Send-the-dates, RSVP return stamps, and thank-you cards later all add to the paper column too.

### How to keep it from creeping

- Get your fully assembled invitation weighed at the post office before you buy stamps in bulk
- Skip the square envelope if you're watching the number, it triggers the surcharge
- Buy RSVP return postage at the same time so it doesn't feel like a surprise second bill

## 4. Tax is on everything, and it's not nothing

This is the one that silently adds hundreds to thousands. Sales tax on rentals, florals, and catering runs 8 to 12%. That's not a fee you can negotiate away, and it applies to your biggest line items.

Do the math on a realistic setup. Say catering is $8,000, rentals are $3,000, and florals are $2,500. That's $13,500 of taxable spend. At 10%, that's $1,350 in tax alone, and that's before any service charge the caterer or venue tacks on (service charges often run 18 to 24% and are their own separate line). Tax and service charge together can add a fifth of your total to categories you thought were locked.

The move here is to always ask for quotes with tax and service charge included, or to add 8 to 12% yourself to every proposal that shows a pre-tax subtotal. A proposal that only shows the pre-tax number looks better on paper, which is exactly why so many do. ## 5. Permit fees for the pretty outdoor spot

That gorgeous public garden, beach, or park? The setting might be free but the paperwork isn't. Outdoor venues can charge $150 to $500 just for the license, and that's before you factor in what a permit can quietly require alongside it.

Permits sometimes come bundled with conditions that cost more than the permit itself: proof of liability insurance, a security deposit, a specific cleanup window, or restrictions on amplified sound and open flame. A $300 permit that also mandates a $200 insurance rider and a licensed cleanup crew isn't a $300 line, it's closer to $800 once you add it all up.

If you're eyeing a non-traditional or public space, call the parks department or municipal office directly and ask three questions: what's the permit fee, what does it require you to carry or provide, and is there a refundable deposit. Getting that answer before you commit is the whole game. Our [planning](/blog/category/planning) posts cover how to vet a venue before it locks in the rest of your budget.

## 6. The vendor meals hiding in your contracts

Your caterer counts heads, and the heads aren't only your guests. Most contracts require one hot meal per vendor, per person. Photographers, videographers, the band, the coordinator, the day-of assistants all need to eat, and most vendor contracts specifically say so.

At a wedding with 8 to 12 working vendors and a per-plate cost of, say, $85, that's another $680 to $1,020 in catering you didn't count when you were pricing guest plates. Some caterers offer a discounted vendor meal rate, which is worth asking for, but some charge full price. Either way, the requirement is usually already written into your photographer's and coordinator's contracts, so this isn't a courtesy, it's a term you agreed to.

Two things to do: ask every vendor whether their contract requires a meal (most do), and ask your caterer for a vendor meal rate. Then add those heads to your final count on purpose instead of discovering them when the balance is due.

## Red flags to watch for on any quote

None of these six costs are scams. But a few patterns should make you slow down and read again:

- A proposal that shows only a pre-tax subtotal with no tax or service charge line
- "Alterations not included" buried in a dress contract with no estimate offered
- A venue that calls itself "free" or "all-inclusive" but stays vague on permits, insurance, or deposits
- A caterer's headcount that matches your guest count exactly, with no mention of vendor meals
- Gratuity policies that aren't stated, so you can't tell if a service charge already covers tips
- Any contract that references fees "per current rates" without listing the actual number

If you see any of these, ask for the missing number in writing before you sign. A good vendor will give it to you without flinching.

## Before you sign anything

These six will find you whether you plan for them or not. Here's the short version to save:

1. Fund a cash tip budget of $50 to $200 per vendor, per person
2. Add $500 to $1,000 to any gown price for alterations
3. Budget past $200 for postage on a 100-guest list, before stamps
4. Add 8 to 12% sales tax to rentals, florals, and catering
5. Ask about $150 to $500 permit fees (and their hidden requirements) for outdoor spaces
6. Count one hot vendor meal per working vendor into your final headcount
