You're four venue tours deep, it's 11pm, and the bar quote in front of you looks refreshingly simple. one number, per person, done. you do quick math in your head, decide it's fine, and move on to the next tab.

then the real invoice arrives a few months later and it's a different document entirely. the per-head number is still there, but now it has company: an overtime line, a gratuity line, and if you were clever and brought your own wine, a corkage line that quietly ate most of what you thought you saved.

none of these are hidden exactly. they're just buried in the details section of a contract you skimmed while exhausted. here are the four rules that keep the bar from wrecking your reception budget, and how to check for each one before you sign.

## rule 1: the per-person number is a floor, not the total

consumption and per-head packages usually run $35-85 per head. that range is wide for a reason. a beer-and-wine package for 100 people sits near the bottom. a full premium open bar with a signature cocktail and top-shelf spirits sits near the top. so before anything else, know which end of that range your quote actually represents.

but here's the part couples miss: the per-person price assumes a fixed window of service. the clock still matters. a package priced at $55 per head almost always means $55 per head for a set number of hours, commonly four or five. go past that window and you're no longer in per-person territory, you're in overtime, which is rule 2.

### do the honest math first

before you compare any two bar quotes, normalize them so you're comparing the same thing:

1. write down the per-person rate for each package.
2. multiply by your realistic guest count (not your invite list, your expected yes count).
3. note the number of service hours each rate includes.
4. divide the total by hours to see the real hourly cost of pouring drinks at your wedding.

a $55 per head package for 120 guests is $6,600 for, say, five hours. that's roughly $1,320 an hour of actual bar service. once you see it that way, the overtime numbers in the next section stop looking small.

## rule 2: overtime is the line that surprises everyone

every extra hour of bar service costs $300-600 more, per bartender. read that last part again, because it's the part that gets people. it's not $300-600 for the bar. it's $300-600 for each bartender working it.

if your reception has three bartenders (a normal ratio for 120 to 150 guests), one extra hour of open bar isn't $300-600. it's potentially $900 to $1,800 for sixty more minutes of pouring. and the moment a wedding runs long is exactly the moment nobody wants to be the person who cuts the bar off. the band is still playing, people are dancing, and the last thing on your mind is a contract clause.

### where overtime sneaks in

- your ceremony started late, so everything after it shifted.
- you added a cocktail hour but didn't extend the bar window to cover it.
- the reception is genuinely great and you extend on the night.
- the "package" covered five hours but your event timeline was always going to run six.

the fix is boring and effective: map your actual timeline hour by hour, from the first poured drink to last call, and make sure the package window covers all of it. if it doesn't, price the overtime in now rather than discovering it later. a $6,600 bar that turns into an $8,000 bar because of two unplanned hours is a very common story.

## rule 3: gratuity is 15-20% and usually mandatory

bartender tips run 15-20% on top, and most contracts list it as mandatory. this is the line couples assume is optional or discretionary, and it usually isn't. it's printed into the agreement as a service charge, and it's calculated on the full bar subtotal.

so take that $6,600 five-hour bar. add a mandatory 18% gratuity and you're at roughly $7,788. that's almost $1,200 you didn't budget if you only ever looked at the per-head number. and if overtime pushed the subtotal higher, the gratuity climbs right along with it, because it's a percentage of the total, not a flat fee.

a few things worth confirming in writing:

- is gratuity calculated before or after tax? (it changes the number.)
- is there a separate "service charge" and gratuity, which would mean two lines, not one?
- does the gratuity already cover the staff, so you're not expected to tip again at the bar?

that last question matters because some venues bundle a service charge that goes to the house, not the bartenders, and guests then tip on top. you want to know exactly where the money goes so you're not double-paying by accident.

## rule 4: corkage can cancel out your "smart" move

bringing your own wine feels like the obvious budget play. you buy at cost, skip the venue's markup, done. then the $15-25 per bottle corkage fee lands and quietly reverses most of the savings.

run the numbers on a real example. say you're serving wine to 120 guests and you plan on roughly 40 bottles for the night. at a $20 corkage fee, that's $800 just for the privilege of opening bottles you already paid for. depending on what you bought and what the venue would have charged, that $800 can erase the entire reason you brought your own wine in the first place.

corkage still wins sometimes. if you have access to genuinely good wine at genuinely low prices, the math can favor BYO even after the fee. but it's not automatic, and it's never a savings until you've subtracted the per-bottle charge from whatever you thought you were saving.

### questions to ask before you haul in cases of wine

1. what is the exact corkage fee, per bottle, in writing?
2. does it apply to champagne and spirits differently than still wine?
3. is there a limit on how many bottles you can bring?
4. does the venue require you to buy some of its stock regardless?

## watch for these red flags in the fine print

when you're comparing bar quotes, slow down on these:

- a quote that lists only a per-person rate with no service hours attached. that's not a full price, that's a starting point.
- "service charge" and "gratuity" appearing as two separate lines. that can be 30% or more added together.
- an overtime rate written as a range with no cap, so a long night has no ceiling.
- a corkage fee mentioned casually in conversation but not written into the contract.
- minimum spend requirements you'll be charged for whether guests drink that much or not.
- gratuity calculated on the post-tax total, which quietly inflates the number.

the pattern across all of these is the same. the number you were quoted verbally, or the one printed in the biggest font, is rarely the number on the final invoice. everything else lives in the details section, which is exactly where tired couples stop reading.

## the quick recap before you sign

the bar quote looks simple until you get to the invoice. here's what actually keeps the reception on budget:

1. treat the per-person rate ($35-85 per head) as a floor tied to a set number of hours, not the final total.
2. price in overtime now: every extra hour costs $300-600 more, per bartender, and it multiplies fast.
3. add the mandatory 15-20% gratuity to your subtotal before you decide the package fits.
4. only count corkage as savings after subtracting the $15-25 per bottle fee.
5. get every one of these written into the contract, not promised over the phone.
