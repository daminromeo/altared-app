say you're standing at the office microwave and someone from two desks over says "so, am I getting an invite?" with a big grin. you have about three seconds. you either freeze and say something vague you'll regret, or you have a sentence ready.

the hardest part of the list is the people who aren't on it. not the seating chart, not the stationery, not the math of who gets a plus one. it's the moment someone finds out they didn't make the cut and you're the one standing there.

here's what to actually say. short, kind and done.

## why the short answer is the kind one

there's a reflex, when you feel guilty, to over-explain. you start talking about the venue capacity, and then your partner's cousins, and then the catering minimum, and then your mom's list, and suddenly you've handed this person a spreadsheet of reasons to argue with.

one warm sentence beats a paragraph of explaining.

the long version sounds like a negotiation. it invites follow-up questions ("well how many spots are left?"). the short version sounds like a decision that's already been made, delivered warmly, which is exactly what it is. you are not asking permission. you are telling someone something true, kindly, and then changing the subject.

a few principles that make the short version work:

1. **lead with warmth, not logistics.** "we kept it really small" lands better than "our venue capacity is limited."
2. **don't apologize more than once.** one "i hope you understand" is sincere. three reads as guilt, and guilt invites pushback.
3. **don't blame a vendor or a number you'll have to defend.** if you say the room only fits a certain amount, someone will do math at you.
4. **offer a real next thing if you mean it.** dinner, drinks, a visit. only if you'll actually do it.
5. **stop talking.** the silence after a kind sentence is fine. let it sit.

## the four conversations you'll actually have

most of the awkward moments fall into four buckets. have a line ready for each and you'll never be caught at the microwave again.

### 01. the coworker who asks

say: we kept it small.

> "we kept it really small, family and a few close friends. i hope you understand."

this works because it's true and it's final. it doesn't rank the person, it describes the wedding. "family and a few close friends" is a category, not a judgment, and almost everyone recognizes that they're outside it without feeling insulted.

one note on work: try to be consistent. if you invite three people from a team of ten, the other seven will find out, because people talk. either invite the group, invite nobody from work, or invite the one or two you genuinely see outside the office and be comfortable saying so with the same sentence.

### 02. the plus-one request

say: names on the invite.

> "we could only invite the names on the envelope. i hope that's okay."

this is the one people dread most, and it's the one with the cleanest script, because the rule is already written on the envelope. you are not inventing a policy on the phone. you are pointing at the one you already set.

do not get drawn into "but i won't know anyone." the answer to that isn't a plus one, it's a good table. tell them who you're seating them with. that's a genuine fix and it costs you nothing.

if you want to avoid this conversation entirely, be ruthless about addressing the envelopes clearly. "the names on the envelope" only works as a line if the envelope actually has names on it.

### 03. the old friend

say: celebrate later.

> "we couldn't fit everyone, and i'd love to take you to dinner after."

this is the hardest category, because it's the one where the person's feelings are real and the history is real. someone who was in your life for a decade and isn't in it weekly now still deserves more than a form response.

the move here is to separate the wedding from the friendship. the wedding is a room with a number attached. the friendship is not. "i'd love to take you to dinner after" says that out loud. then book the dinner. a vague "we should catch up" is the thing people feel bad about, not the invitation itself.

### 04. the family friend

say: ask the couple.

if your parents get asked, they say the couple set the list.

this one isn't a script for you, it's a script for your parents, and it's worth giving it to them before invitations go out. parents get cornered at church, at work, at their friend's barbecue, and the pressure to say "oh i'll ask them" is enormous. one yes from a parent turns into four people on your list and a conversation you now have to un-have.

sit down with both sets of parents, confirm who's on and who's off, and agree on the line: the couple set the list. kind, honest, nothing to argue with. if a parent desperately wants someone added, that's a conversation between you and them in private, not something decided in a driveway.

## watch for these

a few things that turn one awkward moment into actual wedding drama:

- **the maybe.** "we're still finalizing" when you're not. it's a kindness that expires, and when it does it hurts more. if someone's off the list, they're off the list.
- **inviting someone to the after-party as a consolation.** some couples do this intentionally and well. done as a patch for guilt, it reads as a second-tier invitation, and people notice.
- **explaining your budget to people who didn't ask about it.** it turns a boundary into a debate.
- **different stories to different people.** "we kept it small" to one coworker and "my mom made us cut you" to another gets back to everyone. pick one true line and use it.
- **posting the full guest count or venue capacity publicly.** someone will count, and then someone will ask why they weren't one of them.
- **letting a parent or a sibling deliver the news for you** when the relationship is actually yours. if it's your friend, it's your sentence.

## get the list settled before the questions start

these scripts work best when you're sure of your own answer. the wobble people hear in your voice is usually not guilt, it's uncertainty, because the list isn't actually locked yet.

build the list in one place, mark who's confirmed, who's a maybe, and who's a firm no, and get both sets of parents to sign off on their portion before anything goes to print. once it's settled, the sentence comes out steady. and while the list settles, keep the rest of the plan (vendors, quotes, budget and to-dos) in one place so it isn't scattered across four group chats too.
