A couple I know sat down with their venue coordinator, signed a catering proposal that read $84 per head, and felt good about it. Reasonable. On budget. Then the final invoice arrived six weeks before the wedding, and the per-head number had quietly grown by nearly a quarter. Nothing was wrong. Nothing was hidden. It was just that the proposal they signed and the bill they paid were two different documents, and the gap between them was full of charges they had already, in a sense, paid once.

That gap is where most couples lose money. Not to scams, not to bad vendors, but to line items that overlap. You pay the florist for flowers, then pay again for the labor to install them. You pay the baker for the cake, then pay the venue to cut it. The quote looks fine until you read the invoice.

Here are the five that come up most often, with real dollar ranges so you know what to ask about before anything gets locked in.

## 1. Service charge, then a tip on top

The single biggest one. Your catering or venue quote will almost always include a **service charge of 18-22%**. It looks like gratuity. It reads like gratuity. It is not gratuity.

The service charge is an operational fee the venue keeps to cover staffing, coordination, and overhead. In most contracts it does not go to the servers, bartenders, or captain working your event. So when your coordinator hands you the final bill and there is a line for suggested gratuity, you are being asked to tip on top of a charge you assumed was the tip.

On a $30,000 food and beverage total, a 20% service charge is $6,000. If you then add a 15-18% tip because you did not realize the service charge was separate, that is another $4,500 or so. That is real money for something you thought was already covered.

### What to ask

- "Does the service charge go to the staff, or does the house keep it?"
- "Is gratuity included, expected on top, or entirely optional?"
- "Can I see how the service charge is broken down in the contract?"

Get the answer in writing. A good vendor will tell you plainly. If the answer is vague, that is your cue to slow down.

## 2. Floral setup billed separately from the flowers

You get a floral proposal. It lists your centerpieces, your bouquets, the ceremony arch, the aisle arrangements. You add it up, you approve it. Then a second charge appears: **install labor**.

Florals are not just product. Someone has to transport the arrangements, build the arch on site, hang the installations, and often come back at the end of the night to break everything down. That labor is real work and florists are right to charge for it. The problem is that many couples read the flower quote as the whole cost and never account for the install line, which can run into the hundreds or thousands depending on how ambitious your setup is.

This is not a reason to skip flowers. It is a reason to ask for the full picture up front so the install fee is not a surprise on the final invoice.

### What to ask

- Is delivery, setup, and breakdown included in this quote or billed separately?
- If it is separate, what is the estimated labor cost for my specific setup?
- Are there any items (arches, hanging installs) that carry extra rigging or staffing fees?

Ask for one number that covers everything. If the florist can only give you product now and labor "closer to the date," treat the labor as an unknown and pad your budget for it.

## 3. Photo editing that stops at 400 images

Photography packages are one of the easiest places to assume you are getting more than you are. A base package might promise, say, edited images from your full day. Read closer and the fine print says **the base package edits 400 shots**. Everything beyond that, plus albums and prints, costs extra.

Four hundred edited images sounds like a lot until you remember a full wedding day generates thousands of frames. If you want the ceremony, the portraits, the reception, and every candid your photographer caught, you may be looking at an add-on for additional edits, then another add-on for the physical album, then another for prints.

None of this means the photographer is overcharging. It means the number that matters is not the package price, it is what the package actually delivers.

### What to ask

- How many final edited images are included, and what does an extra batch cost?
- Is a printed album included or is it a separate product?
- Do I get the high-resolution files, and are there print-release restrictions?

Compare photographers on deliverables, not just headline price. A $3,500 package that includes 600 edits and an album can be a better deal than a $2,800 one that stops at 400 and charges for everything else. ## 4. Rental delivery billed both ways

Tables, chairs, linens, lounge furniture, dance floors. Rentals are unavoidable for most weddings, and the product cost is usually clear. The delivery is where the double charge hides.

Rental companies frequently bill delivery and pickup as two separate line items, and each direction runs an **average of $150-400 each way**. That is a drop-off fee, then a separate pickup charge. Book from two or three different rental sources (one for furniture, one for tableware, one for the tent) and you are paying delivery and pickup for each of them.

Say your furniture rental delivery is $300 down and $300 back, that is $600 on logistics alone before you have rented a single chair. Now multiply across vendors.

### How to keep this from stacking

1. Consolidate rentals with as few companies as possible so you pay one round-trip fee instead of three.
2. Ask whether the quote includes both directions or just delivery.
3. Check for after-hours or late-night pickup surcharges, which some venues require.
4. Confirm who is responsible for the pickup window so you are not hit with a re-trip fee.

One delivery-and-pickup line on a single vendor is far easier to swallow than four of them scattered across your budget.

## 5. Cake cutting after you already paid the baker

This is the one that makes couples laugh, then wince. You order your cake from a baker. You pay the baker in full. Then the venue charges a **cake-cutting fee of $3-6 per slice** to plate and serve it.

For 150 guests at $5 a slice, that is $750 to cut a cake you already bought. The venue's logic is that their staff are handling, portioning, and serving it, which does take labor. But most couples have no idea the fee exists until it lands on the final bill, long after the baker has been paid.

### What to ask

- Is there a cake-cutting or plating fee, and what is the per-slice rate?
- Does it apply to dessert I bring in from an outside vendor?
- Can it be waived or reduced if I use an in-house dessert option?

Some venues waive the fee if you buy dessert through them. Others will not budge. Either way, you want to know the number before you sign, not after.

## Red flags to watch for

None of these charges are illegal, and most vendors are not trying to trick you. But a few patterns should make you pause before you put a deposit down.

- **A quote that says "estimate" or "starting at" with no ceiling.** Ask what pushes the final number up.
- **Service charge and gratuity that are never clearly separated.** If you cannot tell which is which, ask directly.
- **"Labor" or "setup" listed vaguely without a dollar figure.** Vague today usually means expensive later.
- **Outside-vendor fees mentioned in passing.** Corkage, cake cutting, and cleanup fees for anything you bring in should be spelled out.
- **A vendor who gets defensive when you ask for the full, all-in number.** A good vendor expects this question.

The common thread is simple: the proposal is a sales document, and the invoice is the real one. Ask the invoice questions before you sign the proposal.
