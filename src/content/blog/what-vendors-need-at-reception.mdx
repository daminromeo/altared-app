say it's the Thursday before your wedding. you're chasing a final payment, re-doing table 9 because your cousin and her ex both said yes, and your phone buzzes with a text from your photographer asking which door she should load in through. then your DJ asks the same thing. then your florist asks whether she's getting fed, because it's in her contract and she needs to tell her second shooter whether to eat first.

none of those questions are hard. they're just landing on you during the exact week you have the least capacity to answer them.

your vendors are professionals. but they still need a few things from you on the day, and they are easy to forget to sort out before the rehearsal. here are the four that come up again and again, and how to handle each one before wedding week turns into a group text.

## 01. a point person who is not you

one named contact who answers vendor questions so you don't have to.

this is the single highest-leverage thing on the list. on the wedding day, every vendor on site will have a question at some point: where do we set up, has the cake arrived, is the ceremony running late, who's signing for the rentals. if the answer to "who do I ask" is "the couple," you will spend your reception being project managed.

### who it should be

a coordinator if you've hired one. if you haven't, it's a friend or family member who is organized, reachable, and not in the wedding party photos for the entire cocktail hour. the key word is **named**. not "my sister will probably handle it." a name, a cell number, and their explicit agreement that they're on duty.

### what they actually need from you

1. the full vendor list with company names, contact names, and cell numbers
2. arrival and departure times for each vendor
3. a copy of the timeline (printed, see below)
4. the final payment and gratuity envelopes, labeled, with who gets what
5. permission to make small calls without checking with you (if the florist wants to move an arrangement six feet, they decide, not you)

send that list to every vendor in advance with one sentence: "on the day, please contact [name] at [number] rather than us." that one sentence redirects dozens of messages away from your phone.

## 02. a printed timeline for every vendor on site

a physical copy, not a link. phones die and wifi fails at venues.

this is the beat people argue with until they've been in a barn with one bar of service and a photographer whose battery died at 9pm. a shared doc is great for building the timeline. it is a bad delivery format for the day itself.

print one copy per vendor, plus spares for the point person and the venue manager. hand them out at the rehearsal or leave a labeled stack at the vendor entrance.

### what belongs on it

the timeline you give vendors is not the same as the one you give your bridal party. vendors need:

- arrival and load-in times, by vendor
- ceremony start, and whether that's the real start or the invitation start
- key moments with times: entrance, first dance, toasts, cake, parent dances, last song
- meal service timing, including when vendor meals are served
- the point person's name and cell number at the top of the page
- strike time, and who's responsible for what at the end of the night

put the point person's number in a bigger font than everything else. that's the number people will squint at when something changes.

### keep the versions straight

if you change the timeline after printing, reprint. a vendor working off version three while the venue works off version five is how you get a photographer in the parking lot during the first dance. write the version date in the corner of the page so everyone can confirm they're holding the same one.

## 03. confirmed vendor meals, in writing

most contracts require a hot meal per vendor, confirm the count in writing.

check your contracts, it is almost certainly in there. photographers, videographers, DJs, band members, coordinators and sometimes second shooters and assistants are frequently entitled to a hot meal, and that language is binding. this is not a courtesy question, it's a contract question.

three things to sort out:

1. **the count.** add up every person on site, not every company. a photography team of two is two meals. a five-piece band is five. get the number from each vendor directly rather than guessing.
2. **the type.** your caterer may offer a vendor meal option that differs from the guest plate. check what your contracts actually specify, because some say a hot meal and some say the same meal guests receive. if there's a gap, resolve it now, not at 7pm.
3. **the timing.** vendors usually eat when you eat, so they're not shooting or playing through dinner and they're back on their feet before toasts. confirm with your caterer that vendor plates go out at the same time as the head table.

get the final count to your caterer in writing alongside your guest count, and keep the email. "we said twelve, they plated eight" is a conversation nobody wants to have during dinner.

## 04. load-in access details, sent a week out

tell vendors the door, the elevator, and the parking before the day.

this is the one that's almost always missing, and it's the one that costs the most time on the ground. a florist circling a building with buckets of hydrangeas for twenty minutes is twenty minutes gone from your setup window.

send one email, to all vendors, at least a week out. include:

- the exact load-in address (if it differs from the guest-facing address, say so loudly)
- which door, with a photo if the entrance is unmarked
- whether there's a service elevator, and if it needs to be reserved or keyed
- where to park, whether permits are required, and whether cars can stay on site all night
- the venue contact for day-of access, plus your point person
- any noise, curfew or amplification rules the venue enforces

ask your venue for this information in writing rather than reconstructing it from memory of a walkthrough. venues change entrances, loading docks get blocked, and parking rules shift by season.

## watch for these red flags

a few things that usually mean something needs fixing before the rehearsal:

- **a vendor who hasn't asked about load-in by the week of.** either they already know or they've assumed. confirm which.
- **"we'll figure out meals that day."** your caterer needs a count in advance. this answer means the count doesn't exist yet.
- **a point person who's also a bridesmaid, the officiant, and in charge of the toast.** too many jobs means no jobs get done.
- **timeline mismatches between vendors.** if your DJ's run sheet and your photographer's shot list disagree on when sunset photos happen, fix it now.
- **no written confirmation of anything.** verbal agreements at a walkthrough are not a record. follow up every conversation with a short recap email.
- **a venue coordinator you've assumed is your coordinator.** they're responsible for the building, not your timeline. read the contract and know which is which.

## the short version

none of this is complicated. it is just easy to lose in the chaos of final payments and seating charts. before wedding week:

1. name a point person who is not you or your partner, and tell every vendor their name and number
2. print a physical timeline for every vendor on site, with the point person's number at the top
3. confirm the vendor meal count in writing with both your vendors and your caterer
4. email load-in access details (door, elevator, parking) to all vendors at least a week out
