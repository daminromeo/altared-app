Your florist just emailed the proposal. It's gorgeous. There's a mood board, a color story, a line item called "ambient floral moment," and a total at the bottom that made you close the laptop and go for a walk. Before you open it back up and start cutting real flowers to hit your number, know this: the proposal is beautiful, and it is also padded.

Florals already run 8-10% of the average wedding budget before a single upsell lands. That's the baseline. The line items below are the ones that quietly push a reasonable quote toward doubling, and none of them move the needle on how your wedding actually looks in photos or in person. Here are the five that show up on nearly every floral quote, what they really cost, and what to do instead.

## 1. Flower crowns

A flower crown looks incredible in the fitting-room mirror and in the first hour of getting-ready photos. Then it wilts. Fresh crowns are made from delicate stems with no water source, and in a warm room or an outdoor ceremony they start drooping within four hours. You'll pay $80-200 each for something that peaks before the ceremony even starts.

If you love the look, there are cheaper paths that hold up better:

1. Order a silk or dried crown that stays crisp all day and becomes a keepsake.
2. Ask your florist to build one small crown for the flower girl only, where the wear time is short.
3. Skip it entirely and put that $80-200 toward centerpieces or your bridal bouquet, which appears in far more photos.

### The tell

Watch for crowns quoted "per person" for the whole bridal party. Five crowns at $150 each is $750 of florals that will be in a trash can by dinner. That's a full centerpiece table's worth of budget spent on something with a four-hour shelf life.

## 2. Pew cones (or aisle markers)

Pew cones are the little floral cones or bunches that hang off the ends of your ceremony rows. On paper they read as "aisle decor." In practice, guests are seated in front of them for the entire ceremony, so almost no one sees them, and your ceremony photos frame the couple, not the pews.

Here's the math that gets people: they're billed per cone, at $15-40 each, every single row. A church with 20 rows means 40 cones (both sides) at, say, $25 apiece. That's $1,000 for decorations facing the backs of your guests' heads.

If you want the aisle to feel intentional, concentrate the spend where the camera actually looks:

- Two larger arrangements at the front, framing where you'll stand.
- Petals or greenery down the aisle runner instead of dozens of small cones.
- A few markers on the last two or three rows only, not all twenty.

You get the visual payoff of a decorated ceremony without paying by the row for florals no guest is looking at.

## 3. Floral fog

Floral fog (sometimes sold as a "cloud effect" or "ground fog moment") is the low-lying mist that rolls across the floor during your first dance or ceremony exit. It's genuinely beautiful in a video. It's also a $300-900 add-on for something you can DIY.

The effect comes from dry ice in water. Dry ice from a party supplier runs $40, and plenty of venues or DJs already own the equipment to run it. So you're often paying your florist a several-hundred-dollar markup to coordinate a $40 material through a third party.

### How to handle it without the markup

Ask two questions before you accept this line item:

1. "Does our venue or DJ already offer a fog or haze effect?" Many do, bundled or for a small fee.
2. "Can we source the dry ice ourselves and have your team place it?" If the answer is no or it comes with a big surcharge, that's your sign this is pure margin.

If nobody on your team can safely run it, paying for the service can be reasonable. Just don't pay florist-proposal prices for a $40 bag of dry ice unless real labor and equipment are attached.

## 4. Preserved blooms

Preserved and dried florals get sold as the smart, low-maintenance, "lasts forever" choice. Sometimes they are. But on a lot of quotes they carry a 30-50% markup over fresh, and here's the part the proposal won't tell you: they photograph flat. Preserved blooms lose the depth and light-catching translucency that fresh petals have, so in photos they can read dusty and muted, exactly where you're spending the most.

Preserved florals earn their price in specific spots:

- A bouquet you genuinely plan to keep as a keepsake.
- Installations that have to be built days early and sit in a hot space.
- Boutonnieres and small accents that see a lot of handling.

For centerpieces and the arrangements that anchor your photos, fresh (and in-season) will almost always look better for less. If your florist is defaulting the whole order to preserved "so it lasts," ask which pieces actually benefit and price a fresh version of the rest.

## 5. Bud vase rentals

Bud vases are the small single-stem vessels clustered down long tables, and they're a real trend for good reason. The problem is renting them. At $8-20 per vase, a room of long tables adds up fast. Thirty vases at $12 each is $360 in rental fees for glassware that goes back in a bin at the end of the night.

You can thrift 30 bud vases for under $15 total. Estate sales, thrift stores, and marketplace lots are full of mismatched glass that reads as intentional and collected, not cheap. Then you keep them, resell them, or gift them.

### When renting still makes sense

If you want a perfectly matched set of a specific vase, or you truly don't have time to shop and store 30 pieces of glass, rental is a fair convenience. Just price the difference honestly: $360 in rentals versus $15 in thrifted glass is $345 you could move to something that shows up in every photo, like your head-table florals.

## What's actually worth paying for

Skipping these five doesn't mean going bare. It means putting the money where it counts. Florals are 8-10% of the budget; spend that on the pieces that carry the day:

- Your bridal bouquet, which appears in more photos than anything else you'll hold.
- One or two focal ceremony arrangements where you say your vows.
- Reception centerpieces on the tables guests actually sit at and photograph.
- Fresh, in-season stems over preserved for anything the camera will feature.

A good florist will happily reallocate. A quote is a starting point, not a contract you have to accept line for line.

## Red flags to watch for on a floral quote

Before you sign, scan for these:

1. **Per-unit line items with no total count.** "Pew cones: $25 each" is meaningless until you multiply by every row. Ask for the full quantity and total.
2. **Vague "moment" or "effect" line items** like ambient floral, cloud effect, or floral fog priced at $300-900 with no breakdown of labor versus materials.
3. **Everything defaulted to preserved** without a fresh comparison, quietly baking in a 30-50% markup.
4. **Rentals for items you could own for less,** especially bud vases at $8-20 each when 30 thrifted ones run under $15 total.
5. **Décor aimed away from the camera or the couple,** like crowns that wilt in four hours or aisle markers guests sit in front of.

If a line item is expensive and hard to explain, that's usually the one to question first. For more on spotting padding across every vendor, our [hidden costs](/blog/category/hidden-costs) posts break down where quotes tend to inflate.

## The quick version

Here's what to do before your next florist meeting:

1. Cut or downgrade flower crowns ($80-200 each, wilts in four hours). Use silk or reserve one for the flower girl.
2. Trim pew cones ($15-40 per cone, every row). Concentrate florals at the front and along the aisle instead.
3. DIY floral fog. Dry ice from a party supplier runs $40, versus a $300-900 add-on.
4. Choose fresh over preserved for photo pieces and skip the 30-50% markup unless you're keeping the blooms.
5. Thrift bud vases (30 for under $15 total) instead of renting at $8-20 each.
