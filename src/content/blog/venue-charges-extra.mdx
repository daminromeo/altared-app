You tour the venue on a Saturday morning. The coordinator walks you through the ballroom, quotes you a per-head number that feels doable, and you leave already picturing your first dance under those chandeliers. The proposal that lands in your inbox looks clean. You sign.

Then the final invoice shows up three weeks before the wedding, and it is eleven hundred dollars higher than the number you agreed to. Not because anyone lied. Because your venue quote is a starting point, not a final number. And the gap between those two things is where a very specific set of fees lives.

Here are the five that show up most often, roughly what each one costs, and how to catch them before contract day instead of after.

## 1. Table setup, billed as a "room flip fee"

This is the sneakiest one because it hides behind reasonable-sounding language. A room flip fee ($3-8 per chair) covers the labor of resetting your space, usually turning a ceremony layout into a reception layout, or a cocktail setup into seated dining. The work is real. Someone does have to move those chairs.

The problem is not that the fee exists. The problem is whether it is included in your per-head rate or itemized on top of it, and that is often a conversation the venue does not start unless you do.

Run the math. At $5 per chair for 150 guests, a room flip fee is $750. At the high end of $8 per chair, you are looking at $1,200 for something you assumed was part of "the venue handles setup."

A room flip fee is standard. Whether it is baked into your quote or added later is negotiable, and it is one of the easiest things to fold in if you ask before you sign.

## 2. Cake cutting, charged on top of your baker

You already paid a baker. You have a line item, a deposit, a tasting appointment on your calendar. So it catches people off guard when the venue charges a cake cutting fee ($2-6 per slice) to plate and serve the cake you already bought.

At $4 per slice for 150 guests, that is $600 to cut a cake. The venue's logic is that plating, serving, and clearing take staff time. Fine. But this fee is almost always negotiable, and sometimes it disappears entirely if you ask whether it can be waived or if you are having your baker deliver a sheet cake for the kitchen to portion instead.

### Ask this specifically

When you tour, ask: "Is there a cake cutting or plating fee, and can it be waived?" Get the answer in writing. A venue that includes it in the package will tell you plainly. A venue that plans to charge it will often only mention it if you bring it up first.

## 3. Valet parking, the surprise $500-1,500 line

Valet parking as a surprise $500-1,500 line is one of the larger single hits, and it is frustrating for a different reason than the others. Your guests expect parking to be handled. If your venue is somewhere with limited street parking or a shared lot, valet is not a luxury add-on, it is basically required for the event to function.

That does not mean it should be a surprise. Some venues include it. Some assume you know it is separate. And some quietly leave it off the proposal so the headline number looks competitive against the venue you toured last weekend.

The range is wide ($500-1,500) because it depends on guest count, event length, and how many attendants the venue requires. Ask for the specific number based on your headcount, not a "starting at" figure.

## 4. Coat check, hiding under "guest services"

Coat check ($300-700 flat) is the one people skim right past, because it is often listed as "guest services" or bundled into a vague labor line. If your wedding is in November in a cold climate, you need it. If it is an August evening, you may not, and you should not be paying for staffed coat check you will never use.

This is a good example of why you read every line item, even the ones that sound optional or generic. "Guest services" tells you nothing about what you are actually paying for. Ask what it includes. If the answer is "coat check attendants," and you are getting married in July, that is money back in your pocket.

## 5. Vendor meals, because your photographer eats too

Most contracts require you to feed your working vendors, and they should be fed, they are on their feet for eight or ten hours. But vendor meals at $35-65 a head add up faster than couples expect, and they are almost never in the original proposal.

Here is where it lands. Say you have a photographer, a second shooter, a videographer, a DJ, and a day-of coordinator. That is five vendor meals. At $50 each, that is $250. At the top of the range ($65), it is $325, for people who are not on your guest list.

The negotiation here is not "don't feed them." It is asking whether vendor meals can be billed at a reduced rate instead of the full plated dinner price. Many venues offer a boxed or simplified vendor meal for a fraction of the per-guest cost. If they do not offer it, ask. A lot of the time it exists but is not advertised.

## What all of this adds up to

Run one realistic scenario at 150 guests, choosing the middle of each range:

1. Room flip fee at $5 per chair: $750
2. Cake cutting at $4 per slice: $600
3. Valet parking, midpoint: $1,000
4. Coat check, flat: $500
5. Vendor meals, five vendors at $50: $250

That is $3,100 in charges, none of which were in the original proposal, all of which hit the invoice. Some of it is legitimate labor. Some of it is negotiable to zero. The point is not that venues are scamming you. The point is that the quote is the beginning of a conversation, and if you do not start that conversation, it happens on the final invoice where you have no leverage left.

## Red flags to watch for on contract day

Before you sign anything, look for these:

- **Vague line items.** "Guest services," "event labor," or "service package" with no breakdown of what is inside. Make them itemize it.
- **"Starting at" pricing.** Valet "starting at $500" is not a number, it is a floor. Get your actual figure for your actual headcount.
- **A quote that is noticeably lower than comparable venues.** Sometimes it is genuinely a better deal. Sometimes the difference is a stack of fees that live off the proposal.
- **A coordinator who answers "we'll sort that out later."** Later means after you have signed and lost your ability to negotiate. Sort it out now.
- **No written confirmation.** If a fee is waived in conversation, it did not happen unless it is in the contract. Verbal promises do not survive a staffing change six months from now.

The through-line: read every line, ask what each one includes, and get the answers in the document you sign. If a fee is not written down as waived, assume you are paying it.

## Go line by line before you sign

Most of these fees are negotiable before you sign and nearly impossible to change after. That timing is the whole game. On tour day and contract day you are the customer they want. Once your date is locked and your deposit is in, the balance shifts.

So do this with every venue you are seriously considering:

1. Ask for a fully itemized proposal, not a per-head summary.
2. Go down the list and ask "is this included or extra?" for each line.
3. Specifically confirm the five above: table setup, cake cutting, valet, coat check, and vendor meals.
4. Ask which fees can be waived or reduced, and get the answer in writing.
5. Compare venues on the full number, not the headline quote.
