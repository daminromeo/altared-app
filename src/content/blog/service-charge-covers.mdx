A couple sat across from their caterer, quote in hand, and pointed at a line near the bottom: "18% service charge, $2,340." They assumed it was the tip. It felt like a lot, but they figured the staff were covered, so they mentally checked "gratuity" off their list. Three weeks after the wedding, the coordinator gently asked if they'd set aside envelopes for the servers and bartenders. They hadn't. That $2,340 they thought was going to the team had gone to the house, and now they were scrambling to pull cash together after the fact.

This happens constantly, and it's not because couples are careless. It's because the words "service charge" sound like they mean "service = the people serving you." They don't. That 18-22% on your catering invoice is one of the most misread numbers in the entire wedding budget, and understanding it is the difference between budgeting once and paying twice.

Here's what it actually covers.

## 1. Staff wages (their base pay, not a bonus)

The first and largest chunk of your service charge goes to labor: servers, bartenders, and the setup crew who arrive hours before your guests to build the room. This covers their base pay for the hours they work your event.

The key word is *base*. This is what the venue or catering company is contractually obligated to pay these people to show up and do their jobs. It is not a bonus, not a reward for great service, and not a tip. It's the equivalent of the hourly wage line on any business's payroll, just bundled into a percentage on your invoice.

Most venues pay their staff base rate and nothing more. So when you see that percentage, picture it as the company covering its labor cost, not as your appreciation flowing directly into someone's pocket. That distinction matters more than it sounds, and we'll come back to it.

## 2. Admin overhead (the invisible machinery)

The second thing your service charge absorbs is the stuff you never see: administrative overhead. This is roughly 5-8% of the charge, and it covers the systems that make a large event run without falling apart.

Think about what a catering or venue operation actually needs to function:

1. **Event coordination software** to manage timelines, seating counts, dietary flags, and vendor schedules.
2. **Venue insurance**, which is not optional and not cheap for a business hosting hundreds of guests and open flames.
3. **Coordination labor** on the back end, the planners and managers reconciling headcounts and confirming your details in the weeks before.

None of this shows up as a separate line item, because none of it is glamorous. But it's real cost, and it has to live somewhere. It lives in your service charge. When people say "why is this fee so high," a chunk of the answer is that you're paying for the operational spine of the event, not just the food and the folding chairs.

## 3. Equipment use (wear and replacement, baked in)

Every linen on your tables, every wine glass, every chafing dish keeping the entrée warm has a lifespan. It gets used, washed, chipped, stained, and eventually replaced. Your service charge helps fund that cycle.

This is what "equipment use" means on an invoice. You're not buying the glassware, but you're paying for your share of its wear and eventual replacement. A single reception can put hundreds of glasses and dozens of linens into rotation. Some come back cracked. Some come back with red wine that won't lift. The cost of keeping the inventory stocked and presentable is built into the percentage.

It's easy to overlook because it feels like it should be "included." And it is included, that's exactly the point. It's included *in the service charge*, which is different from being free.

### why this trips people up

Slides one through three cover real, legitimate costs. Nobody's being scammed here. The problem is that all three of these get lumped under a phrase that sounds like "tip," and that leads directly to the most expensive misunderstanding in this whole conversation.

## 4. It is not your tip

Here's the part most couples miss, and it's the reason to save this before your next vendor meeting: **none of the service charge replaces a gratuity.**

That 18-22% covers base wages, admin overhead, and equipment. The staff still expect $20-50 each on top of the service charge, because most venues pay them base rate and the charge itself goes to the house, not to the individuals working your event.

Read that again, because it's the whole ballgame. The service charge goes to the *business*. The tip goes to the *people*. Those are two different destinations, and assuming the first one covers the second is how couples end up tipping twice, or worse, not tipping at all and leaving their team feeling overlooked on the one day they worked hardest for you.

### what this looks like in dollars

Say you have a team of 12 working your reception: eight servers, two bartenders, and a two-person setup crew. At $20-50 each, you're looking at somewhere between $240 and $600 in gratuity, entirely separate from whatever the service charge added to your invoice.

If your catering total was, for example, $13,000 and your service charge ran 18%, that's $2,340 going to the house. And then, on top of that $2,340, you still owe your team their $240 to $600. Budget for both. If you only budget for the service charge and assume it's the tip, you're going to feel blindsided the week of, exactly when you have the least energy to deal with it.

## red flags to watch for on your quote

Not every invoice is written clearly, and some are vague in ways that cost you. Watch for these:

1. **"Service charge" with no explanation of where it goes.** Ask directly: does any portion of this go to staff as gratuity, or does it all go to the house? Get the answer in writing.
2. **A quote that implies gratuity is "included" without saying so explicitly.** If a vendor tells you tips are covered, ask them to put "gratuity included" on the contract in plain language. A service charge and a gratuity are not the same word.
3. **A service charge above 22% with no breakdown.** The 18-22% range is standard. If you're being charged more, you deserve to know what the extra is buying.
4. **Sales tax applied on top of the service charge.** In some states this is legal and normal, but it can quietly inflate your total by hundreds of dollars, so make sure you're seeing the true final number, not just the subtotal.
5. **Verbal promises about tipping that never make it onto paper.** If it's not in the contract, it doesn't exist. This is true for every part of wedding planning, and it's doubly true here.

The best time to ask these questions is before you sign the venue contract, not after. Once your signature is on the line, your leverage is gone. For more on reading the fine print, our [contracts guides](/blog/category/contracts) walk through what to flag before you commit.

## how to budget for both without overpaying

You don't need to guess. You need to separate the two costs and plan for each on purpose.

1. **Read your service charge line and confirm what it covers.** Wages, admin, equipment. Confirm none of it is earmarked as gratuity.
2. **Ask the vendor point blank whether gratuity is included.** Yes or no, in writing.
3. **If it's not included, budget $20-50 per staff member** and count how many people will actually be working your event.
4. **Prep envelopes ahead of time** so you're not pulling cash together after the wedding like the couple at the top of this post.
5. **Cross-check the math** so a service charge and a tip never get counted as the same dollar.
