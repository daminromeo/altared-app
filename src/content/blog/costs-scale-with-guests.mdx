Picture the moment you add your cousin's new boyfriend to the list. One name, one chair, no big deal. Except that one name just added a $130 plate, a $70 bar tab, part of a new floral centerpiece because he tipped a half-empty table into a full one, and a place card, menu, and invite with his name printed on them. That single guest didn't cost you a seat. He cost you closer to $250 before anyone poured a drink.

This is the part most couples don't see coming. The guest list is the most expensive decision you will make, and most people don't realize it until the invoices are already in. The number at the top of your spreadsheet quietly multiplies through five different line items, and every one of them moves with your headcount. Once you know which costs scale per guest, cutting five names stops feeling awkward. It becomes a budget decision you can make with actual numbers instead of guilt.

Here's exactly where your guest count hits your budget, line by line.

## 1. Catering: the biggest per-head number on your budget

Catering is the clearest example because it's almost always priced per plate. Expect $85 to 200 per head depending on your region, menu, and service style, and that's before the 20 to 25% service charge stacked on top.

That service charge is the part people miss. It's not the tip (though sometimes it partially covers gratuity, and sometimes it doesn't, which is a question worth asking directly). It's a percentage applied to your food and beverage total, so it grows with every plate you add. On a $130 per-head menu, a 22% service charge adds roughly $29 per guest. That means each additional person isn't $130, it's closer to $159 once the charge lands.

Run the math on ten guests at that rate and you're looking at about $1,590, not $1,300. Multiply across a full list and the gap between what you budgeted and what you owe can reach four figures on the catering line alone.

### what to ask before you sign

- Is the service charge separate from gratuity, or does it include it?
- Does the per-head price include staffing, or is that billed separately?
- Are tastings, cake cutting, and coffee service extra?
- What's the price difference between a plated dinner and buffet or family style?

## 2. Bar package: $40 to 100 per person, and rarely optional

An open bar runs $40 to 100 per person, and it's rarely something you can skip without your guests noticing. Some venues require you to use their bar service. Others let you bring alcohol but charge a corkage or bartending fee that quietly closes the gap.

Bar pricing is usually structured one of two ways, and the difference matters for how it scales:

1. **Per person, flat rate.** You pay a set amount per guest for a block of hours (say, $60 per person for a four-hour open bar). This scales directly with headcount, full stop.
2. **Consumption based.** You pay for what's actually poured. This can be cheaper for a lighter-drinking crowd, but it's unpredictable, and a big or thirsty guest list can blow past a flat-rate estimate fast.

Either way, more guests means more drinks. A 20-person swing on a $60 per-person package is $1,200, and that's assuming the mid-range. On the high end, at $100 per person, those same 20 guests add $2,000. The bar is one of the easiest places for a "we'll figure it out later" list to quietly overrun the plan.

## 3. Venue rental: you pay by the tier, not the person

Venue is the sneaky one, because it doesn't look like it scales per guest until you hit a threshold. Then it jumps all at once.

Most venues charge per tier. A room rated for 100 guests costs one price. Cross into 150 and you're renting a bigger room, or a different package entirely, often with a higher minimum spend attached. The moment your headcount crosses that line, the price steps up whether you added one guest or forty.

This is why the difference between 98 guests and 105 guests can be thousands of dollars, while the difference between 105 and 130 might be nothing. The cost isn't smooth. It's a staircase.

### how to use tiers to your advantage

Before you fall for a venue, ask for the exact headcount cutoffs for each package. If your list is hovering at 152 and the next tier down caps at 150, cutting two names might save you a full room upgrade plus its higher food-and-beverage minimum. That's the kind of call you can only make well if you know where the lines are drawn. We break down more of these threshold traps in our guide to [hidden wedding costs](/blog/category/hidden-costs).

## 4. Centerpieces: every seat you add fills a table

Florals scale in a way that feels invisible until you're staring at a floor plan. The rule is simple: one table equals one arrangement, and centerpieces run $150 to 500 each.

Here's the trap. Guests don't get added one seat at a time in a way that maps cleanly to cost. They get added until a table fills, and then the next guest opens a brand-new table. That new table needs its own centerpiece, its own linen, sometimes its own rental chairs and place settings.

So the person who tips you from 80 guests (ten tables of eight) to 81 guests doesn't just cost a plate and a drink. They open table eleven, which needs a $150 to 500 arrangement, plus the linen and rentals to dress it. One guest, one whole new table's worth of decor.

If you're seating eight per table and you're sitting at a comfortable, full count, adding a few names is cheap. Adding one name that opens a new table is expensive. Knowing your seats-per-table number tells you exactly when the next guest gets pricey.

## 5. Stationery: $8 to 20 per name, printed everywhere

Stationery is the smallest per-guest number and the easiest to underestimate, because your name shows up on so many pieces. Invites, save-the-dates, place cards, menus, escort cards, thank-you notes. It adds up to $8 to 20 per guest, and it bills per name.

At $14 per guest across a 140-person list, that's $1,960. Add 20 guests and you've added $280 without touching a single design choice. It feels like a rounding error next to catering, but it's a real line that moves with headcount, and it's one of the few you have full control over.

### where couples overspend on paper

- Ordering separate save-the-dates when a digital version would do
- Individual printed menus at every seat instead of a few shared table menus or a single large sign
- Overordering invites (order 10 to 15% extra for mistakes, not double)
- Premium finishes (letterpress, foil, custom envelope liners) priced per piece and multiplied by your count

## red flags to watch for

A few warning signs that a quote is going to scale worse than it looks on paper:

- **A per-head price with no service charge listed.** It's almost always coming. Ask for the all-in number before you compare vendors, or you'll compare a pre-charge quote against an all-in one and pick wrong.
- **A venue that won't tell you the tier cutoffs.** If they're vague about headcount thresholds, you can't plan around them, and you'll likely find out you crossed one after you've committed.
- **"Starting at" floral pricing.** $150 might be the base arrangement, not the one in the photo you loved. Get quoted on the actual design at your actual table count.
- **Bar minimums buried in the contract.** Some venues set a food-and-beverage minimum that only makes sense at a certain guest count. A smaller list can mean you're paying for drinks nobody ordered.
- **Rental counts that don't match your final headcount.** Chairs, linens, and place settings all track to your number. Confirm the vendor is quoting your current count, not an early estimate.

Read your contracts closely for these, and if the language is confusing, that's a fair thing to push back on before you sign. Our [contracts breakdown](/blog/category/contracts) covers what to look for.

## the takeaway: cut names with numbers, not guilt

Cutting five guests off the list isn't awkward, it's a budget decision. When you know which line items move with headcount, you can make that call with real math instead of second-guessing yourself.

Here's your quick reference before you finalize the list:

1. **Catering** runs $85 to 200 per head, plus a 20 to 25% service charge on top of that. Always ask for the all-in number.
2. **Bar** adds $40 to 100 per person, and it's rarely optional. Know whether you're paying flat rate or by consumption.
3. **Venue** charges by tier. Get the exact headcount cutoffs so you don't cross one for the sake of two guests.
4. **Centerpieces** cost $150 to 500 each, and every seat you add can open a whole new table's worth of decor and rentals.
5. **Stationery** bills $8 to 20 per name across invites, place cards, and menus. Small per person, real in total.

Add those together and one extra guest can cost $200 to 400 all in, not the single seat it looks like. That's the number to keep in mind when the list starts creeping.
