You're three quotes deep into catering, and one of them has a line at the bottom: "22% service charge, $4,840." Your first thought is, great, the tip is handled. You cross "tip the servers" off your mental list and move on to the linen upgrade.

Here's the problem. That line item that looks exactly like a built-in gratuity often is not one. In most states, the venue or caterer keeps that 18-25% as part of their operating margin, and the servers who worked your wedding see none of it. So the people refilling water glasses and clearing plates for eight hours walk out with nothing, and you've mentally checked a box that was never actually checked.

The fix is not complicated, but it does require one question before you sign and a small line in your budget. Here are the six rules that sort out when a service charge counts as a tip and when you still owe cash.

## Rule 1 and 2: know what it is and who keeps it

Start with what you're actually looking at.

A service charge is a mandatory 18-25% line item added by the venue or caterer. It is not optional, it is not negotiable most of the time, and it shows up on nearly every catering and venue invoice. On a $22,000 catering bill, a 22% service charge is $4,840. That is real money, and it is easy to assume it's flowing to the staff.

It usually isn't. In most states, the house keeps it, not the servers. The service charge is legally different from a gratuity in a lot of places. A gratuity, by definition, goes to the person who served you. A service charge is revenue the business collects and can distribute however it wants, which frequently means it covers overhead, coordination, insurance, and margin. Some of it may reach staff as wages. Some of it may not reach them at all.

This is the single most misunderstood fee on a wedding invoice, which is exactly why it belongs in the [hidden-costs](/blog/category/hidden-costs) conversation and not the etiquette one. You are not being cheap by asking about it. You are being accurate.

## Rule 3: ask one question before you sign

You do not have to guess, and you should not. There is one question that settles it:

**Does any portion of the service charge go directly to the staff?**

Ask it before you sign, not after the wedding. Get the answer in writing if you can, even if that just means a follow-up email where you restate what they told you on the phone. Contracts are the right place for clarity like this, and if you want more on that, [reading the fine print](/blog/category/contracts) is a whole skill worth building.

Listen carefully to how they respond. There are really three possible answers:

1. **Yes, a defined portion goes to staff.** Ideal. Ask what percentage or what dollar amount, and whether it covers all staff or just the lead. If the number is generous, you may not need much additional cash at all.
2. **No, the service charge is a house fee.** Clear and honest. Now you know to budget separate tips, and you can plan for it without surprise.
3. **Vague, deflecting, or "it depends."** Treat this like a no. If a vendor cannot or will not tell you where your money goes, assume it is not reaching the servers and budget accordingly.

### Watch for these red flags

A few things should make you slow down and ask more questions:

- The invoice says "gratuity" and "service charge" as two separate lines, and you're being charged both. Sometimes legitimate, sometimes double-dipping. Ask what each one covers.
- The salesperson says "the tip is included" without being able to point to where it goes. "Included" and "goes to your servers" are not the same sentence.
- The contract is silent on distribution entirely. Silence usually favors the house.
- You're told tipping is "not necessary" but the staff are clearly working on standard wages. Not necessary for whom?

None of these mean your vendor is dishonest. They mean the language around this fee is genuinely confusing, and it is on you to get a straight answer.

## Rule 4: if the answer is no, still tip your servers

If they tell you the service charge does not reach the staff, build a separate cash envelope into your budget. The range most planners recommend is $20-50 per server in cash.

Do the math ahead of time. If your caterer is staffing your reception with, say, ten servers plus two bartenders, that's twelve people. At the middle of the range, roughly $35 each, you're looking at about $420 in cash. That is not a rounding error, and it is not something you want to discover you forgot at 11pm when the band is loading out.

Ask your caterer or coordinator how many staff will actually be on-site for your event. That headcount is what your envelope math should be based on, not a guess.

## Rule 5: tip the vendors who never had a service charge at all

Plenty of the people who make your wedding happen never send you a service charge in the first place. Photographers, planners, DJs, hair and makeup artists, and officiants usually just quote you a flat rate. There is no automatic line item, so tipping is entirely on you.

For vendors without a service charge at all, the standard is 10-15% or a flat $50-300 in an envelope. Here's how that shakes out in practice:

- **Photographer or videographer:** $50-300, often toward the higher end if they brought a second shooter or worked a very long day.
- **DJ or band:** $50-300 for a DJ; for a full band, tip per musician, closer to the $50 each range.
- **Wedding planner:** 10-15% of their fee, or a flat amount in the $50-300 range if a percentage feels steep.
- **Hair and makeup:** 10-15%, the same way you'd tip at a salon.
- **Officiant:** a $50-100 donation is common, especially if they're affiliated with a house of worship.

These are not mandatory in the legal sense, but they are the accepted standard, and skipping them is noticeable. If a vendor went above and beyond, the top of each range is where you land.

## Rule 6: set aside 2-3% of your total budget, in cash

Here is the simplest rule that makes all of the above painless: set aside 2-3% of your total wedding budget in cash before the wedding day.

On a $40,000 wedding, that is $800 to $1,200 in cash, budgeted from the start rather than scrambled together the week of. It sounds like a lot until you add up servers, bartenders, your photographer, your DJ, your planner, and hair and makeup. Then it looks about right.

The delivery matters as much as the amount. Hand it out in labeled envelopes at the end of the night. Write each person's role on the front. Better yet, hand the whole stack to your planner or a trusted family member and let them distribute it, so you are not tracking anyone down during your own reception.

No scrambling, no awkward moments, no math on a day you shouldn't be doing math.

### A quick pre-wedding tip checklist

1. Total your wedding budget and set aside 2-3% in cash.
2. Ask every venue and caterer: does any of the service charge go to staff directly?
3. Get the answer in writing before you sign.
4. Confirm your final on-site staff headcount for server math ($20-50 each).
5. List your no-service-charge vendors and assign each a number ($50-300 or 10-15%).
6. Fill labeled envelopes a few days before, and hand the stack to your planner.

## The takeaway

A service charge that looks like a tip is the most expensive assumption on your invoice, because it can leave you thinking you're done when you're not. You do not need to overpay, and you do not need to feel awkward. You need one question and one envelope.

Before you book, run this list:

- **Read the line item.** A service charge is a mandatory 18-25% fee, and in most states the house keeps it.
- **Ask the one question.** Does any portion go directly to staff? Vague counts as no.
- **Still tip servers** at $20-50 each in cash if the charge doesn't reach them.
- **Tip the flat-rate vendors** (photographer, planner, DJ) at 10-15% or $50-300.
- **Budget 2-3% of your total** in cash from day one.
- **Use labeled envelopes** and hand them out at the end of the night.
