Your mom asks if you can "just add the Hendersons." It's four people. You say yes, because four people sounds like nothing, and because saying no to your mom in the kitchen on a Tuesday is not a fight you want to have.

Four people is not nothing. Four people is roughly $568 at the low end and well over $1,600 at the high end, spread across six different vendor invoices you won't see side by side until much later. Catering bills you in March. The rental company bills you in May. The venue's cake cutting fee shows up on your final banquet check the week of the wedding, when you have exactly zero leverage.

That's the real problem with guest list math. It isn't that guests are expensive. It's that the expense is fragmented across six line items that never appear on the same page, so nobody feels the weight of one more chair until the last invoice lands.

Here's every line, priced out.

## the six line items every guest touches

Every single person you invite triggers all six of these. Not some. All six, unless you actively cut one.

1. **Catering: $85-$175 per head**, before service charge
2. **Bar package: $40-$90 per person**, priced per head not per drink
3. **Seat rental: $8-$25 per chair**, plus linen and delivery both ways
4. **Cake serving: $2-$5 a slice**, charged by the venue on top of the cake price
5. **Stationery: $4-$12 per invite suite**, plus postage both ways
6. **Favors: $3-$15 per person**, skippable but often the last add-on

Add the floors: roughly **$142 per guest**. Add the ceilings: **well over $400**. And that's before tax, service charge, or gratuity, which in most markets adds another 20-30% on top of the catering and bar lines specifically.

One extra table of ten moves your total by $1,420 at the absolute floor. At the ceiling, it's over $4,000. For one table. That's a photographer upgrade, or a band instead of a DJ, or the honeymoon flights.

### 01. catering, $85-$175 per head

This is the number most couples already know, and it's still the one that surprises them, because the quoted per-head price is almost never the per-head price you pay.

A $110 plated dinner quote is $110 for food. Then the venue adds a 22% service charge ($24.20), then sales tax on the food *and often on the service charge* depending on your state (call it another $9-$11), then a gratuity line if the service charge doesn't already cover staff. You're at $145 or so on a $110 quote.

Ask your caterer this exact question: "what is the all-in per-person number including service charge, tax, and any gratuity?" Write down the answer. That's your real catering line. Everything you budget from there should use that number, not the menu number.

Also watch for the vendor meals clause. Your photographer, videographer, planner, and band eat too, usually at a discounted rate of $35-$60 each. Six vendor meals is a real line item that nobody puts on their spreadsheet.

### 02. bar package, $40-$90 per person

The thing to understand about bar packages is right there in the slide: **priced per head, not per drink**. Your 78-year-old great aunt who nurses one glass of chardonnay costs the same as your college roommate. You are buying access, not consumption.

That cuts both ways. If your crowd drinks, a package is protective. If half your guest list is kids, elderly relatives, pregnant friends, and people who don't drink, you are paying full freight for a lot of empty glasses.

Run the comparison before you sign:

- **Package pricing** at $40-$90 per person for four to five hours
- **Consumption bar**, where you're billed for what's actually opened
- **Limited bar** (beer, wine, one or two signature cocktails), which usually lands at the low end of the package range

Ask your venue to price all three for your headcount. Some will resist. Push anyway. On a 120-person wedding, the spread between a $40 limited bar and a $90 premium open bar is $6,000.

### 03. seat rental, $8-$25 per chair, billed round-trip

Chairs are the sneakiest line on this list because the sticker price looks trivial. Eight dollars. Who cares about eight dollars?

Then you read the rental contract. That $8 chiavari is $8 per chair, plus delivery, plus pickup (the "both ways" part), plus a setup fee if the rental crew places them, plus a second setup fee if you're doing a ceremony-to-reception flip and need the same chairs moved. Linens land on the same invoice at $15-$30 per table. Cushions are sometimes separate.

A 120-guest wedding with $18 chairs is $2,160 in chairs, and the invoice total will be closer to $3,200 once delivery, pickup, and the flip are in. Ask your rental company for a line-item quote, not a bundled one, and ask specifically: **is delivery and pickup included, or billed separately?** Get it in writing. We go deeper on rental contract language over in [contracts](/blog/category/contracts).

### 04. cake serving, $2-$5 a slice

This is the one that makes people genuinely angry, and fairly so. You pay a baker for a cake. Then your venue charges you $2-$5 per slice to cut and plate the cake you already bought.

At 120 guests and $4 a slice, that's $480 to cut a cake. On top of the $600-$900 you paid the baker.

The fee exists because the venue is providing plates, forks, labor, and dishwashing. Fine. But it is negotiable more often than couples realize, and there are workarounds:

- Ask for the cake cutting fee to be waived as part of your catering package negotiation (this works surprisingly often when you're spending five figures on food)
- Do a small display cake for photos plus sheet cake in the back, and confirm whether the fee applies to the sheet cake
- Skip cake entirely for a dessert table the venue is already staffing

Whatever you do, find this number *before* you sign the venue contract. It's usually buried in the banquet event order addendum, not the headline pricing sheet.

### 05. stationery, $4-$12 per invite suite, plus postage both ways

Stationery is priced per household, not per guest, which sounds like a break until you count how many pieces are in a "suite": invitation, response card, details card, outer envelope, inner envelope, sometimes a belly band or vellum overlay.

At $4-$12 per suite, a 70-household list is $280 to $840 in paper. Then postage, **both ways**. Outer envelope postage has gone up, and if your invitation is square, oversized, or thick enough to trigger a non-machinable surcharge, you're paying roughly double per piece. You're also buying stamps for every RSVP return envelope, and a meaningful number of those never get mailed back.

Add save-the-dates and you've run the paper line twice. Budget the full suite, not just the invitation.

### 06. favors, $3-$15 per person

Technically optional. Almost always the last thing couples add, usually in the final six weeks when decision fatigue is highest and $6 a person feels like a rounding error.

It isn't. At 120 guests and $8 each, that's $960 spent on something that has a real chance of being left on the table at the end of the night. If favors matter to you, budget them at the start and protect the line. If they don't, cut them first. They're the cleanest $360-$1,800 you'll ever save.

## watch for these

- **Quoted prices that exclude service charge and tax.** A $95 per-head quote is functionally $125. Always ask for the all-in number.
- **"Per chair" that doesn't include delivery and pickup.** The round-trip fee can add 30-40% to a rental invoice.
- **Cake cutting fees hidden in the BEO addendum**, not the main contract. Find them before you sign.
- **Guest count minimums.** Many venues have a floor (say, 100 guests). If your list is 85, you're paying for 100 anyway, and adding 15 people genuinely is free. Know which side of the minimum you're on.
- **Kids and vendors counted as full guests** by some caterers and not others. Ask.
- **Postage surcharges** on square or oversized invitations. Weigh and measure a full sample suite at the post office before you order 80 of them.
- **The "it's only four more people" conversation** happening after contracts are signed. Most caterers let you raise your count up to about 10 days out. Lowering it is much harder.

## how to actually use this

Before you finalize your list, do this:

1. Get the all-in per-person number from your caterer, including service charge, tax, and gratuity.
2. Price your bar three ways (package, consumption, limited) at your current headcount.
3. Ask your rental company whether delivery and pickup are included or billed separately.
4. Find the cake cutting fee in your venue contract before you sign it.
5. Weigh a full stationery sample at the post office, then budget postage both ways.
6. Decide on favors now, not in week 44. Either fund the line or kill it.
7. Add up all six. Multiply by ten. That's what one more table costs, and that's the number to bring to the guest list conversation.
