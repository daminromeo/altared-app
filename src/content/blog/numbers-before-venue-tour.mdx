you are standing in a barn with string lights already up, a coordinator is telling you the sunset hits the ceremony lawn at 7:40 in september, and she asks how many guests you are expecting. you say "um, like 150? maybe 180? we haven't finalized." she nods, writes something down, and quotes you a number that may or may not apply to you. you leave the tour in love, with a PDF you cannot actually evaluate.

showing up to a venue tour without these six numbers is like going to a car lot with no idea what you can spend. you will fall in love with something you cannot afford, or walk away from something perfect because you were not prepared.

here are the six numbers. write them on your phone, in a note, before tour number one.

## the two numbers that set your ceiling

guest count and total budget are not details you figure out after you find the venue. they are the filter that decides which venues are worth touring at all.

### 01. guest count

venues build pricing tiers around headcount, so know your range before you ask. most venues have a minimum spend, a food and beverage minimum, or a flat site fee that shifts at 100, 150, and 200 guests. a venue that is reasonable at 180 can be wildly overpriced at 90, because you are paying a minimum you will never hit.

you do not need a final list. you need a range with a floor and a ceiling: "130 invited, we expect 100 to 115 to come." say that sentence out loud on the tour. the coordinator will immediately tell you whether you are in their sweet spot or fighting their pricing structure.

one thing couples underestimate: the difference between invited and attending. a venue quoting you per-person catering at 150 when you are realistically hosting 115 is quoting you a wedding that does not exist. get clear on both numbers before you walk in.

### 02. total budget

know the most you will spend on the venue, before you fall in love with one over it.

this is a venue number, not a wedding number. if your all-in wedding budget is $45,000 and you have decided venue plus catering should not exceed $20,000, then $20,000 is the number you carry into the tour. venues will often ask for your total wedding budget, and some will quietly design a proposal that consumes most of it. answering with your venue ceiling instead keeps the conversation honest.

a useful move: decide your ceiling and your "we would stretch for this" number before any tour. maybe the ceiling is $18,000 and the stretch is $20,500. when a quote comes in at $23,000, you already know the answer, and you are not making that decision while standing under a chandelier.

if you have not built a working budget yet, start there before you book tours. our [budgeting posts](/blog/category/budgeting) walk through how to split the total across venue, catering, photo, and the rest.

## the number that decides if there is anything to discuss

### 03. desired date (plus two or three backups)

peak weekends book 12-18 months out. if you walk into a tour with one date and no flexibility, you are rolling dice. the coordinator pulls up the calendar, that saturday is taken, and the tour is effectively over before it starts.

have 2-3 backup dates ready too. and be strategic about what the backups are:

1. **your first-choice date.** the one with the meaning behind it.
2. **the same month, different weekend.** usually the easiest swap, same season, same light, same weather odds.
3. **a shoulder-season date.** may or october instead of june or september. this is where you find pricing relief.
4. **a friday or sunday.** many venues discount these meaningfully, and the guests who matter will come anyway.

bring all of them. ask what is open across all four before you ask about price. availability is binary, and it saves you from negotiating over a date that does not exist.

## the three numbers hiding in the contract

these are the ones that catch couples. the site fee is on the website. these are not.

### 04. deposit size

venue deposits run $1,000-$5,000+ to hold a date. know what you can wire that week.

this matters more than people expect, because venue availability moves fast and most venues will not hold a date on a handshake. if the coordinator says "we have another couple looking at october 10th, the deposit holds it," you need to know in that moment whether you can move $2,500 by friday.

so before the tour, answer three things:

1. what is the maximum deposit we can pay this month without touching money earmarked for something else?
2. which account is it coming from, and how fast can we move it?
3. is it refundable, partially refundable, or gone the second it clears?

that third one is a question for the coordinator, and you should ask it on the tour, not after. also ask what the payment schedule looks like after the deposit. a $2,000 deposit followed by 50% due at six months out is a very different cash-flow picture than a $2,000 deposit with the balance due two weeks before.

### 05. hour block and overtime

most venues sell 6-10 hour blocks; overtime runs $150-$500 per extra hour. hour block length and overtime rates determine whether your reception ends on a dancefloor or in a parking lot.

an 8-hour block sounds generous until you lay out the actual day. ceremony at 5:00. cocktail hour. dinner. toasts. first dance. by the time the party is genuinely going, you are at 9:30 and your block ends at 11:00 with vendor breakdown inside that window.

ask these four questions about the block:

1. **when does the clock start?** vendor load-in, or guest arrival? this can be a two-hour difference.
2. **is breakdown inside the block?** if your band needs 45 minutes to strike and that is on your clock, your party ends 45 minutes earlier than you think.
3. **what does overtime cost per hour, and is it pre-approved or decided night-of?** $150 an extra hour is a reasonable call to make at 10:45pm. $500 an hour is a decision you want to make in advance, sober, with a number in the contract.
4. **is there a hard cutoff?** some venues have a noise ordinance or a municipal curfew and cannot sell you overtime at any price.

do the math on the tour. if a $14,000 venue with a 6-hour block means you will buy two hours of overtime at $350, that venue costs $14,700, and it should be compared that way against the $15,200 venue with a 10-hour block.

### 06. liability limit

this is the line item nearly every couple misses until the contract arrives.

many venues require $1M-$2M in event liability coverage, and a one-day event insurance policy runs $75-$250 depending on your state. that is not a surprise you want the week before you sign.

on its own, $75-$250 will not break a budget. the problem is timing and requirements. some venues require the certificate of insurance before they will release your date. some require the venue to be named as an additional insured, which means you cannot buy a generic policy and call it done. some require host liquor liability on top of general liability if you are serving alcohol, which is a separate line.

ask on the tour: what coverage limit do you require, do you need to be named as additional insured, and is host liquor liability required separately? write the answers down. that is a thirty-second question that prevents a scramble later. there are more of these buried costs than most couples expect, and we cover them in our [hidden costs](/blog/category/hidden-costs) posts.

## red flags on a venue tour

a tour is a sales meeting. that is fine, it is supposed to be. but a few things should make you slow down:

- **the coordinator will not give you a written quote.** "i'll put something together" with no timeline usually means pricing is flexible in a direction that is not yours.
- **"this rate is only good today."** real venues hold pricing for a stated period. artificial urgency on a decision this size is a reason to leave.
- **the contract is not available to read before deposit.** you should be able to read the full agreement, including overtime, insurance, cancellation, and vendor rules, before any money moves.
- **vague answers about what the block includes.** if nobody can tell you clearly whether setup and breakdown are inside your hours, assume they are and price accordingly.
- **a required vendor list with no pricing attached.** exclusive caterers and required rental partners can add thousands. ask for their pricing on the tour, not after you sign.
- **a deposit that is non-refundable with no date-change clause.** ask what happens if you need to move the date. get the answer in writing.

## bring these six numbers to every tour

1. **guest count range.** invited and realistically attending.
2. **venue budget ceiling.** plus the number you would stretch to, decided in advance.
3. **desired date plus 2-3 backups.** including a shoulder-season or friday/sunday option.
4. **deposit you can actually wire this week.** venue deposits run $1,000-$5,000+.
5. **hour block you need, and the overtime rate you will accept.** overtime runs $150-$500 per extra hour.
6. **liability coverage the venue requires.** usually $1M-$2M, policy cost $75-$250.

walk out of every tour with a written quote that names all six. then compare venues on total real cost, not on the headline site fee.
