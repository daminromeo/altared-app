your cousin texts at 11pm: "hey! can i bring someone?" no name. no context. you know she's been seeing this guy for about six weeks. you also know your caterer's final headcount is due in nine days, and you're already three seats over the number you built the budget around.

that is the whole problem with plus ones. the request always arrives as a feelings conversation, and it always lands as a numbers conversation.

plus ones are not just a feelings conversation. they are a numbers conversation. at $75-250 per head, one extra seat is a real line item, and having a clear rule makes it so much easier to hold. so before rsvp season starts, you need four things: a locked number, a real cost, one consistent policy, and something to offer instead of a seat.

here are the four scripts, in the order you should use them.

## why the guest list is where budgets actually break

most couples think the budget breaks on the dress, or the flowers, or a photographer upgrade they said yes to at 10pm. it usually breaks on the guest list, because the guest list multiplies.

per-head cost runs $75-250 per guest. that is catering, but it is also the chair, the place setting, the linen, the welcome drink, the extra bottle of wine on the table, the slice of cake, the favor, and sometimes a share of the bar minimum. one plus one can mean $200 you don't have.

now run that forward. if ten people each bring an unvetted guest, that is not ten awkward conversations. at the middle of that range, it is thousands of dollars, a bigger tent, a second shuttle run, and possibly a venue capacity problem. it compounds quietly, one text at a time, which is exactly why "we'll just figure it out" is the most expensive sentence in wedding planning.

if you don't know your own per-head number yet, get it before you answer a single plus one request. ask your caterer what the fully loaded cost per guest is, including service and rentals, not just the food price on the proposal. that one number turns every future conversation from a debate into arithmetic. ## script 01: the number

**lead with the headcount, not the person.**

> "we are locked at 80 guests total."

this is the whole strategy in one sentence. the moment you say "we don't think you should bring jake," you are now in a conversation about jake. is jake nice? is jake serious? does aunt patty like jake? you will lose that conversation, because it is unwinnable and also none of your business.

when you lead with the number, there is nothing to argue with. 80 is 80. the venue holds what it holds. the number is not a judgment of anyone, it is a fact that existed before they texted.

a few notes on delivering it:

- say "locked," not "trying to stay around." soft language invites negotiation. locked is a door.
- use "we." this is a decision two people made together, not a preference one person is enforcing.
- do not explain your seating chart. you are not presenting evidence. one sentence, then stop talking.

### what this sounds like in real life

them: "can i bring someone?"

you: "i wish we could, but we are locked at 80 guests total and we're already right at the line. it's going to be a tiny one."

that's it. no apology spiral. warm, short, done.

## script 02: the cost

**per-head cost runs $75-250 per guest.**

> "one plus one can mean $200 you don't have."

you will not use this script with everyone. you should not. your coworker does not need your catering invoice.

but you will use it with the people who are genuinely close to you and genuinely pushing, and with the people who are helping pay. parents who added six names to your list are often working from a 1998 mental model where an extra guest costs the price of a chicken dinner. naming the actual number changes the conversation instantly.

> "we'd love to, but each additional guest is about $200 once you count the catering, rentals, and bar. adding four people is another $800 and we'd have to pull it out of photography."

notice what that does. it moves the decision from "do you like my boyfriend" to "which line item do we cut." nobody wants to be the reason photography got downgraded. this is also the version of the conversation that works on yourself, on the night you're tempted to add three people because you feel guilty. guilt is not a budget.

## script 03: the rule

**set one policy and apply it to everyone.**

> "plus ones are for couples together 1+ year."

this is the piece that keeps you from being the villain. a rule that applies to everyone is a policy. a decision made guest by guest is favoritism, and people can smell it.

common policies that hold up:

1. **married, engaged, or living together.** the cleanest and easiest to defend. no one has to define anything.
2. **together one year or more.** couples together one year or more is a common one, and it gives you a bright line that doesn't require you to judge the relationship.
3. **everyone in the wedding party gets one.** simple, generous to the people doing the most work, and easy to say out loud.
4. **no plus ones for anyone outside the above.** yes, including your best friend's new person. consistency is the entire point.

then write it down. literally put it in a note on your phone, because you will be asked eight separate times over four months and your answer has to be identical every time. the second you make one exception you cannot defend, the policy is gone and you are back to negotiating with everyone individually.

> "our rule is plus ones for couples together a year or more, and we've kept it the same for everybody, including my brother. i hate being rigid about it but it's the only way the number works."

adding "including my brother" is not a throwaway. it proves the rule is real.

### the one exception worth making

if someone will genuinely know no one at your wedding, and they are traveling alone, consider giving them a plus one regardless. that is not favoritism, it is a different category: guest comfort. just be able to say why it is a category, and apply it consistently to everyone in it.

## script 04: the offer

**redirect with something real to look forward to.**

> "we are doing a post-wedding dinner, you'll meet them then."

this is the script people skip, and it is the one that prevents resentment. a no with nothing after it feels like a door closing. a no with a next thing feels like a scheduling issue.

and if someone pushes back, redirect to something they can look forward to after the wedding.

> "i'm so sorry, the number is locked. but we're doing a dinner a few weeks after with everyone's people, and i genuinely want to meet him properly then, not for four minutes while i'm being pulled toward a first dance."

it has to be real. do not invent a dinner you will never host. options that actually work:

- a casual post-wedding dinner or backyard thing, six to eight weeks out
- a brunch or drinks the week you get back from the honeymoon
- an open invite to the engagement party or a shower, if those are happening
- a genuine plan: "let's do dinner, the four of us, in march"

one more thing here. the offer is also for you. it takes the pressure off the wedding day being the only moment you ever get to meet the people in your friends' lives.

## red flags and things to watch for

these are the moves that quietly blow up a headcount. watch for:

- **"and guest" on an invitation you didn't intend.** if your policy is no plus one, the envelope and the rsvp card should name only the invited guest. do not leave a blank line. a blank line reads as an invitation.
- **the rsvp that adds a name.** someone writes in a second name on the card. respond within 48 hours, by phone or text, not by ignoring it. silence gets read as yes.
- **the escalation through a parent.** when a request comes to you secondhand, answer it directly with the guest, not through the messenger. policies get softened in translation.
- **the "it's just one person" from someone paying.** the fastest fix is the cost script and a specific tradeoff.
- **your own late-night guilt adds.** the 11pm additions are the ones you regret, because they always arrive in pairs.
- **a caterer who won't quote a fully loaded per-head number.** if they'll only quote food and vaguely gesture at "service charges," you cannot hold a headcount you can't price.
- **rsvp deadlines with no consequence.** pick a date, say what happens after it, and mean it.

none of this is cold. it is just honest, and it keeps you from resenting a guest list you can't afford.

## the short version

1. know your fully loaded per-head cost before you answer anyone. per-head cost runs $75-250 per guest.
2. lead with the headcount, not the person: "we are locked at 80 guests total."
3. use the cost only with people close enough to hear it, and name a specific tradeoff.
4. write one policy, apply it to everyone including family, and repeat it word for word.
5. pair every no with a real invitation to something after the wedding.
6. never print "and guest" unless you mean it, and answer written-in names within 48 hours.
7. track every guest and every quote in one place so the number stops being a guess.

save this before the rsvp chaos starts. the headcount holds.
