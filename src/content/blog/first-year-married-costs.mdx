Six weeks after the wedding, a couple sits at the kitchen table with a laptop, a folder of receipts, and a stack of unaddressed thank-you cards. The venue is paid. The photographer delivered the gallery. The credit card that carried the florist deposit is finally back at zero.

Then the passport renewal fee comes up on screen. Then the DMV appointment. Then the jeweler emails back about insuring the ring, quoting an annual premium that nobody had penciled in anywhere. Then, months later, a tax bill that looked nothing like last year's.

the wedding budget gets all the attention. the first year of marriage? nobody talks about what that actually costs.

None of these are line items on a wedding invoice. All of them show up anyway. Here are the four that hit hardest, what they actually run, and how to get ahead of them while you still have a spreadsheet open.

## 01. Name change fees stack to $200+ before you're done

Changing your name sounds free. It isn't. It's a sequence of small government fees, each one reasonable on its own, that add up to a number you didn't plan for.

The typical path looks like this:

1. **Certified marriage certificate copies.** You need the certified version, not the pretty keepsake one. Most couples end up ordering two or three because agencies want originals and don't always return them quickly.
2. **Social Security card update.** This is the first stop, because most other agencies want the Social Security record updated before they'll process anything.
3. **New driver's license or state ID.** Fees vary by state, and some states charge a full renewal price rather than a duplicate-card price.
4. **Passport update or renewal.** This is the one that quietly doubles the total. Whether you qualify for the cheaper correction process or the full renewal depends on how recently your passport was issued, and the difference is significant.
5. **Everything downstream.** Bank accounts, credit cards, car title, voter registration, professional licenses, insurance policies, 401(k) beneficiaries. Most of these are free, but they cost time, and several require notarized forms.

passport, license, social security, fees stack to $200+ before you're done. That's the floor, not the ceiling. If you need expedited passport processing because you booked a honeymoon under a name that doesn't match your ticket yet, add more. If you hire a name-change service to handle the paperwork, add more again.

### the timing trap

Book the honeymoon in your current legal name. Airlines require the name on the ticket to match the name on the passport, and a mid-process name change is exactly how people end up paying change fees on top of everything else. Change the name after you travel, not before.

## 02. Ring insurance runs 1-2% of the ring's value, every year

Here's the assumption that costs people the most: "it's covered under our renter's policy."

Sometimes it is, partially. Most standard homeowner's and renter's policies cap jewelry coverage at a low sublimit, and that cap often applies to all jewelry combined. Worse, many policies only cover theft, not loss. The ring that slides off in the ocean or disappears down a sink drain is not a theft claim.

ring insurance runs 1-2% of the ring's value every year, and most homeowner policies won't touch it.

Run the math on a real ring. At 1-2% annually:

- A $5,000 ring costs roughly $50 to $100 a year to insure.
- A $10,000 ring costs roughly $100 to $200 a year.
- A $20,000 set (ring plus band, plus a partner's band) costs roughly $200 to $400 a year.

That's a recurring cost, not a one-time one. Over a decade of marriage it's real money, and it's money nobody put on the wedding spreadsheet because the ring was bought before the spreadsheet existed.

### what to actually ask for

When you call, use these words:

1. **"Scheduled personal property"** or a standalone jewelry policy, not a blanket rider.
2. **"All-risk" or "all-peril" coverage**, which includes mysterious disappearance (the ocean, the drain, the gym locker).
3. **No deductible**, which many specialty jewelry insurers offer and most homeowner policies do not.
4. **Agreed value**, so you know exactly what you get paid rather than arguing over depreciation.
5. **Worldwide coverage**, which matters on the honeymoon specifically.

You'll need a current appraisal. Most insurers want one dated within the last few years, and appraisals themselves usually cost $50 to $150 depending on the jeweler. Some jewelers include one with the purchase, so check your original paperwork before you pay for a new one.

## 03. Filing jointly can shift your bracket by around $1,200

This is the one that genuinely blindsides people, because it arrives months after the wedding when the budget folder is closed.

filing taxes jointly for the first time can shift your bracket in ways that catch couples off guard. an average $1,200 swing year one is more common than people admit.

The short version: your marital status for the entire tax year is determined by your status on December 31. Get married on December 28 and you file as married for that whole year. Two incomes that were each withheld as "single" can combine into a household that lands differently than either person's W-4 assumed.

Some couples get a marriage bonus and pay less. Some get hit. The couples most likely to owe more tend to be the ones where both partners earn similar, solid incomes, because the combined figure pushes into a higher bracket than either salary did alone. Couples with very different incomes more often come out ahead.

The problem isn't really the tax. It's the surprise. A $1,200 bill in April is manageable if you saw it coming in September. It is genuinely painful if it arrives the same month the credit card statement with the honeymoon on it does.

### three moves that defuse it

1. **Update your W-4 within 30 days of the wedding.** Both of you. The IRS withholding estimator is free and takes about twenty minutes with two recent pay stubs.
2. **Run a rough projection in the fall**, not in April. If you're going to owe, you want four months of notice, not four weeks.
3. **Compare filing jointly versus separately once.** For most couples jointly wins, but if one of you has income-driven student loan payments or large medical expenses, the math can flip. A single conversation with a tax preparer costs less than guessing wrong.

## 04. Thank-you cards: $150-250, with a three-month clock

The cards feel like an afterthought. They're not, because there are two costs and one deadline.

thank-you cards, 100 printed cards plus postage, run $150-250 with a three-month etiquette clock ticking.

Break down where that goes:

- Printed cards, typically $70 to $150 for 100 depending on paper weight, foil, and whether you're using a custom photo card
- Postage for 100 cards, which is the piece people forget entirely
- Envelope addressing, if you're paying for printed or calligraphed addresses rather than doing it yourselves
- Extras, because you will need more cards than you think once you count shower gifts, engagement gifts, and vendors you want to thank

The three-month window is the part with teeth. Traditional etiquette says send within three months of the wedding, and the practical reason is that a card sent at month two feels gracious while a card sent at month ten feels like an apology. Writing 100 cards in one weekend is miserable. Writing ten a night for ten nights is fine. Start while the gifts are still arriving, not after.

## red flags to watch for

- **"It's covered" without a policy number.** If nobody can tell you the jewelry sublimit on your renter's policy, assume the ring isn't properly covered.
- **A jeweler who insists you insure through their partner only.** Get an outside quote. The 1-2% range is your benchmark, and anything meaningfully above it deserves a question.
- **A honeymoon booked in a name that doesn't match your passport yet.** This is a change fee waiting to happen.
- **Withholding you never touched after the wedding.** If neither W-4 changed, you are choosing to find out in April.
- **A stationer quoting cards without quoting postage.** Postage on 100 cards is a real number. Make them include it.
- **Ordering exactly 100 cards.** You will need more. Order the overage now, when it's cheap, instead of reordering a small batch later at a higher per-unit cost.

## the short version

Build these four into the budget before the wedding, not after it:

1. **Name change: $200+.** Certified certificates, Social Security, license, passport. Travel first, change second.
2. **Ring insurance: 1-2% of ring value, annually.** Standalone or scheduled coverage, all-risk, worldwide, with a current appraisal.
3. **Taxes: plan for roughly a $1,200 swing in year one.** Update both W-4s within 30 days and project in the fall.
4. **Thank-you cards: $150-250 for 100 cards plus postage.** Three-month clock. Ten a night beats one brutal weekend.

That's $1,500 to $1,900 of first-year cost sitting outside every wedding invoice you'll receive. It isn't a reason to panic. It's a reason to write it down now, while you're already in budget mode, instead of discovering it one line item at a time.
