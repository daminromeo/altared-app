the invoice arrives eleven days after the wedding. the venue quote you signed said $18,400. the final number says $21,960. you read the line items twice: a bar shortfall, a coat check staff fee, generator use for the band, forty minutes of overtime, a cleaning charge because someone's aunt threw rose petals in the courtyard, and eleven vendor meals at $45 each.

none of those lines were in the original proposal. all of them were technically in the contract.

the venue quote is a starting point, not a total. the real number shows up on the wedding day, and by then you have already signed. here are the six charges that most reliably appear after the fact, what they actually cost, and the exact language to ask for before your signature goes anywhere near that document.

## 01. the bar minimum you didn't hit

venue bars often carry a $3,000-$5,000 spend floor. it sounds generous when you're touring in march and picturing 140 guests ordering old fashioneds. then your actual crowd turns out to be light drinkers, half the table is pregnant or sober, and the bar rings up $3,100 against a $4,500 minimum.

you owe the gap. that's $1,400 for drinks nobody ordered.

the minimum is not a scam, exactly. the venue staffs bartenders and stocks inventory based on that number. but it's underwritten with your money, and the number is almost always negotiable before signing and never after.

### what to ask before you sign

- can the minimum be met with food and beverage combined, not beverage alone?
- if we fall short, can the shortfall convert to a credit (bottles to take home, a late-night snack service, an upgraded liquor tier) instead of a straight charge?
- is the minimum pre-tax and pre-service-charge, or does the 22% service charge count toward it? this single question can move the number by over a thousand dollars.

if your guest list skews toward light drinkers, say so out loud during the tour. a venue that wants your saturday in october will often drop the floor by $500-$1,000 rather than lose the booking.

## 02. coat check, billed per head

this one is small and irritating and hits every winter wedding. coat check is billed per guest or as a flat $150-$400 staff fee, and it rarely appears in the original quote.

the logic is that it's a labor line, not a venue line, so it lives in a separate staffing addendum you never asked to see. for a november wedding in a venue with a real lobby, it isn't optional. guests arrive in coats and the coats go somewhere.

ask for it in writing: "is coat check staffing included in the rental, or billed separately? if separately, is it flat or per guest, and at what rate?" then ask whether you can self-serve with a rack and a sign. some venues allow it, some don't for insurance reasons, but you will never find out from the proposal pdf.

## 03. the power surcharge nobody mentions until load-in

live bands and photo booths pull extra power. a ten-piece band with amps, a lighting designer, a neon sign, and a photo booth running for four hours can exceed what a historic barn or a converted warehouse has wired.

venues charge $200-$600 for generator use, and this one has the worst timing of all six. it usually surfaces at load-in, when your band's sound engineer walks the space at 2pm and says the circuits won't hold. at that point you are not negotiating. you are paying.

### how to get ahead of it

1. ask your band or dj for their power requirements in writing (amps and number of dedicated circuits).
2. forward that document to your venue coordinator before signing.
3. ask the venue to confirm in the contract that existing power is sufficient for that spec, or to quote the generator fee up front.
4. if a generator is required, ask whether you can source it yourself. rental houses often beat the venue's markup.

this takes one email. it is the highest-return email in your entire planning process.

## 04. overtime, and how fast midnight arrives

most venues charge $250-$500 per half-hour past contract end. clocks run fast at midnight.

here is how it actually happens: your contract ends at 12:00am. at 11:40 the dance floor is finally, genuinely full. someone requests one more song. the photographer is still shooting. your uncle is holding a drink in the courtyard. at 12:12 the venue manager finds you, and you have already bought a half hour at $400.

then load-out runs long, because your florist has to break down twelve centerpieces and the band has to strike, and now you are into a second half hour. two extra half-hours is $500-$1,000 on a night you thought was over.

what to do:

- ask whether overtime is billed in half-hour increments or rounded up to the full hour. the difference is real money.
- ask whether vendor load-out counts against your contracted end time. at many venues it does, and nobody tells you.
- negotiate a pre-purchased overtime rate. buying an extra half hour in advance is usually cheaper than buying it at 12:12am, and if you don't use it, ask whether it's refundable.
- assign one person (planner, coordinator, a decisive friend) to make the overtime call at 11:45 so you're not making a $400 decision while holding a sparkler.

## 05. cleaning fees triggered by decor you thought was fine

confetti, petals, glitter: $300-$800 extra if the contract lists prohibited decor. the word "prohibited" is doing enormous work there, because the list is usually buried in an appendix, and it's usually longer than you'd guess. real petals. dried petals. biodegradable confetti. glitter of any kind. sparklers. sand. sometimes candles without hurricane glass.

the charge is rarely discretionary. if the contract says $500 for confetti cleanup and a guest throws confetti, you pay $500, even though you never bought confetti and have no idea who did.

practical fix: find the prohibited decor list, photograph it, and send it to your florist, your planner, and whoever is running your exit. if you want a petal toss, ask for written approval and a quoted cleanup fee up front. a venue will frequently approve it for a flat $150 rather than enforce the $800 penalty, but only if you ask in advance.

## 06. vendor meals, at real-person prices

venues bill $25-$75 per vendor head. photographers, djs, planners all count. so do second shooters, videographers, assistants, band members, and hair and makeup if they're staying through the reception.

do the math on a normal vendor team: photographer, second shooter, videographer, dj, coordinator, two hair and makeup artists staying for touch-ups. that's eight meals. at $45 each, that's $360. at $75 each with a ten-piece band added in, you're past $1,300.

most vendor contracts require a meal for anyone on-site more than five or six hours, so this is not a line you can simply cut. what you can do:

- ask the venue for a reduced-price vendor meal option (many have one at roughly half the guest plate price, they just don't volunteer it).
- count your actual vendor heads, including assistants, before you sign the catering contract.
- confirm whether vendor meals are subject to the same service charge and tax as guest plates. sometimes they aren't, and sometimes the venue bills them as though they are.

## the red flags to watch for on a walkthrough

you can usually tell which venues will surprise you. watch for:

- **a proposal with no line items.** one number, one page, "all inclusive." all-inclusive of what? get the itemized version.
- **"we'll figure that out closer to the date."** anything figured out closer to the date is figured out at a price you don't control.
- **a service charge percentage that's mentioned verbally but not printed.** 20% versus 24% on a $20,000 food and beverage spend is $800.
- **refusal to put a verbal promise in the contract.** if a coordinator says "oh, we never charge for that," the answer is "great, can we add that line?" a reasonable venue says yes. a venue that says "our standard contract doesn't allow changes" is telling you how the rest of the relationship will go.
- **an appendix you haven't read.** the prohibited decor list, the staffing addendum, and the overtime schedule live there. read the appendix.

read the contract before you fall in love with the space. if a line is missing, ask. venues will often cap or waive fees you bring up in writing before signing, not after. that sentence is the whole post, honestly.

## before your next venue tour

1. ask for the itemized proposal, not the summary number, and ask for every appendix.
2. get the bar minimum in writing, including whether food and service charges count toward it.
3. send your band or dj's power spec to the venue and get written confirmation about circuits and generator fees.
4. confirm the overtime rate, the increment (half hour or full hour), and whether load-out counts against your end time.
5. photograph the prohibited decor list and send it to your florist and planner.
6. count your real vendor head count, assistants included, and ask for the reduced vendor meal rate.
7. put every "we usually don't charge for that" into the contract as a written line before you sign.

running each of these questions costs you one email and maybe twenty minutes. skipping them costs somewhere between $1,000 and $4,000, delivered eleven days after the wedding when you have no leverage left.
