four months after her wedding, a bride we talked to found a box in her parents' garage. inside were eleven unopened cards, a gravy boat, and a note from her mother's college roommate who had rsvp'd no and mailed a gift anyway. nobody had logged it. the gift table got loaded into a car at midnight by someone's uncle and the box never made it back into the story.

she wrote the note. it was four months late and slightly mortifying and the roommate was delighted anyway. but that box is the whole problem in one image: the people who show up quietly are the people who fall off the list.

the gift givers are obvious. the vendors, the wedding party, the family who hosted your shower are a little less obvious. the people who rsvp'd no and still sent something, or the coordinator who held the whole day together quietly? somehow they end up at the bottom of the list, or off it entirely.

thank you notes are not just etiquette. they close the loop on every person who gave time, money, or energy to get you to the altar. here is the full list, in the order most couples work through it, plus the three groups that get skipped.

## the timeline rule that actually holds up

the usual rule is within three months, and sooner for anyone who travelled to be there.

three months sounds generous until you are back at work, unpacking a honeymoon suitcase, and staring at 140 cards. so split it:

1. **within two weeks of the wedding:** anyone who travelled. flights, hotel nights, two days of pto. they spent real money to stand in a room with you and they should hear from you first.
2. **within one month:** your wedding party, your family hosts, and anyone who gave a gift before the wedding (engagement party, shower, early mailers).
3. **within two months:** everyone else who gave a gift at or after the wedding.
4. **within two months:** your vendors, paired with a public review while the details are still sharp in your memory.
5. **the moment you remember:** anyone you missed. a late note beats no note, always. do not spiral about it, just write it.

if a gift arrives after the wedding (and they will keep trickling in for weeks), the three month clock starts when it arrives, not on your wedding date.

### log gifts as they arrive, not after

the single biggest reason notes go unwritten is that nobody knows who gave what. the card separates from the box. the shower gift gets mixed in with the wedding gifts. venmo transfers sit in a feed with coffee payments.

assign one person at the shower and one person on the wedding day to write down giver and gift as things are opened or received. a phone note is fine. a shared list in whatever you are already using to [track every cost, timeline, and detail in one place](/get-started) is better, because then it lives next to your guest list instead of in someone's camera roll.

## 01. gift givers

the obvious one, and still the one that takes the longest. aim for within three months, sooner for anyone who travelled to be there.

what makes a gift note land is naming the gift and naming the use. "thank you for the beautiful serving platter" is fine. "thank you for the serving platter, it got christened on sunday with an aggressively large lasagna" is the one they read twice.

for cash or a honeymoon fund contribution, you do not name the amount. name what it did: "your gift is going toward the diving day in tulum, which was the single best thing we did all week."

a few things worth knowing:

- a group gift gets individual notes, not one note to the group. if eight coworkers chipped in, that is eight short notes.
- a gift you are returning or exchanging still gets a warm, honest note. you just do not mention the return.
- a gift that arrives damaged still gets a thank you. handle the damage with the retailer, separately and quietly.

## 02. your vendors

photographer, florist, caterer. a note plus a public review is the real thank you.

this is the group couples skip because they think payment covers it. payment covers the work. it does not cover the florist who rebuilt an arch at 6am because the wind took it, or the caterer who plated a separate meal for your gluten-free grandmother without being asked twice.

**the note is nice. the review is the actual gift.** a specific, named, five-star review on google or their platform of choice is worth more to a small vendor than almost anything else you could hand them, because it directly affects their bookings next season. write it while you still remember the details.

a strong vendor review names:

1. what you booked them for and the approximate scale (guest count, hours)
2. one specific thing that went wrong or nearly went wrong, and how they handled it
3. one specific thing they did that was not in the contract
4. whether you would book them again

if you also want to tip, tip in cash on the day or in the week after, not three months later. if you want a sense of what is standard and what gets padded into a final invoice, our [hidden costs](/blog/category/hidden-costs) posts cover the gratuity and service charge math.

## 03. wedding party

they paid for their own attire, travel, and a lot of group chats. name what they did.

a bridesmaid in a destination wedding can easily be out $800 to $1,500 once you add the dress, alterations, shoes, hair, flights, and two nights in a hotel block. groomsmen are rarely cheaper once the suit rental and the bachelor weekend land. that is a meaningful amount of money handed over out of love, and a generic "thanks for being part of our day" does not meet it.

name what they did. specifically.

- "you drove four hours with my dress in your back seat and did not let me see it wrinkle."
- "you handled my aunt at the rehearsal dinner and i will never forget it."
- "you answered 300 messages in that group chat and never once said you were busy."

if you gave gifts on the morning of the wedding, you still write a note after. the gift is the gesture, the note is the gratitude, and they are not interchangeable.

## 04. guests who couldn't come

they rsvp'd no and still sent a gift. they are the easiest ones to forget.

the logic of why this happens is simple and bleak: your thank you list usually gets built from the seating chart or the day-of guest list. if someone was not in the room, they are not on the list.

so build your list from the **gift log**, not the attendance list, and cross-reference the two. the people in this group tend to be far-flung family, your parents' close friends, and former colleagues. they get nothing out of sending a gift except the act of sending it, which makes them exactly the people you want to acknowledge.

their note should not open with an apology that they missed it. open with the gift, mention that you were thinking of them, and keep it warm.

## 05. family hosts

whoever hosted a shower, rehearsal dinner, or brunch. hosting costs real money.

a shower with 30 guests, even a casual one at someone's house, runs into real catering, rental, and decor spend. a rehearsal dinner for 40 is frequently a four-figure line item, and it almost never appears on the couple's own budget spreadsheet because someone else absorbed it.

that person gets a note, and the note should acknowledge the cost without being weird about it. "we know what it takes to host 40 people and we are so grateful you did it for us" is the right register.

also on this list:

- whoever let out-of-town family stay in their spare room
- whoever did airport runs
- whoever cooked or hosted the day-after brunch
- whoever lent a car, a yard, a projector, or a dog that was in the photos

### a note on the people who did labor, not money

your friend who made the playlist. your cousin who did everyone's makeup for free. your coworker who built the seating chart spreadsheet. labor is a gift and it rarely leaves a paper trail, which means it never makes a list. write those down the week after the wedding while you still remember who did what.

## 06. the coordinator

venue coordinator, day-of planner, or officiant. often the most forgotten three.

the venue coordinator is the single most skipped person in wedding thank yous, because couples think of them as "the venue" rather than a person. they are the one who noticed the cake table was in the sun and moved it before anyone else clocked it. the day-of coordinator spent eight hours making problems disappear specifically so that you would never find out about them.

your officiant, if a friend or family member, absolutely gets a note and usually a gift. if a professional, a note and a review. if clergy, a donation to the church or organization is standard on top of the fee.

## watch for these

a few things that quietly derail the whole process:

- **the "we'll remember" trap.** you will not remember. two people at the shower and two on the wedding day, assigned to write down giver and gift, is the only version of this that works.
- **cards separated from gifts.** tape the card to the box the moment it arrives. this fixes most of the problem by itself.
- **waiting for the perfect stationery.** couples delay six weeks waiting on custom cards that match the invitation suite. plain cards sent in week two beat gorgeous cards sent in month five.
- **venmo and cash app gifts with no paper trail.** screenshot and log them the day they land.
- **splitting the pile unevenly.** if one person writes 140 notes alone, the last 40 will be identical and lifeless. divide by relationship: you write yours, your partner writes theirs, you co-sign all of them.
- **assuming a verbal thank you at the wedding counts.** you were overstimulated and so were they. nobody remembers what was said in the receiving line.
- **skipping anyone you are annoyed with.** the aunt who had opinions about your dress still sent a gift. write the note. it is four sentences.

## the short version

1. gift givers, within three months, sooner for anyone who travelled.
2. vendors, a note plus a specific public review.
3. wedding party, name what they actually did.
4. guests who rsvp'd no and sent something anyway.
5. family who hosted a shower, rehearsal dinner, or brunch.
6. venue coordinator, day-of planner, and officiant, the most forgotten three.

build the list from your gift log, not your seating chart. assign a scribe at every event. write the travellers first. and if you are still at the stage of collecting quotes and figuring out who is doing what, you can [add your vendor quotes free](/get-started) and keep every cost, contact, and detail in one place so this list builds itself as you go.
