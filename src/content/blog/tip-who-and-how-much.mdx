It is 8:40 on the wedding morning. The hair and makeup team is packing brushes, the photographer just arrived, and someone (usually the maid of honor, sometimes the groom's mom) leans over and whispers, "did we get cash for tips?" Everyone looks at each other. Nobody did. So a bridesmaid ends up sprinting to an ATM in a robe, or worse, a few people who worked hard on your day walk out without the gratuity they expected.

That scene plays out constantly, and it is completely avoidable. Tipping at a wedding is not optional, it is just under-discussed. Almost none of these amounts are written into your quotes. They show up as an afterthought instead of a line item, which is exactly why they blow up the budget you thought you had locked.

Here is the fix: treat tips like any other vendor payment. Budget them alongside every deposit, build the envelope amounts into your total before you sign anything, and label the cash the week before so nobody is guessing at 8:40 a.m. Below are the seven vendors you tip and exactly how much each one expects.

## the 7 vendors you tip (and the exact amounts)

Think of this as your checklist. Each of these is a real cash outflow you should plan for, even though most of them never appear on a contract.

1. **Catering staff:** $20 to $50 per server, handed to the captain.
2. **Photographer:** $50 to $200, and tip the second shooter too.
3. **DJ:** $50 to $150, more if they go overtime.
4. **Hair and makeup:** 15 to 20% per artist, cash on the day.
5. **Officiant:** $50 to $100, more for custom ceremony writing.
6. **Videographer:** $50 to $200, the same range as your photographer.
7. **Driver:** 15 to 20% of the total, even if gratuity is already listed on the contract.

Now the details, because "how much" depends on who you are tipping and how they get paid.

## the day-of team: catering, hair, and makeup

### catering staff

Your catering team is often the largest group of people working your wedding, and they are also the ones most people forget. The standard is **$20 to $50 per server**, and the clean way to handle it is one envelope handed to the captain or banquet manager to distribute. If you have a formal, plated dinner with a big service team, the total adds up fast. Ten servers at $30 each is $300 in cash you need on hand.

One thing to check before you write that check: read your catering contract for a "service charge." A service charge is not always the same as a gratuity, and it does not always reach the staff. If your contract already includes a true gratuity that goes to the workers, you can adjust. If it is a vague "service fee," ask directly where that money goes. That single question can save you from double-tipping or, worse, assuming the staff got taken care of when they did not.

### hair and makeup artists

Beauty is the one place people underestimate the most, because the tip is a percentage, not a flat number. The standard is **15 to 20% per artist, cash on the day**. "Per artist" matters. If you booked a lead stylist plus an assistant, each one gets their own tip based on their portion of the work.

Here is a concrete example. Say your hair and makeup total is $800 for the bride plus five bridesmaids. A 20% tip is $160. Split between two artists, that is $80 each, in cash, handed to them before they leave. Nobody wants to Venmo a makeup artist while the ceremony is starting, so pull this cash out the week before.

## the creative team: photographer, videographer, DJ

These three shape how your wedding is remembered, and they tend to work the longest hours of anyone on your team.

### photographer and second shooter

Tip your photographer **$50 to $200**, and do not forget the second shooter. The second shooter is a real person capturing half the angles you will actually use, and they are frequently left off the tip list entirely. If you booked a package with two shooters, plan two envelopes.

A quick note on studios versus solo owners: if you hired a large studio and the photographer is an employee, a tip is a genuine thank-you rather than an expectation. If you hired the owner directly, a tip is not always expected but is always appreciated for exceptional work. Either way, budget for it so the choice is yours and not forced by an empty wallet.

### videographer

Same job philosophy, same range. Tip your videographer **$50 to $200, the same range as your photographer**. Video crews often stay late to capture the last dance and the exit, so if they went above and beyond, land at the top of the range.

### DJ

Your DJ or band leader gets **$50 to $150, more if they go overtime**. Overtime is the key phrase here. If the party is going strong and you ask the DJ to play an extra 45 minutes, that is both an overtime fee (check your contract) and a reason to bump the tip. Bands are different: you generally tip each musician a smaller amount, or tip the band leader to distribute, so ask how they prefer it when you book.

## the sometimes-forgotten two: officiant and driver

### officiant

Tip your officiant **$50 to $100, more for custom ceremony writing**. If your officiant wrote a personalized ceremony, met with you a few times, and worked your custom vows into the script, that is hours of unpaid prep, and the higher end (or a bit above) is fair.

The exception: some religious officiants cannot accept a personal tip, in which case a donation to their house of worship is the traditional move. Ask early so you know which envelope to prepare.

### driver

Your driver or limo/shuttle service gets **15 to 20% of the total, even if gratuity is already listed on the contract**. This is the sneakiest one. Transportation contracts almost always list a gratuity line, and people assume that covers it. Sometimes it does. But that listed gratuity does not always reach the actual driver behind the wheel, and it is customary to hand the driver something directly. Read the contract, ask where the listed gratuity goes, and plan a cash tip for the person driving you.

## red flags and things to watch for

A few traps catch couples every year. Watch for these:

- **"Service charge" disguised as a tip.** A 20 to 24% service charge on a catering invoice is often an administrative fee, not staff gratuity. Confirm in writing where it goes before you assume the servers are covered.
- **Gratuity listed on the driver contract.** As above, listed does not always mean it reaches the driver. Ask.
- **Forgetting the second shooter and assistants.** These are the people most often left off the list. If a vendor brings help, budget for the help.
- **No cash on the day.** Most of these tips are cash, handed over in the moment. An ATM run in a wedding dress is not the vibe. Withdraw and label envelopes the week before.
- **Tips treated as an afterthought.** If you only think about tips the night before, you are pulling hundreds of dollars out of a budget you already considered final. That is how "we're on budget" quietly becomes "we're $700 over."

## build the envelopes into your budget

Add it up and tipping is not a rounding error. A mid-size wedding can easily run $600 to $1,200 in tips once you count catering staff, two photographers, a videographer, a DJ, two beauty artists, an officiant, and a driver. That is real money, and it should live in your budget from the start, not appear as a surprise on the wedding morning.

The move is simple: every time you sign a vendor and log the deposit, log the expected tip right next to it. Put the cash aside as you go, or at minimum earmark the total, then withdraw and split it into labeled envelopes the week of. Hand the beauty and catering tips out day-of, and hand the rest to your planner or a trusted person to distribute so you are not thinking about it during cocktail hour.

## the actionable summary

Before you book a single vendor, run this list:

1. **Catering staff:** $20 to $50 per server, one envelope to the captain.
2. **Photographer:** $50 to $200, plus a separate tip for the second shooter.
3. **DJ:** $50 to $150, more for overtime.
4. **Hair and makeup:** 15 to 20% per artist, cash on the day.
5. **Officiant:** $50 to $100, more for custom vows (or a donation if they cannot accept cash).
6. **Videographer:** $50 to $200, same range as the photographer.
7. **Driver:** 15 to 20% of the total, even if gratuity is listed on the contract.

Budget these alongside every deposit, confirm what any listed "service charge" or "gratuity" actually covers, and pull the cash the week before. Do that, and nobody has to whisper "did we get cash?" at 8:40 on the morning of.
