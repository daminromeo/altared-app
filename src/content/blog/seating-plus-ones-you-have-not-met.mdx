say you're at the kitchen table with a printed floor plan, a stack of sticky notes and an rsvp list that's finally closed. the easy tables go fast. your college friends, your cousins, your parents' neighbors who have known each other for thirty years. then you hit the names you don't recognize at all: your coworker's boyfriend, your cousin's new partner, the person your college roommate has mentioned twice and you've never actually met. suddenly the whole chart stalls.

the plus-one situation is genuinely one of the trickiest parts of a seating chart. you don't know them, you can't predict how they'll mix, and 'guest of' on a place card is the fastest way to make someone feel like an afterthought. the good news is that seating a stranger well isn't a personality test or a guessing game. it's five decisions, made in order, before you commit anything to the final chart.

## 1. anchor them

seat every plus-one next to the guest who brought them. always.

this is the one rule that doesn't bend. it is tempting to split couples across a table to "spread the energy around," or to park a plus-one next to someone chatty because you assume they'll hit it off. don't. the person who brought them is the only social bridge they have in the room. remove that bridge and you've handed a stranger a four-hour cocktail party where they know nobody and everyone else has history.

anchoring also quietly solves a logistics problem. the plus-one has someone to follow to the bar, someone to sit with during speeches, someone who can introduce them. your guest does the hosting work you can't do from the head table.

### what "beside" actually means

next to, not across from. round tables are loud, and conversation across a centerpiece is harder than people expect. if you're using long banquet tables, same rule, adjacent seats.

## 2. mix tables, don't isolate

group unknowns with other plus-ones, shared footing breaks ice faster.

the instinct is to scatter the people you don't know into established friend groups so they'll be "absorbed." in practice that often backfires. a table of eight who went to high school together will spend the night on inside jokes, and the one person outside the group gets a very polite, very long evening.

a table where two or three people are in the same position (new to the group, here with someone, learning names) sets a different tone. nobody's the outsider because everybody is slightly outside. the conversation starts at "so how do you know the couple," which is exactly the right opening question when it's genuinely being asked both ways.

a few ways to build that table:

1. pair plus-ones by anchor, so each couple stays together and the table has two or three mini-units rather than one clique plus a guest.
2. look for a shared handle you can mention in passing, same city, same job field, both new to the group. you don't need to force it, just knowing it helps.
3. seat one warm, generous host-type at the table, someone who naturally asks questions and pulls people in.
4. avoid putting a single plus-one at a table where everyone else has known each other for a decade.

you're not matchmaking. you're lowering the floor on how awkward the first fifteen minutes can get.

## 3. use their real name on the card

write their first name, not 'guest of'. get it from your guest early.

this one costs you nothing and it's the detail people remember. "guest of jordan" on a place card tells someone they were processed, not invited. their name, spelled right, tells them the opposite.

the hard part isn't the printing, it's the collecting. names come in late, change after a breakup, or never come at all because your guest didn't realize you needed one. ask on the rsvp itself if you can, with a line that requires the plus-one's full name rather than a checkbox. if your rsvps are already out, a short text works: "so excited you're coming. what's your plus-one's name and spelling? putting place cards together."

set a hard internal deadline for name collection, earlier than your stationery deadline, so you have a buffer for chasing. a dated to-do for each follow-up makes this much less painful than a spreadsheet plus a text thread. you can [start free](/get-started) and keep every deadline, vendor and payment in one place.

## 4. ask first

text your guest: "is there anyone they shouldn't sit near?"

you cannot know the history of someone you've never met. your guest can. one message, sent before the chart is final, surfaces things no amount of careful planning would catch: an ex at the wedding, a former boss, a family member they're not speaking to, a friend group that ended badly, a dietary or accessibility need they haven't mentioned.

keep the ask light and low-pressure. you're not inviting a seating negotiation, you're asking for a heads-up. and ask early, while the chart is still sticky notes. once the escort cards are printed, a revelation about who shouldn't sit where becomes a much more expensive problem.

### what to do with the answer

if there's genuine tension, you don't need to explain or mediate. put distance between the tables, keep sightlines in mind (across the dance floor is better than back-to-back), and move on. nobody at the reception will notice the choreography, which is the entire point.

## 5. leave buffer space

leave one open seat per table of 8. late rsvps and surprises fill it.

seating charts are not final until the day they're final, and sometimes not even then. someone's plus-one becomes a yes at the last minute. a guest brings a partner you didn't know about. a table needs to absorb one person who got separated from their group. an empty chair is the cheapest insurance you can buy against all three.

it also looks better. a table packed to the exact seat count feels tight, especially once chargers, glassware and favors are on it. a little breathing room reads as intentional.

## watch for these

- **"guest of" on any card.** if you're printing cards and one still says this, you have time to fix it. you don't have time after.
- **the single stranger at a long-established table.** scan your chart for any table where exactly one person doesn't know anyone. that's the seat that goes wrong.
- **names collected secondhand.** "i think it's sara with no h" is how a misspelled place card happens. confirm with the guest who brought them.
- **a chart with zero slack.** every seat assigned, no buffer, means one late yes forces you to rebuild three tables.
- **asking about tension too late.** if you're asking the week of, you're not solving a problem, you're learning about one.
- **assuming a breakup didn't happen.** if a plus-one rsvp'd months ago, confirm before you print.

## the short version

1. anchor them beside the person who brought them, always.
2. group unknowns with other plus-ones so everyone's on shared footing.
3. use their real first name on the card, and ask your guest early to get it.
4. text your guest to flag any tension you should know about before the chart is final.
5. leave one open seat per table of 8 for late rsvps and surprises.
