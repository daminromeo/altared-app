A couple books a garden ceremony at a public park for a saturday in june. the site fee is listed at $150, which feels like a steal next to the venues quoting five figures. they pay it, they book a band, they tell 90 people to save the date.

six weeks out, the parks department emails asking for the special event permit application, proof of liability insurance, and the amplified sound permit (separate form, separate fee). the sound permit comes with a 9pm cutoff. the band was booked until 11.

nothing here was a scam. every one of those requirements was published on a municipal website the whole time. the couple just did not know to look, because the marriage licence gets all the attention, but it is only one of four documents most weddings actually need before the day is legal and running.

here is what each one costs, when to file it, and what happens if you skip it.

## 01. the marriage licence: $35 to $115

this is the one everybody knows about, and it is still the one people cut too close.

a marriage licence typically runs **$35-$115 depending on your county**. same state, two counties over, and the price can double. some counties knock off a chunk of the fee if you complete a premarital counseling course, which is worth a five-minute search before you pay.

the cost is not the problem. the timing is. **apply at least 30 days before** your date, and understand the two clocks that govern your licence:

1. **the waiting period.** some jurisdictions make you wait 24 to 72 hours between getting the licence and using it. destination couples fly in thursday for a saturday wedding and find out the hard way.
2. **the expiration window.** licences expire, often in 30 to 90 days. apply too early and it lapses before your date.
3. **the return deadline.** after the ceremony, your officiant has to file the signed licence back with the county, usually within a set number of days. if it does not get returned, you are not legally married, even though you had the party.
4. **the ID requirements.** certified birth certificate, government photo ID, sometimes a divorce decree or death certificate if you were previously married. ordering a certified copy of anything takes weeks.

### the part nobody mentions: name changes

the licence itself is cheap. the certified copies afterward are what you actually need for the DMV, social security, your bank, and your passport, and they usually cost extra per copy. order three or four at once. you will use them.

## 02. the venue permit: $50 to $500

if you are getting married on public land, you need a permit. parks, beaches, historic sites, city plazas, trailheads, botanical gardens run by a municipality. **$50-$500 for parks and public land** is the normal range, and the top of it is real. a venue or park permit can run up to $500 depending on whether you are on public land.

**private venues usually handle their own.** that is the good news. if you are booking a hotel ballroom, a barn, a restaurant buyout, or a dedicated event space, permitting is baked into their operation and their price. you do not file anything.

but "usually" is doing work in that sentence. ask directly, in writing: *are there any municipal permits, event licences, or approvals required for my event that are not included in my contract?* get the answer in an email. a private estate rented out for weddings a few weekends a year is often a different legal animal than a purpose-built venue, and some of those properties operate on a conditional use permit with a hard cap on guest count or end time.

### what the permit fee does not cover

the permit is the entry ticket. the extras that ride along with it:

- **liability insurance.** most public-land permits require a certificate naming the city or parks department as additional insured. a one-day event policy is its own line item.
- **refundable deposits.** cleaning, damage, or trash deposits are common, and refundable in theory, slower in practice.
- **attendant or staff fees.** some sites require a paid ranger, custodian, or security presence for events over a certain size.
- **parking and vehicle access.** loading a catering van onto a park path is often a separate approval.
- **rehearsal time.** your permit may cover the ceremony hour only. the rehearsal the day before can need its own booking.

a $150 site fee that turns into $150 permit plus $200 insurance plus $300 refundable deposit plus a $75 attendant is not a $150 venue. run those numbers before you fall in love with the location. if you are comparing outdoor sites against traditional venues, put the all-in figure in your [budgeting](/blog/category/budgeting) spreadsheet, not the headline number.

## 03. the one-day liquor licence: $25 to $200

**a one-day event licence runs $25-$200**, and it is **required if your venue is not licensed**. this is the permit that surprises the most couples, because "we're just bringing our own wine" feels like a private decision, not a regulated one.

it is regulated. the trigger is usually one of three things: alcohol is being served to the public or a large gathering, alcohol is being sold (including indirectly, via a ticket or a cash bar), or alcohol is being served on public property. a dry venue that lets you bring your own bar is exactly the scenario a one-day licence exists for.

### who actually files it

three common setups:

1. **your caterer or bar service holds a licence and files for the event.** easiest path. confirm it is in your contract and ask for a copy of the certificate.
2. **you file personally as the host.** doable, and the cheapest at $25-$200, but you may need to attend a short server training or submit a floor plan showing where alcohol is served.
3. **a nonprofit or fraternal hall files on your behalf.** common with legion halls and community centers, and usually cheap, but their licence may carry restrictions on what you can serve.

whoever files, the licence names a responsible party. if that is you, you are the one with liability exposure if a guest overserves and drives. host liquor liability coverage is worth pricing out even when the permit does not require it.

### the "friend who bartends" problem

a friend pouring drinks feels like a $0 bar. it is not. many one-day licences require a certified server, and your venue's own insurance may require a licensed, insured bar service regardless of what the city says. if your friend is pouring and something goes wrong, the liability lands on you and on the person who signed the permit. price a professional bartender against the risk before you decide. more on that in our [vendor tips](/blog/category/vendor-tips) posts.

## 04. the sound permit: $0 to $150

this is the one people find out about at 8:47pm.

a sound permit, often overlooked, can sit anywhere from **free to $150**, but the noise curfew it governs is local law, so missing it means your band stops early whether you are ready or not. **check before you book the band.**

the fee is trivial. the curfew is everything. a 10pm amplified sound cutoff changes your entire timeline: when dinner is served, when speeches happen, when you do the first dance, whether you get a real last hour of dancing or a polite acoustic wind-down. and the enforcement is not your venue being strict. it is a municipal code, sometimes with a fine attached, sometimes with a complaint-triggered shutdown that arrives mid-song.

ask these four questions before you sign anything:

1. is amplified sound permitted at this location, and does it require a separate permit?
2. what time is the amplified sound cutoff, and does it differ for outdoor versus indoor?
3. is there a decibel limit, and has this venue ever been cited?
4. can the curfew be extended, and what does the extension cost?

if the answer to number four is yes, that extension fee belongs in your budget right now, not in a panicked phone call in month nine.

## red flags to watch for

- **a venue that says "don't worry about permits" and will not put it in writing.** vague verbal reassurance is how permit costs become your problem in month eleven.
- **a site fee that seems dramatically below market for the location.** often that is because the permit, insurance, and deposit are unbundled and not on the quote.
- **a caterer who is cagey about their liquor licence number.** it is a public record. they should hand it over without hesitation.
- **"we've done a hundred weddings here, nobody's ever needed that."** maybe true, maybe the code changed, maybe the complaints started. verify with the municipality yourself.
- **no written end time in the contract.** if the curfew is not in the contract, nobody is accountable for it but you.
- **a permit application window shorter than your booking timeline.** some cities only accept applications 60 to 90 days out, which means you can book a date you cannot actually permit.

## your checklist

run this before you sign with any venue or bar vendor:

1. **marriage licence:** confirm your county's fee ($35-$115), waiting period, expiration window, and ID requirements. apply at least 30 days before. order extra certified copies.
2. **venue permit:** ask in writing whether any municipal permit is required. if you are on public land, budget $50-$500 plus insurance, deposits, and attendant fees.
3. **liquor licence:** if the venue is not licensed, someone needs a one-day event licence at $25-$200. confirm in the contract who files it and who is named as responsible.
4. **sound permit:** $0-$150, but get the curfew time in writing before you book a band or DJ, and price the extension if one exists.
5. **put every fee in the same place as your vendor quotes.** permits are not a separate category in your life. they are part of what the venue actually costs.

none of this is scary once you know where to look. it is four phone calls and a folder. the couples who get burned are not the ones who researched and made a tradeoff, they are the ones who assumed someone else was handling it.
