You are three quotes deep. The caterer's proposal has a food and beverage minimum that feels aggressive, a 25% service charge tacked onto the bottom, and a delivery line you didn't ask for. Your instinct is to either sign it or ghost the vendor entirely. There is a third option, and it is the one experienced couples use: you reply with a calm, specific ask.

Here is the thing nobody tells you when you start collecting quotes. Most vendors build negotiation room into their initial numbers. The f&b minimum, the second shooter add-on, the floral markup, the service charge percentage, the dj package length, the cake delivery fee, the planner rate structure. All of it has give, if you know what to ask.

This isn't about being difficult or grinding a small business into the ground. It's about knowing which seven numbers are almost always flexible, and having the exact words ready when you review the contract. Vendors respect couples who come prepared. They negotiate every week. You do this once.

Below are the seven line items worth a conversation, the dollar range each one tends to move, and the script you can copy into an email. Save this for the week you start signing.

## The seven numbers with the most give

Before the scripts, a quick map. These are the line items where the gap between the sticker price and what you actually pay is widest.

1. **Venue food and beverage minimum**
2. **Second shooter (photography)**
3. **Floral markup**
4. **Service charge percentage**
5. **DJ package length**
6. **Cake delivery and setup fee**
7. **Planner rate structure**

Notice these are not "ask for a discount because you love them." Each one is a structural choice inside the quote. That framing matters. You are not begging. You are picking the version of the package that fits your day.

### 01. Venue food and beverage minimum

The f&b minimum is the dollar amount you commit to spending on food and drink, and it is the single biggest lever most couples never touch. It is priced around demand. Saturdays in peak season carry the highest minimums. Off-peak fridays or sundays are a different story.

F&b minimums drop 10 to 20% for off-peak fridays or sundays. On a $20,000 minimum, that is $2,000 to $4,000 back in your budget for choosing a day the venue is happy to fill.

**The script:** "We love the space. Our date is a little flexible. What does the food and beverage minimum look like for a friday or sunday in [month]? We're comparing a couple of dates and want to make the numbers work."

### 02. Second shooter

A lot of photography packages include a second shooter by default, and a lot of weddings genuinely don't need one. If your guest count is modest, your venue is a single location, and you are not doing a first look plus separate getting-ready coverage across town, one experienced photographer is often plenty.

Ask to remove it and save $400 to $900, or swap it for an extra hour of the lead photographer's time. That swap is frequently the better move. An extra hour of your primary shooter at the end of the night gets you the open-dance-floor and sparkler-exit shots people actually frame.

**The script:** "Can you break out the second shooter as a separate line? We're leaning toward removing it, or possibly swapping it for an extra hour with you. What would each option do to the total?"

### 03. Floral markup

Florists mark up stems 2 to 3x. That markup pays for their sourcing, cold storage, labor, and design eye, and it is completely fair. What is negotiable is the structure. If you are attached to a specific expensive bloom, the markup on it stacks fast.

Ask for a design-only fee instead, where the florist charges for their expertise and labor and you're paying closer to true cost on some of the product. Alternatively, tell them your total floral budget as a hard number and let them design to it. Good florists would rather hit your number with smart substitutions than lose the booking.

**The script:** "Here is our total floral budget: $[X]. Can you design to that number and swap in seasonal stems wherever it stretches further? We care more about the overall look than any specific flower."

### 04. Service charge

This is the line that surprises people most, so read it carefully. The service charge is a percentage added on top of your food and beverage total. Standard is 20 to 25%. On a $20,000 f&b spend, a 25% service charge is $5,000. Dropping it to 18% is $3,600. That is $1,400 from one sentence.

Ask to cap it at 18%, or ask whether it can be credited toward food and beverage instead. Not every venue will move, and some genuinely can't because the charge funds staff wages. But a surprising number have room, especially if you ask before you sign rather than after.

**The script:** "Can we cap the service charge at 18%, or apply a portion of it as a credit toward our food and beverage minimum? Want to make sure I understand exactly what this line covers before we sign."

### 05. DJ hours

5-hour packages average $1,800 or more. Map your actual timeline before you buy hours you won't use. If your reception runs from a 6pm entrance to a 10pm exit, 4 hours is usually enough. That is the ceremony-to-last-song window most weddings actually fill.

**The script:** "Our reception runs [start] to [end]. That's closer to 4 hours of active coverage. Do you have a 4-hour rate, or can we adjust the package length down?"

Just ask. Worst case, they explain why the 5-hour package makes sense for your timeline and you keep it, now with real reasoning instead of a default.

### 06. Cake delivery

Delivery plus setup runs $150 to $350, and it hides at the bottom of the invoice where nobody reads. Sometimes it is genuinely necessary, a tiered cake in summer heat is not a car-trunk situation. But for simpler cakes or a nearby bakery, there is often another path.

Ask if venue pickup waives the fee, or whether a member of your crew (a designated aunt, a planner's assistant) can pick it up. On a $600 cake, cutting a $300 delivery-and-setup line is a real 50% savings on that vendor's total.

**The script:** "Is the delivery and setup fee required, or can we arrange pickup to waive it? If delivery is safest for the design, no problem, just want to understand the option."

### 07. Planner rate

Full-service planning runs 10 to 15% of your total budget. On a $50,000 wedding, that is $5,000 to $7,500. Full-service is worth every dollar if you want someone building the whole thing with you from month one. Many couples don't need that.

Month-of coordination saves $3,000 or more. You do the planning and booking yourself (which the seven items above help you do), then hand off a finished plan to a coordinator who runs the day so you're not chasing the caterer during cocktail hour.

**The script:** "We're comfortable handling most of the planning ourselves. Do you offer month-of coordination, and what's the rate difference from full-service?"

## Red flags to watch for

Negotiating well means knowing when a "no" is fair and when a quote is a warning sign. Watch for these:

- **Vendors who won't itemize.** If you ask to see a line broken out and get pushback or a vague "it's all bundled," that is worth a second look. You can't negotiate what you can't see.
- **Service charges described as "gratuity" that are not gratuity.** These are different things. Ask directly whether staff tips are included or expected on top.
- **Pressure to sign today for a "disappearing" rate.** Real vendors give you time to read a contract. Manufactured urgency is a tactic.
- **Add-ons that appear only in the final contract**, not the initial quote. Compare the two documents line by line before signing.
- **Cancellation and payment terms buried at the end.** Negotiate the deposit and refund schedule too, not just the headline price.

A calm vendor will explain their pricing without getting defensive. That reaction tells you as much as the number itself.

## How to actually run these conversations

Send your asks together, not one nagging email at a time. Pick the two or three line items that matter most for your specific quote and raise them in a single friendly message. Lead with what you love about the vendor, be specific about the number, and give them an easy way to say yes.

You do not have to win all seven. Landing three of these on a mid-size wedding can easily add up to several thousand dollars, real money that goes toward the honeymoon instead of a delivery fee.

## The checklist to save

1. Ask about off-peak friday or sunday f&b minimums (10 to 20% lower).
2. Break out the second shooter, remove or swap it ($400 to $900).
3. Give your florist a total budget and ask for a design-only fee.
4. Request an 18% service charge cap or a food credit.
5. Match dj hours to your real timeline (4 hours is often enough).
6. Ask whether pickup waives the $150 to $350 cake delivery fee.
7. Consider month-of coordination over full-service (saves $3,000+).
