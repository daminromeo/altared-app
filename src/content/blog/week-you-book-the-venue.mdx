say you sign the venue contract on a thursday afternoon. you cried a little in the parking lot. by friday your aunt has heard the date from someone who is not you, and by sunday you have told yourself you will "read the contract properly next weekend." that next weekend never arrives, and three months later you are googling what an overtime rate is at 11pm.

the week you book is the most important week in your planning timeline, and most of it has nothing to do with decor. it is five unglamorous tasks, done in a specific order, that each save you a conversation you do not want to have later.

here is the order that actually matters.

## 01. lock the date with immediate family first

send a save-the-date text or email to immediate family before anyone else hears.

this sounds like etiquette. it is actually logistics. a wedding date spreads faster than you can control it, and the people most likely to be hurt by hearing it secondhand are the exact people whose calendars you need to clear. parents, siblings, grandparents, anyone who is traveling or paying or both.

keep it short. the date, the city, and a line that says formal invitations come later. you are not announcing. you are preventing a version of the story where your brother finds out from instagram.

### do this the same day you sign

the gap between "we booked it" and "everyone knows" is where the awkwardness lives. close it fast. one group text beats five apology calls.

one more quiet benefit: the moment immediate family knows the date, you start getting the conflicts early. a cousin's graduation, a surgery, a work trip that cannot move. you cannot fix all of those, but knowing about them in week one is very different from learning about them when the invitations go out.

## 02. read the full contract the same day, not next week

you have never had more motivation to read this document than the day you sign it. you will never again be this interested in section 7.

read the whole thing, but find these three things specifically:

1. **the cancellation window.** what is the last date you can cancel, what happens to your deposit, and what happens if the venue cancels on you. these are three separate answers and the contract may bury them in three separate places.
2. **the overtime rate.** what it costs if the reception runs long, how it is charged (by the hour, by the fraction of an hour), and who has the authority to approve it on the night. you want to know this before someone on your wedding day is making that call for you.
3. **who owns the vendor list.** is it a required list, a preferred list, or a suggestion? required lists mean your "we will shop around for a caterer" plan may already be decided. preferred lists sometimes carry fees for outside vendors. ask for that in writing.

while you are in there, note the final payment date, the guaranteed guest count deadline, the load-in and load-out times, and whether the venue requires specific insurance from your vendors. put every one of those dates in your calendar before you close the document.

### if something is unclear, ask this week

email the venue coordinator with your questions in one message, numbered, and ask for answers in writing. a friendly email the week you sign reads as organized. the same email in month six reads as a problem. and you now have a paper trail for anything that was explained to you verbally during the tour.

for more on the clauses worth arguing over, our [contracts coverage](/blog/category/contracts) goes deeper.

## 03. name one point person, and tell them in writing

name one person the venue can call if you are unreachable, and tell them in writing.

on the wedding day you will be in a dress or a suit, your phone will be in someone's bag, and a delivery driver will be at a locked gate. the venue needs a human who can answer and decide. without one, the decision gets made by whoever happens to be standing near the door.

pick someone who is calm, not someone who is closest to you emotionally. a sibling who panics is worse than a cousin who does not. then:

1. ask them first, out loud, so they can say no.
2. email the venue their full name, phone number, and relationship to you.
3. send your point person a copy of that email so everyone has the same information.

in writing matters. a verbal "my brother will handle it" is not something a venue coordinator can act on at 4pm on a saturday.

this one person also becomes your buffer for the months before the wedding. when a vendor cannot reach you, they reach them.

## 04. call hotels near the venue about room blocks

room blocks release at 30-60 days out. call hotels this week, before you forget.

room blocks fill and release faster than you think, and the hotels closest to your venue are the first to go, especially in a popular month or a small town. the week you book is the week to call, because you now have the one piece of information every hotel will ask for: the date.

when you call, ask:

1. what the room block minimum is and whether you are financially responsible for unbooked rooms (attrition).
2. when unbooked rooms release back to the hotel.
3. whether there is a group rate and what the booking link or code looks like.
4. whether they offer a shuttle, or will work with a shuttle company you hire.

get two or three blocks at different price points if you can, so guests have options. then note the release dates somewhere you will actually look, because a room block that quietly releases is a room block your guests cannot book.

### watch for

- **attrition clauses** that make you pay for rooms nobody books. read that line twice.
- **"we will hold it informally"** with nothing in writing. if it is not an agreement, it is not a block.
- **blocks that release earlier than your invitations go out.** if your save-the-dates and invitations land after the release date, the block is decorative. line up the dates before you sign anything.
- **venue preferred-hotel deals** that sound like a discount but come with a minimum you cannot hit.

## 05. log every vendor quote you already have

log every vendor quote you have now, while the venue date is still fresh context for every decision ahead.

by the time you booked the venue, you probably collected a handful of quotes already: a photographer who emailed a pdf, a florist who texted a number, a caterer who quoted you verbally on a call. right now you remember which number included tax, which one was for a different date, and which one expires. in six weeks you will not.

write down, for each quote: the vendor, the service, the number you were given, what is included, what is excluded, whether travel and service charges are in it, how long the quote is valid, and the date you got it. the venue date is the context that makes all of those comparable, because quotes change with the date, the season, and the day of the week.

this is also where you catch the quotes that were never apples to apples. our [budgeting posts](/blog/category/budgeting) cover how to compare an all-in number against a line-item one.

you can [add your quotes free](/get-started) and track every vendor from day one, so the comparison is already built when you start booking.

## the week-one checklist

none of this is glamorous. all of it saves you a conversation you do not want to have later.

1. text or email immediate family the date before anyone else hears it.
2. read the full contract the same day. find the cancellation window, the overtime rate, and who owns the vendor list.
3. name one point person, and tell the venue in writing.
4. call hotels near the venue about room blocks, and note when they release.
5. log every vendor quote you already have, while the venue date is fresh.

save this for the week you sign, and tag the friend who just got engaged.
