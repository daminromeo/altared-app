You've finalized your guest count. You email the caterer the number, feel a little rush of accomplishment, and move on to the seating chart. Then, four days before the wedding, you reread your photographer's contract while looking for the arrival time and you spot a line you skimmed past six months ago: "client agrees to provide one hot meal per crew member for events exceeding five hours."

Suddenly your "final" headcount is off by six people. The caterer needs an answer today. And you're doing math you should have done in the spring.

Vendor meals are one of those costs that sneaks up on every couple, because it's not in the main quote. It's buried in a clause halfway through the contract. The number is never huge, but it almost always catches couples off guard the week of the wedding. So let's do the math early, together, and walk through exactly who you're feeding and why.

## why vendor meals exist (and why they're contractual)

This isn't a gratuity or a nice gesture. For a lot of your vendors, a meal is written into the agreement you signed.

The logic is simple. Your photographer and videographer are likely on site for 8+ hours. A band might be 4 to 6 people. Your coordinator brought an assistant. Your hair and makeup team started at 6 a.m. Nobody performs well on an empty stomach after a ten-hour day, and vendors learned long ago to put a meal requirement in writing so they're not left buying gas station snacks at your reception.

Most contracts specify a *hot* meal, not a boxed sandwich, and many name the hour it kicks in (often hour five). Some let you serve a discounted "vendor meal" from your caterer, which is usually cheaper than a full plated guest dinner. Either way, the rule of thumb is straightforward: budget $25 to $35 per person and multiply it out before you give the caterer a final headcount.

Here's the part that trips people up. These people don't sit at guest tables and they're often invisible on your seating chart, so they're easy to forget when you're counting heads. But the caterer still has to plate for them.

## the 5 vendors you have to feed

Not every wedding has all five, and not every contract requires a meal. But these are the roles where a meal clause shows up most often, in the order they tend to add up.

### 1. photographer

Your photographer is usually the first vendor to arrive and one of the last to leave. Getting-ready shots through the last dance can easily stretch past eight hours, and most contracts name a hot meal by hour five. If your photographer brings a second shooter, that's two meals, not one. Check whether your package includes an assistant, because that's the detail couples miss most.

### 2. videographer

Same hours as the photographer, same all-day presence, and often the same clause. If you hired photo and video as separate vendors, you likely have two crews, each with their own meal requirement. Budget $25 to $35 per crew member here too. A photo and video team of four people is $100 to $140 before you've fed anyone else.

### 3. band or DJ

This is where the number gets real. A DJ might be one person. A band might be 4 to 6 people, and that's $150 to $210 in vendor meals alone. Bands sometimes travel with a sound tech too, so read the rider carefully. When couples tell me their vendor meal total "came out of nowhere," it's almost always the band that did it.

### 4. day-of planner (and the +1)

Your coordinator is running your entire timeline, which means they're on site from the early setup through breakdown. Many planners bring an assistant, especially for larger weddings, and that assistant needs a meal too. Check your contract for the +1 clause. If it says "coordinator and one assistant," that's two plates, and it's the exact kind of line that reads like fine print until it shows up on your final catering invoice.

### 5. hair and makeup

The 6 a.m. call times are brutal, and your glam team is often working straight through the last touch-up before the ceremony. Some artists leave before the reception, so they may only need a light meal or none at all. But if your contract keeps them on site through the last look, a meal on-site keeps them fed and keeps them there. Ask when they're scheduled to leave, because that answer tells you whether you're feeding them dinner or just coffee and a snack.

## do the actual math

Let's put numbers to it so this stops being abstract. Say you have a fairly standard vendor lineup:

1. Photographer plus a second shooter: 2 meals
2. Videographer plus an assistant: 2 meals
3. A five-piece band: 5 meals
4. Day-of coordinator plus assistant: 2 meals
5. Hair and makeup team staying through the ceremony: 2 meals

That's 13 vendor meals. At the low end of $25 each, you're looking at $325. At $35 each, you're at $455. Somewhere in that range is a real line item you didn't have on your budget spreadsheet a paragraph ago.

Now here's the trick most couples miss: many caterers charge a reduced rate for vendor meals compared to guest plates. If your guest dinner runs $85 a head and the vendor meal is $30, you're saving real money by asking for the vendor rate instead of just adding those 13 people to your guest count. Always ask your caterer, "do you offer a vendor meal rate?" before you finalize anything.

## red flags and things to watch for

The meals themselves are predictable. It's the vague language that costs you. Watch for these:

- **"One meal per crew member" with no crew size named.** If the contract doesn't say how many people are coming, ask in writing and get the number in your email thread. A "two-person team" can quietly become three on the wedding day.
- **The +1 clause hidden under the planner or band section.** Assistants and sound techs are the people who don't show up on your headcount but do show up hungry.
- **"Hot meal" requirements when you were planning a food truck or grazing stations.** Some contracts specifically require a seated hot meal, which can conflict with a casual reception format. Read before you book the food truck.
- **Meal timing.** A few contracts specify vendors eat *before* guests are served, so photographers aren't shooting mid-bite during your first course. Coordinate this with your caterer or you'll get a confused kitchen the night of.
- **Overtime meals.** If your event runs long, some vendors are entitled to a second meal or snack after a certain hour. Rare, but it's in there sometimes.

## how to handle it without stress

The fix is boring and effective: read every contract with a highlighter and a running tally.

1. Pull up every signed vendor agreement and search for the words "meal," "food," and "crew."
2. Write down how many people each vendor is bringing. If it's not stated, email and ask.
3. Add up your total vendor meal count and multiply by $25 to $35.
4. Ask each relevant vendor when they arrive and leave, so you know who needs dinner versus a snack.
5. Ask your caterer about a discounted vendor meal rate, then add the final number to your catering headcount, clearly labeled so nobody double-counts.

Do this once, early, and vendor meals become a solved problem instead of a wedding-week scramble. If you're still building out your budget from scratch, our [budgeting resources](/blog/category/budgeting) can help you slot this line in where it belongs.
