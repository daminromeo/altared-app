A couple sits down with a floral quote they love. It says $3,200. It feels reasonable. They sign, they relax, they move on to the cake tasting. Three months later the final floral invoice lands at $4,600, and nobody did anything wrong. The install fee, the extra bud vases the venue coordinator suggested, the arch that grew from "greenery" to "greenery plus blooms," the breakdown at the end of the night. None of it was hidden, exactly. It just wasn't in the first number they saw.

That gap between the quote and the final invoice is where most wedding budgets quietly blow up. Almost every couple hits at least three of these without seeing them coming, because they're not in the initial quote. They're in the contract, the addendum, or the final invoice. Here are the seven places it happens most, with real dollar ranges so you can build the buffer in now instead of scrambling later.

## 1. Florals that balloon before the install

Florals eat 8 to 10% of total budgets, and that's before add-ons. The number you get quoted is almost never the number you pay, because floral pricing has a habit of growing between the consultation and the wedding day.

The base quote covers the arrangements. What it often leaves out:

1. Delivery and setup, which can be a flat fee or a percentage
2. On-site install for anything structural, like an arch or hanging installation
3. Breakdown and removal at the end of the night
4. Rental items you're "borrowing," like stands, vases, and candles
5. The upsells that appear once you're excited, like moving from greenery to full blooms

None of those are scams. They're standard. But if you only budgeted the $3,200 base and the install and rentals add another $1,000, that's real money you didn't plan for. Ask for an itemized quote that separates flowers, labor, and rentals before you sign anything.

## 2. Open bar, then a service charge on top

Open bar runs $85 to $150 per head, plus the service charge on top. That "plus" is the part that gets people.

Say you have 120 guests at $100 per head. That's $12,000 for the bar alone. Now add a 20% service charge, and you're at $14,400. Add sales tax on top of that, and the "$12,000 bar" is closer to $15,500. The per-head number felt manageable. The stacked charges are what move it.

### watch for

The service charge is not the same as a tip, even though it sounds like one. On many catering contracts, the service charge goes to the house, not the staff, which means you may still be expected to tip on top of it. Read the line that defines where the service charge goes. If it doesn't say the staff receives it, assume you're tipping separately, and budget for that. ## 3. Stationery, and the postage nobody budgets

Postage alone runs $400 to $700 for 100 guests. Not the invitations. The postage.

This is the one couples forget every single time, because it feels like an afterthought. But save-the-dates, invitations, RSVP cards, and thank-you notes each need stamps, and heavy or square invitations get charged extra by the post office. Multiply that across 100 households and you're at several hundred dollars in stamps for pieces of paper you already paid a designer to make.

Add in the pieces that don't show up in the design quote:

1. Envelope liners and calligraphy
2. Postage for the return RSVP envelope (yes, you pay that too)
3. Extra printed invitations for the inevitable address mistakes
4. Day-of stationery like menus, programs, and place cards

Budget stationery as a full category, not just "the invites."

## 4. Vendor tips across six or eight people

Tipping at 15 to 20% across 6 to 8 vendors adds $800 to $1,500 unplanned. This one hides in plain sight because tips don't appear on any quote at all. They're just expected, in cash, at the end of the night.

Count how many people you're realistically tipping: the photographer, the videographer, the DJ or band, the catering staff, the bartenders, the hair and makeup team, the officiant, the delivery drivers. That's easily six to eight envelopes. Even modest tips across that many people land in the four-figure range.

### watch for

Some contracts already fold gratuity into the service charge (see the bar section above), and some don't. Before you hand out cash, check which vendors have already been tipped through their contract so you're not double-paying. Then build the rest into your budget as a line item, not a surprise you cover with whatever's left in your checking account.

## 5. Alterations that hit after the deposit is gone

Gown alterations average $500 to $1,200, and they hit post-deposit. This is the sequence that catches people: you fall in love with a dress, you pay the deposit, the gown budget in your head is now "handled." Then months later the alterations bill shows up, and it's hundreds of dollars you'd mentally already spent.

Alterations aren't optional. Almost no gown fits off the rack. Hemming, taking in the bodice, adding a bustle, and any custom work all add up, and couture fabrics or heavy beading cost more to alter. A $1,800 dress can easily become a $2,600 dress by the time it fits.

Ask the boutique for an alterations estimate before you buy, not after. A good shop will give you a realistic range for your specific dress, so the $500 to $1,200 doesn't arrive as a shock once the deposit is already gone.

## 6. A second shooter added mid-booking

A second shooter runs $400 to $800 added mid-booking, after you've already said yes. Here's how it happens: you book the photographer you love based on their headline package. Then, as the date gets closer, the conversation turns to coverage. Getting-ready shots in two locations, the ceremony from multiple angles, the reception while the couple is also being photographed elsewhere. The photographer suggests a second shooter, and it's a genuinely good idea, but it's an add-on that wasn't in the package you compared against everyone else's.

The problem isn't the second shooter. It's that you priced photographers against each other on the base package, then added $400 to $800 to one of them after the decision was made. If you know you want two shooters, ask about that up front so you're comparing the real total. ## 7. Venue extras that aren't in the venue quote

Required vendors, cleaning fees, and corkage run $1,000+ extra, and none of it is in the venue quote. The venue rental number is the beginning of the venue conversation, not the end of it.

The three that hit hardest:

1. **Preferred or required vendor lists.** Some venues require you to use their caterer, their bar service, or a name from their approved list. That can remove your ability to shop for a cheaper option, which quietly raises your total.
2. **Cleaning and breakdown fees.** Charged separately from the rental, often a flat few hundred dollars, sometimes taken from your security deposit after the fact.
3. **Corkage.** If you bring your own wine or champagne to save money, many venues bill corkage per bottle, which erases the savings you were counting on.

### watch for

All of this lives in the venue's contract and addendums, not the headline quote. Before you sign, ask directly: are there required vendors, is there a cleaning fee, and how is corkage billed? Get the answers in writing. A venue that's straightforward about its extras is telling you something good about how the rest of the process will go. ## the pattern behind all seven

Notice what connects these. None of them are surprises once you know to look. The floral install, the stacked service charge, the postage, the tips, the alterations, the second shooter, the venue fine print. Every one of them lives somewhere other than the first quote you see. That's the whole trick. The initial number is designed to be comparable and clean. The real cost accumulates in the addendum and the final invoice.

The couples who stay on budget aren't lucky. They just built buffer in early, because they knew where the leaks tend to be before they started signing.

## your quick checklist

Save this and run through it before you book anything:

1. **Florals:** get an itemized quote that separates flowers, labor, and rentals.
2. **Bar:** calculate per head, then add the service charge and tax before you decide.
3. **Stationery:** budget postage as its own line, $400 to $700 for 100 guests.
4. **Tips:** count your vendors, plan $800 to $1,500, and check which are already covered.
5. **Alterations:** ask for a $500 to $1,200 range before you pay the gown deposit.
6. **Photography:** confirm second shooter pricing up front, not mid-booking.
7. **Venue:** ask about required vendors, cleaning fees, and corkage in writing.
