You get the catering proposal back and it looks clean. A per-head number, a bar package, maybe a line for rentals. You do the math, it fits your budget, and you feel like you can finally exhale on the biggest single expense of the whole wedding.

Then the final invoice lands and it is four figures higher than the pretty proposal page. Nobody lied to you. The extra money was always going to be there. It just lived in line items that do not make it onto the quote you say yes to.

Here is the thing worth saying out loud: this is not about caterers being shady. Catering invoices simply carry a lot of moving parts, and most of them only surface after you have already committed. The fix is not distrust. The fix is knowing exactly what to look for so you can negotiate before you sign, not argue after.

These are the six upcharges that quietly pad your final bill.

## 1. The service charge (and no, it is not the gratuity)

This is the big one, and the one people misread most often. The service charge is typically **18-22% on top of your food and bar total**, and a lot of couples assume it covers the staff tip. It usually does not.

The service charge is the caterer's operational fee: coordination, staffing overhead, insurance, the cost of running the event. Gratuity for the servers and bartenders is often a separate line, or expected on top. Read the contract language carefully, because "service charge" and "gratuity" mean different things and appear in different places.

Run the numbers so this lands. On a $20,000 food and bar total, a 20% service charge is $4,000. If you also budgeted a separate 18% gratuity because you assumed it was not included, that is another $3,600. That is not a rounding error. That is a honeymoon.

### what to ask
- is the service charge inclusive of staff gratuity, or separate?
- what specifically does the service charge cover?
- is the percentage applied to food only, or food plus bar plus rentals?

That last question matters. A service charge applied to your entire invoice, rentals included, costs meaningfully more than one applied to food alone.

## 2. Cake cutting, charged by the slice

You bought the cake from an outside baker to save money. Smart. Then your caterer charges you to cut and plate it.

Cake cutting fees run **$2-8 per slice, per guest**. At the high end, with 150 guests, that is **up to $1,200 extra** just to have someone slice the dessert you already paid for. Some caterers frame it as a plating and service fee, which is fair enough, but the amount can be startling when you see it multiplied across your whole guest count.

You have a few real options here:

1. Negotiate the fee down or out entirely, especially if you are already spending heavily on catering.
2. Ask whether a lower flat fee is available instead of per-slice pricing.
3. Skip the traditional cake and do a dessert the caterer already includes, or a display guests serve themselves.
4. Have your baker or a friend handle cutting if the venue allows it (many do not, so confirm first).

Even shaving the per-slice rate from $8 to $4 halves the charge. On 150 guests that is $600 back in your pocket for one line of the contract.

## 3. Corkage fees on every bottle you bring

If your venue or caterer lets you supply your own alcohol, wonderful, that is often where couples save the most. But watch for corkage. Corkage runs **$15-45 per bottle you bring in**, charged for opening, pouring, and serving wine or spirits you sourced yourself.

Do the math against your savings before you get excited. If you buy wine at $12 a bottle and the corkage fee is $25 a bottle, you are now paying $37 a bottle, and the "savings" evaporated. On a wedding pouring 60 bottles, a $25 corkage fee is $1,500 on top of the wine itself.

Always ask before you source your own. The answer changes the entire math of whether bringing your own alcohol saves you anything at all.

### watch for
Corkage that only applies to some categories. Some contracts charge corkage on wine but not on beer, or on spirits but not on wine. Others charge a flat per-person bar service fee on top of corkage. Ask for the full picture in writing, not a verbal "yeah that should be fine."

## 4. Vendor meals buried in the contract

Your photographer, your planner, your band, and your videographer all need to eat during a long event. Most contracts require you to feed them, and caterers charge for it.

Vendor meals run **$25-75 per vendor, per contract clause**. Photographers, planners, band members, all count, and it adds up faster than couples expect because you are counting people, not roles. A band is not one meal. A six-piece band plus a sound tech is seven meals. Add a photographer, a second shooter, a videographer, a planner, and an assistant, and you are at twelve or thirteen vendor meals before you have fed a single guest.

At $50 each, thirteen vendor meals is $650. Not catastrophic, but it is real money that never appeared on the proposal.

### what to ask
- is there a discounted vendor meal rate versus the full guest plate?
- can vendor meals be a simpler boxed or plated option at a lower price?
- exactly how many vendor meals does my current vendor lineup require?

Some caterers offer a reduced vendor meal that is not the full multi-course guest experience. That alone can cut this line significantly.

## 5. Overtime, charged by the hour

The reception is flying. Nobody wants to leave. So you keep it going past the contract end time. The clock, however, is running on the caterer, not on you.

Overtime rates run **$150-500 per hour past contract end**, and that is often just the catering staff. Your venue, band, DJ, and photographer may each have their own overtime rate too, meaning one extra hour can trigger three or four separate charges at once. An hour that "just happened" can cost more than $1,000 across all your vendors.

The move is to decide your timeline before the wedding, not in the moment at 11 p.m. when you are emotional and the dance floor is packed.

1. Confirm the exact contract end time in writing.
2. Ask the per-hour overtime rate for staff, and whether it is billed in full-hour increments.
3. Build a realistic timeline with your planner so you are not surprised.
4. If you genuinely want a later end, price the extra hour up front. Booked in advance it is often cheaper than triggered live.

## 6. The guest minimum you pay for no matter what

Caterers set a floor. Book for a certain guest count and you are committed to it, even if reality comes in lower.

Here is how it bites: you contract a **guest minimum**, half your RSVPs fall through, and you still **pay for 100 guests regardless** because that was the floor. You are buying meals nobody eats. On a $150 per-head package, the gap between 100 contracted and 70 who actually attend is 30 unserved plates, or $4,500 you paid for empty chairs.

### watch for
- a minimum set higher than your realistic guest list
- language that locks the minimum in early, before final RSVP counts
- whether the minimum is a headcount, a food-and-beverage spend, or both

Ask if there is flexibility to adjust closer to the date, and get the final headcount deadline in writing. A minimum you are confident you will clear is fine. A minimum set optimistically high is a trap you sign yourself into.

## red flags to catch before you sign

A few patterns should make you slow down and ask more questions:

- a proposal with a clean per-head number and no mention of service charge anywhere
- "gratuity included" language that does not specify who it goes to
- corkage or cake-cutting fees mentioned verbally but absent from the written contract
- a guest minimum noticeably higher than your actual guest list
- overtime terms that are vague about the hourly rate or increment
- vendor meal clauses without a stated per-meal price

None of these mean run away. They mean ask, in writing, before your signature is on the page. A good caterer will answer every one of these plainly. If a vendor gets cagey when you ask about line items, that itself is the answer.

## before you sign, do this

1. Add the service charge (18-22%) to your food and bar total, then confirm separately whether gratuity is on top.
2. Multiply any cake cutting fee by your full guest count. 150 guests at $8 a slice is up to $1,200.
3. Compare corkage per bottle against what you would actually save bringing your own alcohol.
4. Count your real vendor lineup and multiply by the vendor meal rate ($25-75 each).
5. Get the overtime rate ($150-500 per hour) and exact contract end time in writing.
6. Make sure the guest minimum matches a headcount you are confident you will hit.

The catering quote is never the final number, but it does not have to be a surprise either. Every one of these six lives somewhere in your contract, and every one is a conversation you can have before you commit.
