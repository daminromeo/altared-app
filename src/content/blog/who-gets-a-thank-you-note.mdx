say you get back from the honeymoon, open the registry dashboard, and start a list. forty-two gifts, forty-two notes. feels manageable. then you remember your aunt who drove six hours, your maid of honor who hemmed her own dress at midnight, the coordinator who found a sewing kit when your bustle broke, and your parents, who did about nine hundred things you only half noticed at the time.

the thank-you list is always longer than the gift list. that's the whole thing. gifts are the easy part to track because someone else tracked them for you. everything else lives in your memory, and memory gets worse the further you get from the wedding.

here's who gets a note, in the order worth writing them.

## 01. gift givers

a note: always.

every gift, including cash and anything off the registry. that last part trips people up. a check doesn't get a shorter note than a stand mixer, and the hand-thrown bowl your cousin bought outside your registry absolutely counts, arguably more than the registry items you picked yourself.

a few rules that make this painless:

1. **name the gift.** "thank you for the cast iron set" beats "thank you for the generous gift" every time. it proves a human opened the box.
2. **for cash, name the use, not the amount.** "we're putting it toward the apartment" or "this is going straight into the house fund." you never have to write the number down.
3. **write them in batches, not all at once.** ten a night is survivable. ninety in one sitting makes every note sound identical, and people compare.
4. **log the gift as it arrives.** the gift, the giver, and one detail about how it got to you (shipped, handed to you at the shower, left on the gift table). that detail becomes your first sentence.

if gifts started arriving before the wedding, those notes can go out before the wedding too. there's no rule saying you have to sit on them.

### what about group gifts?

everyone who chipped in gets a note. one note to the group reads like a group email. if you only know the organizer, ask them for the names. they'll have them.

## 02. your parents

a note: yes. even if they hosted or paid. especially if they did.

this is the one people skip, because you'll see your parents on sunday and you already said thank you forty times during the planning. but a spoken thank-you in the chaos of a wedding week is not the same object as something they can keep in a drawer.

write it like a letter, not a card. you have more to say to them than to someone who sent a gravy boat. mention a specific thing: the morning they drove the flowers over, the fight they didn't have about the seating chart, the thing they paid for quietly and never brought up. if they hosted or contributed financially, you don't have to name figures to acknowledge what it took.

this applies to in-laws, stepparents, and the family friend who functioned as a parent all weekend. if someone carried weight, they get a note.

## 03. the wedding party

a note: yes. for the time, the travel and the outfits they bought for you.

being in a wedding party costs money and vacation days. the dress or suit, the shoes, the travel to the bachelorette, the hotel block, the hours of availability you asked for. most of that spend is invisible to you because it never touched your budget. it touched theirs.

a gift at the rehearsal dinner is not a substitute for a note. the gift is the gift. the note is the thank-you. write them separately, and write them specifically: thank the person who handled your mother, the one who held your dress in the bathroom, the one who showed up to every single thing without being asked twice.

## 04. vendors who went further

a note: worth it.

a short note and a review mean a lot to a small vendor. not every vendor, necessarily. the ones who did the job they were paid for got paid for the job. but the ones who went further, the photographer who stayed past the contracted end, the baker who redid the tier after a delivery mishap, the coordinator who solved something you never heard about until later, those people are usually running small businesses where a specific public review changes their month.

do both:

1. a short handwritten note or email to them directly.
2. a review that names what they actually did, on whichever platform they're on.

if you want it to land, send the review link to anyone who asks you for a recommendation too. word of mouth is how most small wedding vendors survive. for more on how to work well with vendors from the first inquiry on, see [vendor tips](/blog/category/vendor-tips).

## 05. guests who traveled

a note: kind.

a gift isn't required to deserve one. the trip was the gift. someone who flew in, took days off, booked a room and showed up for a six-hour event gave you something, whether or not a box came with it.

this is the category people argue about, and the argument is usually "but they didn't give us anything." they gave you a flight, a hotel night, and a weekend. send the note. keep it short and warm: you're glad they made the trip, you loved the five minutes you got with them at the bar, you want to see them properly next time.

## watch for

a few things that quietly turn a thank-you note into a problem:

- **the printed card with a signature.** a pre-printed "thank you for celebrating with us" with two names at the bottom reads as a receipt, not a thank-you. if you're printing anything, leave real room to write.
- **the group text thank-you.** fine as a first response, not a replacement.
- **naming the dollar amount of a cash gift.** it makes the note feel like a ledger entry. name what you're doing with it instead.
- **waiting until you have the "perfect" stationery.** the note that goes out on a plain card beats the one that never goes out because the custom order was delayed.
- **one person writing all of them.** if both of you are in the marriage, both of you are on the list. split by relationship: you take yours, your partner takes theirs, and you each sign off on the other's.
- **losing the address list.** the addresses you collected for invitations are the same addresses you need now. don't let that spreadsheet disappear after the wedding.

## how to actually get through them

the practical version:

1. **build the list from five sources, not one:** the gift log, the guest list, the wedding party, your vendor contracts, and your travel/hotel block list. that last one catches the people who traveled without sending a gift.
2. **assign every name to one of you.** nobody should be guessing who wrote to whom.
3. **set a pace, not a deadline.** a fixed nightly number finishes faster than a vague "by the end of the month."
4. **keep the stationery, stamps, and address list in one box.** the friction is almost never the writing. it's finding the stamps.
5. **mark each one off as it's sealed,** so you never second-guess whether someone got one.
