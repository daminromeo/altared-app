Your venue quote from january is already higher than the one from november. Same room, same saturday, same everything, but the number moved because the calendar did. This is the part nobody warns you about: six of the most common wedding vendors raise prices on a schedule, and most couples find out after they waited too long.

The good news is that this is fixable, and it doesn't require you to become a hard-nosed negotiator. Timing is the negotiation. None of this requires haggling. It just requires going first. Here's the list, in the order the price hikes usually hit, and exactly when to lock each one in.

## 01. the venue: prices jump 10-20% every january

Venues are the single biggest line item for most couples, which makes them the most expensive thing to get wrong on timing. Most venues re-rate their pricing at the top of the calendar year, and the jump is real: prices jump 10-20% every january.

Think about what that means in dollars. If a venue quotes you $15,000 in november and you sit on it "to think about it" until february, that same package can land at $16,500 to $18,000. You did nothing except wait, and it cost you up to $3,000.

The move: book by november of the prior year. Venues that release next-year pricing early are essentially giving you a window to lock the old rate. If a coordinator tells you "we're finalizing next year's pricing," that is your cue to sign now, not later.

### what to actually ask the venue

- Is this quote guaranteed, and for how long?
- When does next year's pricing take effect?
- Are there date-based tiers (peak vs off-peak) inside the same year?
- What's the deposit to hold today's rate?

If they can't tell you when pricing changes, assume it changes soon. For a deeper breakdown of what hides inside a venue contract, our [hidden costs](/blog/category/hidden-costs) posts go line by line.

## 02. the photographer: rates rise after every booked season

Photographers don't reprice on a fixed date the way venues do. They reprice based on demand, and demand is measured in booked seasons. Once a photographer fills a season, they raise their rates for the next one, because they can. Their calendar is proof that they're worth more.

That's why signing early is worth so much here: locking in 12-18 months out saves around 15%. On a $4,000 photography package, 15% is $600 back in your pocket, and all you did was book before their next season filled up.

The trap is the "we love them but let's compare a few more" delay. By the time you circle back, they've shot two more weddings, gotten two more referrals, and quietly bumped their collections. The rate you saw is not the rate that waits for you.

The move: if a photographer's work is right and the vibe is right, sign 12-18 months out. You are not just buying photos, you are buying today's price.

## 03. the florist: wholesale flower costs spike in spring

Florists are at the mercy of a supply chain most couples never see. Wholesale flower costs spike in spring, around valentine's day, mother's day, and the start of peak wedding season. When the florist's costs go up, your quote goes up, and there's no negotiating away a global wholesale market.

The lever you do control is timing. Contract in off-season, and you can save up to 20%. A $5,000 floral order contracted in the quiet late-fall or winter months can come in closer to $4,000 than the spring version of the same arrangements.

### how to lock a florist rate off-season

1. Get a written itemized proposal, not a lump-sum number.
2. Confirm the quote holds through your wedding date regardless of when you actually pay in full.
3. Ask which blooms are priced as "market rate" (those are the ones that move most).
4. Where you can, choose in-season flowers for your date to blunt the spikes.

You won't control the wholesale market, but you can decide when you sign against it.

## 04. live music: bands add a travel surcharge after 40 miles

Live bands raise rates fastest of any vendor on this list, and they have a second cost lever most couples forget: travel. Bands add a travel surcharge after 40 miles. That surcharge can quietly stack lodging, transport, and per-musician fees onto an already-rising base rate.

Because bands raise rates fastest, the booking window matters more here than almost anywhere else. Book 14+ months out, and their rates hold longest. Wait, and you're negotiating against both a higher base and a travel line you didn't budget for.

The move: book 14+ months out, and get the travel surcharge spelled out in writing before you sign. If your venue is more than 40 miles from where the band is based, ask for that number now, not in a follow-up email three months before the wedding.

### watch for these on the band contract

- A travel surcharge that's "TBD" instead of a fixed figure
- Overtime rates buried below the base fee
- Separate charges for sound equipment, lighting, or a smaller ceremony set
- Meal requirements for the full band (real, and worth budgeting)

## 05. the caterer: per-head price rises with food costs quarterly

Catering is priced per head, which means every small increase gets multiplied by your guest count. And caterers reprice per-head quarterly as food costs move. A quote you get in the spring can be repriced by summer, and again by fall.

Here's the rule that saves people: your rate at signing is the only rate that's safe. Lock your rate at signing, not six months out. A verbal "this is roughly what it'll be" is not protection. If the per-head number isn't in a signed contract, assume it will move.

Run the math. At $120 per head for 100 guests, you're at $12,000. If the caterer reprices up 8% before you sign, that same menu is $12,960. Nearly a thousand dollars, from a quarterly adjustment you never saw happen.

The move: get the per-head number, the guest minimum, and the date-of-service rate all in writing, and sign to lock it. Anything unsigned is a moving target. Our [contracts](/blog/category/contracts) breakdowns cover what a real per-head catering agreement should include.

## 06. hair + makeup: lead artists raise rates every 6-9 months

The lead artist (the one whose name is on the studio, the one you actually want doing your face) is the one whose price climbs steadily. Lead artists raise rates every 6-9 months. It's a smaller line item than your venue, but the increase is frequent, so waiting has a real cost.

There's a clever move here that doubles as smart planning: book the trial first, it locks their price. When you book and pay for the trial, most lead artists will hold your date and your rate at the current number. You get to test the look and freeze the price in one step.

The move: don't treat the trial as a "later" task. Booking the trial first is what locks the artist and the rate before the next 6-to-9-month bump.

## the pattern behind all six

Notice what these six vendors have in common. None of them are trying to trick you. They raise prices because their costs rise, their calendars fill, and their demand grows. The couples who "get a deal" usually didn't haggle harder. They booked earlier.

That's the whole strategy: timing is literally the negotiation. Going first beats bargaining second, almost every time.

Here's the quick-reference version to save:

1. **Venue** (jumps 10-20% every january): book by november of the prior year.
2. **Photographer** (rates rise after every booked season): sign 12-18 months out to save around 15%.
3. **Florist** (wholesale spikes in spring): contract off-season to save up to 20%.
4. **Live band** (raises rates fastest, plus travel surcharge after 40 miles): book 14+ months out.
5. **Caterer** (per-head reprices quarterly): lock your rate at signing, not six months out.
6. **Hair + makeup** (lead artists raise every 6-9 months): book the trial first to lock the price.

### red flags to watch for before you sign anything

- A quote with no expiration date and no "held until" clause. If it can move, it will.
- A verbal "roughly this much" on catering or florals with nothing in writing.
- A "TBD" travel surcharge on a band contract.
- A coordinator who mentions "next year's pricing" but won't tell you the date it starts.
- Any vendor pressuring you to sign a rate that's higher than what you saw in writing weeks ago.

The fix for all of these is the same: get the number in a signed contract, with the date it holds until.
