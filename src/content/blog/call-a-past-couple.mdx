say you're deciding between two photographers. both have five-star pages, both have galleries that made you a little emotional, both answered your first email within a day. on paper there is nothing to choose between them. so you pick the one whose instagram grid you like more, and you find out in month seven that one of them replies to stressful emails in four days and sends an assistant you've never met.

reviews tell you what the vendor wants you to know. a past couple tells you what actually happened.

that gap is where most booking regret lives. a review is written in public, usually within a week of the wedding, usually while the couple is still floating. it's a snapshot of a feeling. a past couple on a ten-minute phone call, a year out, with nobody watching, will tell you about the 45-minute slip, the line item nobody mentioned, and the person who actually showed up.

here are six questions worth asking before you sign anything.

## how to actually get a past couple on the phone

ask the vendor directly: "could you connect me with a couple from last season?" a good vendor says yes without flinching. they may ask for a day to check with the couple first, which is normal and polite.

a few things that make this work better:

1. ask for a couple whose wedding looked like yours. same rough guest count, same venue type, same season. a photographer's experience of a 40-person garden lunch tells you very little about their experience of a 200-person ballroom night.
2. ask for someone recent, ideally within the last year. teams change, second shooters change, pricing structures change.
3. offer a specific, short window. "ten minutes, any evening this week" gets a yes far more often than "whenever you're free."
4. lead with the easy questions and save the rebook question for last.
5. take notes while they talk, not after. the detail you'll want in three months is the exact one you'll forget.

if the vendor can't or won't connect you with anyone, that is information too. more on that below.

## the six questions

### 01. timeline gaps

ask: **"did they run behind, and by how long?"**

not "were they on time," which gets you a polite yes. the "by how long" is the part that does the work, because it forces a number out of a vague memory.

a 45-min slip changes your whole day order. it eats the golden hour portraits, it pushes speeches into dessert, it means the band starts late and the venue's hard stop doesn't move to accommodate it. one vendor running behind is rarely contained to that vendor.

listen for whether the slip was communicated. "they were behind but they told my coordinator at noon so we shuffled things" is a completely different vendor from "they were behind and we found out when nobody showed up."

### 02. contact speed

ask: **"how fast did they reply when things got stressful?"**

everyone is responsive during the sales conversation. the question is what happens in month eight when the couple needs a seating-chart decision answered, or the week of, when something changes and they need a yes or no today.

slow replies pre-wedding mean slow on the day. if a past couple says they waited a week for an answer in april, assume you'll be waiting on the day you cannot afford to wait.

ask the follow-up too: what channel actually worked? some vendors are unreachable by email and instant on text. that's fine, as long as you know it going in.

### 03. hidden costs

ask: **"did the final invoice match the quote?"**

this is the single most useful question on the list and the one couples skip because it feels nosy. it isn't. you're not asking what they paid, you're asking whether the number moved.

note every line they mention that surprised them. overtime. travel. meals for the team. a delivery or pickup window. an additional hour of setup because the venue's access rules weren't what everyone assumed. you are not collecting prices here, you are collecting the names of line items so you can ask your vendor about each one before you sign.

then take those line items into your own quote conversation and ask for the all-in number. if you're comparing multiple vendors, the only honest comparison is all-in against all-in. more on that in [hidden costs](/blog/category/hidden-costs).

### 04. problem handling

ask: **"did anything go wrong, and what did they do?"**

something goes wrong at every wedding. the vendor who tells you nothing has ever gone wrong is either new or not being straight with you.

how a vendor recovers matters more than the slip. you're listening for ownership and speed. did they notice before the couple did? did they fix it quietly, or did it become the couple's problem to solve in a dress at 4pm? did they offer anything afterward without being asked?

a past couple describing a recovery warmly is one of the strongest signals you'll get in this whole process.

### 05. point person

ask: **"who actually showed up, or was it a different team?"**

some vendors send assistants without saying so. this is not automatically bad. big teams are often the reason a vendor can deliver at all. it is only bad when it's a surprise.

so find out: was the person you met in the consultation the person on the floor? if not, who was, and were they good? and the practical follow-up for your own contract, is the named person written into it, or does it just say the company name?

### 06. would you rebook

ask: **"knowing everything now, would you rebook them?"**

that last one especially. a pause before yes is its own answer.

people are generous about vendors they've already paid. they don't want to be the reason someone loses work. so they will say yes, but the shape of the yes tells you everything. "yes, absolutely, I already recommended them to my sister" is a different answer from "yeah... I mean, yes, for the price, yes."

if you hear the pause, ask one gentle follow-up: "what would you do differently?" that's where the real answer lives.

## red flags to watch for

- the vendor can't connect you with any past couple, or keeps saying they'll check and never does
- the reference is clearly coached, speaks in marketing language, or can't recall a single specific detail about their own wedding day
- every answer is positive and completely non-specific
- the past couple mentions invoice surprises and your quote doesn't address them in writing
- the past couple doesn't know who showed up, which usually means nobody told them in advance
- the vendor responds to your questions by getting defensive instead of explaining

none of these are automatic no's on their own. two or more together, and you should slow down before you sign.

## what to do with the answers

you'll have three or four conversations like this across different vendors, and by week two they blur. write everything down in the same place, in the same shape, so you're comparing the same things.

1. ask the vendor for a recent past couple with a similar wedding to yours
2. run the six questions in order, saving "would you rebook" for last
3. write down every surprise line item by name, don't estimate what it cost
4. take those line items back to your vendor and ask for the all-in number in writing
5. check that the named point person appears in the contract, not just the company
6. compare your quotes side by side before you commit, not after
