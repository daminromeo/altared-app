four emails land in the same 36 hours. the caterer wants your final headcount by friday. the photographer wants the confirmed timeline "whenever you have it, ideally in the next two weeks." the dj's invoice shows a remaining balance due 14 days out, check or zelle, no cards. and buried in the catering email, one line you skimmed past the first time: *please also confirm vendor meal count.*

none of these are unreasonable requests. all of them arrive at once, usually during the week you are also chasing down your uncle's entrée choice and figuring out whether the shuttle company actually confirmed.

the last month before your wedding is not a vibe check. it is an admin sprint, and every vendor on your list will come to you with the same four questions at roughly the same time. here is exactly what they are asking for, when it is actually due, and what it costs you if you get it wrong.

## 01. your final headcount

most contracts lock guest count 2-3 weeks out, and you pay the minimum even if rsvps drop.

that second half is the part that catches people. your catering contract almost certainly has a guaranteed minimum, a floor number of guests you committed to when you signed. if you contracted for 120 and 104 people actually rsvp yes, you are still paying for 120. the caterer bought food, scheduled staff, and held the date based on that number.

so the "final headcount" your caterer asks for is really two numbers: the number you are serving, and the number you are *paying for*. they are often not the same.

### what to have ready

- your contracted minimum (pull the exact figure from your catering contract, not from memory)
- your current confirmed yes count
- your meal selections broken down by type, if you are doing plated service
- allergy and dietary restriction counts, separately, because the kitchen needs these in their own column

### the people everyone forgets to count

the headcount conversation gets messy because different vendors count different people. a few that routinely get missed:

1. **kids.** some caterers charge a reduced children's rate, some charge full, some require a separate count. ask which.
2. **the wedding party's plus-ones** who rsvp'd verbally to someone else and never hit the form.
3. **day-of helpers** who are not technically vendors but are definitely eating.
4. **yourselves.** yes, really. couples get left off the count more often than you would think.

once you send that number, it is locked. you can usually add a guest or two after the deadline (for a fee). you can almost never subtract. so the move is to send a number you are confident in, not an optimistic one.

## 02. your remaining balance

remaining balance is due 7-30 days before the date, and ask each vendor how they want to be paid.

this is where couples get genuinely blindsided, not because the money is unexpected but because the *timing* is. you have been paying deposits over twelve or eighteen months in comfortable increments. then four or five final balances all come due in the same two-week window.

if your photographer's balance is due 30 days out, your caterer's is due 14 days out, your florist's is due 10 days out, and your dj's is due 7 days out, you are moving a lot of money through your checking account in a very short period. and if one of those payments is a wire, you need the funds *settled*, not pending.

### payment methods are not negotiable (usually)

ask each vendor how they want to be paid, and ask early. the final balance is usually wire or check only. a lot of vendors who accepted a card for the deposit will not accept one for the balance, because the processing fee on a $6,000 payment is real money to them. some will accept a card with a 3% surcharge added. some will not take a card at all.

things worth confirming for every vendor on your list:

1. accepted payment method for the final balance specifically
2. the exact due date (and whether "due" means postmarked or received)
3. who the check is made out to (business name, not the owner's name, usually)
4. whether there is a late fee, and what it is
5. for wires, the account details, confirmed by phone and not just email

that last one matters. wire fraud targeting wedding couples is a real thing. if you get an email with "updated" wire instructions, call the vendor at the number in your signed contract before you send anything. not the number in the email.

### watch for

- **a balance that does not match your contract.** add your deposits, subtract from the total, and check the math yourself before you pay.
- **a service charge you forgot was a percentage.** a 22% service charge on a catering bill scales with your headcount. if your count went up, so did that line.
- **"due 7 days before" when the 7th day is a holiday weekend.** banks close. build in a buffer.
- **a vendor who will not confirm payment details in writing.** that is not a scheduling issue, that is a red flag.

if you are still in the earlier stages and want to see how these payment schedules stack up before you sign anything, our breakdown in [/blog/category/contracts](/blog/category/contracts) covers what to look for in the payment terms section.

## 03. your confirmed run-of-show

photographers, djs, and planners all need your confirmed timeline 2-4 weeks before.

your run-of-show is the minute-by-minute schedule of your wedding day. it is not a vibe document. it is the thing your photographer uses to decide when to put on a different lens, the thing your dj uses to know when to kill the music, and the thing your coordinator uses to know when to start herding people toward the ceremony.

the reason vendors want it 2-4 weeks out is that it determines their staffing and their own internal schedule. a photographer who thought they were shooting an 8-hour day and discovers in the final week that your sendoff is at 11pm has a problem. so do you.

### what a usable run-of-show actually includes

- vendor arrival times, each one listed separately
- hair and makeup start and finish, with a named person per slot
- first look, if you are doing one
- family photo list with a time block assigned to it
- ceremony start (and the real start, not the invitation time)
- cocktail hour, reception entrance, toasts, first dance, cake, sendoff
- vendor departure and breakdown windows
- phone numbers for every vendor and every person responsible for something

### the circular problem

here is the part nobody warns you about: your photographer wants the timeline, but your timeline depends on when your photographer arrives, which depends on your hair and makeup schedule, which depends on your headcount of people getting ready. so you are building a document from inputs that are still moving.

the fix is to send a draft early and label it a draft. vendors would overwhelmingly rather see a 90% timeline four weeks out and give you notes than receive a perfect one five days before. send the draft, collect their feedback, send the confirmed version, and then send it again in the final week with a subject line that says "FINAL." people's inboxes are a mess in that last week.

## 04. your vendor meal count

caterers require a vendor meal count separately, typically $15-35 per vendor, billed on the final invoice.

this is the one that shows up as a line item people did not budget for. most vendor contracts include a clause requiring a hot meal for any team member working more than a certain number of hours. your photographer brings a second shooter. your dj has an assistant. your planner has two day-of coordinators. your band has seven people.

count them. a realistic vendor meal count for a mid-size wedding might be 10 to 14 people. at $15-35 per vendor, that is roughly $150 to $490 added to your final catering invoice. not a catastrophe, but not nothing either, and it is unpleasant to discover it the week you are already writing four other checks.

### how to handle it

1. **read the meals clause in every vendor contract.** it will specify how many people and sometimes what kind of meal.
2. **ask your caterer what a vendor meal actually is.** at some caterers it is the same plated entrée at the same price as a guest. at others it is a boxed or buffet-style option at the lower end of that $15-35 range. the difference across 12 people is meaningful.
3. **ask where they eat and when.** vendors usually eat during your dinner service so they are available during toasts and dancing. your venue needs to have a space for that.
4. **confirm the count in writing with the caterer,** separately from your guest headcount. these are two different numbers on two different lines.

### watch for

a vendor who adds a team member in the final weeks and does not tell you. a band that shows up with a sound engineer you did not know about is three more plates. ask every vendor, in writing, how many people from their team will be on site on the day. then match that against your meal count.

## none of this is a surprise if you write it down early

the four numbers are predictable. the deadlines are in your contracts. the only reason the final month feels like an ambush is that the information lives in eleven different pdfs, three email threads, and a text message from your florist.

none of this is a surprise if you have it written down before month one of planning ends.

**do this now, whatever month you are in:**

1. open every signed contract and write down the final payment due date and the accepted payment method
2. write down your contracted minimum headcount and the date it locks
3. ask each vendor how many team members will be on site, so your vendor meal count is real
4. start a timeline draft today, even a rough one, and send it to your photographer and coordinator 4 weeks out
5. put all five of those things in one place where you and your partner can both see them
