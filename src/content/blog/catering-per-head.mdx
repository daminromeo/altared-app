Two couples get quotes from the same caterer. One is told "$95 per head." The other is told "$78 per head." Same venue, same season, same headcount. The first couple walks away thinking they got quoted high. The second signs on the spot, thrilled.

Six weeks later, both couples have a final invoice within $40 of each other. The difference was never the food. It was everything sitting quietly underneath the per-head number, priced differently, formatted differently, and in one case not listed at all.

The price per head your caterer quoted is a starting point, not a final number. That is the single most useful thing to know before your next tasting or venue walkthrough. Below are the five line items that quietly bump your catering price per head, roughly what each one costs, and exactly where to look for it in a proposal.

## why comparing headline numbers is a trap

Here is the problem most couples run into. You collect three catering proposals to compare them, which is the right instinct. But the add-ons aren't even listed the same way across the three. One caterer bakes the bar into the per-head number. Another lists it as a separate package. A third quotes you dinner only and mentions cocktail hour verbally at the tasting, so it never makes it onto the page you're comparing.

So you end up comparing a number that includes the bar against a number that doesn't, and the "cheaper" caterer isn't cheaper at all. None of this is a surprise once you know where to look. The trick is knowing that the headline number is doing different work in each quote.

Let's go line by line.

## 1. the bar package

Upgrading from beer and wine to full bar adds $18 to $40 per head. That is one of the largest single swings on this list, and it's often presented as a casual upsell at the tasting ("do you want to do a full bar or keep it simple?") rather than a $30-per-person decision.

Run the math on your actual guest count and it gets real fast. At 120 guests, the gap between a beer-and-wine setup and a full bar can be:

1. 120 guests times $18 = $2,160 on the low end
2. 120 guests times $40 = $4,800 on the high end

That is a $2,640 spread on one checkbox. Neither number is wrong. Full bar genuinely costs more in liquor, mixers, glassware, and a bartender who's making cocktails instead of pouring pinot. But you want to make that call as a budget decision, not a mood decision at a tasting where you've already had two glasses of the pairing wine.

### what to ask

Ask whether the quoted per-head number includes any bar at all, or whether alcohol is a separate line. Ask what "full bar" actually covers, house liquor or premium, and whether signature cocktails count as an upgrade on top of that. The words "full bar" mean different things at different companies.

## 2. service style: plated vs buffet

Plated service needs more staff than buffet, and that runs $12 to $25 more per head. It feels counterintuitive because a buffet has more food out at once. But a buffet mostly staffs itself, while plated service means a server for roughly every two tables, coursed timing, and a kitchen firing hundreds of individual plates on a schedule.

At 120 guests, choosing plated over buffet adds somewhere between $1,440 and $3,000. It's a real upgrade in feel, and plenty of couples decide it's worth it. The point is that "which service style" is a per-head pricing question, not just an aesthetic one, and it should be on the same page you're comparing across caterers.

Family-style usually lands between the two, so if the plated number stings, ask whether family-style is an option. It gets you most of the seated-dinner feel without the full plated staffing cost.

## 3. cocktail hour appetizers

Here is the one that catches people, because it sits outside the dinner quote entirely. Passed appetizers run $8 to $15 per person on top of the dinner number. Cocktail hour is often discussed as a given ("guests will mingle while we take photos") without anyone connecting it to a separate charge.

At 120 guests, that's another $960 to $1,800 that may not appear anywhere near the per-head figure you've been comparing. If one caterer folded cocktail hour into their quote and another didn't, the second one looks cheaper for a reason that has nothing to do with value.

### the two ways this hides

- Stationary vs passed. Stationary displays (a cheese board, a grazing table) are usually cheaper per head than passed hors d'oeuvres that require staff walking trays. Some quotes assume one, some assume the other.
- Piece count. "Passed appetizers" might mean three pieces per guest or six. Doubling the piece count roughly doubles this line. Get the number of pieces per person in writing.

## 4. guest count and the minimum

Dropping below the minimum can raise the per-head rate by 10 to 20%. Most caterers and venues set a guest minimum (or a food-and-beverage minimum in dollars) to make an event worth their staffing. Come in under it and you either pay a flat penalty or the per-head rate climbs to make up the difference.

This bites hardest for smaller weddings. If your quoted rate is $95 per head and you fall below the minimum, a 10 to 20% bump pushes you to roughly $105 to $114 per head, before any of the add-ons above. A 60-person wedding can end up with a worse per-head rate than a 150-person one at the same venue.

The flip side matters too. Minimums are a negotiation point. If you're comfortably above the minimum, that's leverage. If you're hovering just under it, ask what the exact penalty is and whether shifting to an off-peak date lowers the minimum instead of the rate.

## 5. the gratuity line (and the tip it isn't)

That 18 to 22% service charge on every line is mandatory at most venues, and it's still separate from the tip you'll want to leave the team.

This is the one that changes your total the most because it's a percentage of everything above it. Stack up the bar upgrade, plated service, and cocktail hour, then add 18 to 22% on top of the whole thing. The service charge grows every time one of the earlier line items does.

Work an example at a $12,000 catering subtotal:

1. Subtotal: $12,000
2. Service charge at 18%: $2,160 (bringing you to $14,160)
3. Service charge at 22%: $2,640 (bringing you to $14,640)

And here's the part couples miss: the service charge is often an administrative and operations fee, not a tip that goes to your servers and bartenders. Many contracts say so in the fine print. So you may still want to tip the team on the day, on top of that 18 to 22%. Budget for it as its own line.

## red flags to watch for in a catering quote

When you're reading a proposal, slow down at these:

- A per-head number with no itemized breakdown underneath it. If you can't see the bar, service style, and cocktail hour as separate lines, you can't compare it to anything.
- "Plus applicable fees" or "service charge additional" with no percentage. Make them name the number. 18% and 22% are a meaningfully different total.
- Cocktail hour discussed out loud but missing from the written quote.
- A quoted rate that assumes a guest count you haven't confirmed. If your headcount drifts down, does the rate change?
- A service charge described as gratuity when it's actually an operations fee, or the reverse. Ask directly: does any of this reach the staff, and should we tip separately?
- Rentals (glassware, linens, chargers, china) bundled into some quotes and excluded from others.

If a caterer gets defensive when you ask for line-item detail, that's information too. The good ones expect these questions and answer them fast.

## how to compare quotes the right way

Once you know the five levers, comparing proposals gets simple. Rebuild each quote so it includes the same things, then compare the true totals.

1. Confirm your working guest count and whether it clears each caterer's minimum.
2. Pick one service style and price every quote that way (plated vs buffet vs family-style).
3. Add the bar package at the same level across all quotes (beer and wine, or full bar).
4. Add cocktail hour with the same piece count per guest to every quote.
5. Apply each caterer's actual service charge percentage to their own subtotal.
6. Add a separate tip line so it's never a surprise on the day.

Do that and the headline number stops mattering. You're comparing final totals for identical events, which is the only comparison that tells you anything.

## the short version

Before you sign a catering contract, run every quote through these five:

- Bar package: full bar adds $18 to $40 per head over beer and wine.
- Service style: plated runs $12 to $25 more per head than buffet.
- Cocktail hour: passed appetizers add $8 to $15 per person, outside the dinner quote.
- Guest count: dropping below the minimum raises the rate 10 to 20%.
- Gratuity line: an 18 to 22% service charge is usually mandatory, and still not the tip.
