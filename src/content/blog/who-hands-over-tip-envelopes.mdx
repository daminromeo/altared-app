say it's two days out and you're sitting at the kitchen table with a stack of envelopes, a pen, and a vendor list. you write a name on each one. officiant. photographer. catering. DJ. hair and makeup. you seal them, stack them, and feel very organized.

then the morning of, the envelopes are in a tote bag under a chair in the getting-ready room, and you are in a dress, and the officiant is already walking to his car.

the envelopes are labeled. now who actually hands them over? decide it before the day.

that second half is the part most couples skip, and it's the part that actually determines whether the money gets where it's going. labeling is a filing task. handing over is a logistics task, and logistics need a name and a time attached.

## why the handoff needs a name, not a vibe

"we'll give them out at the reception" is not a plan. on a wedding day, you are the least available person in the building. you're being photographed, pulled into toasts, hugged by relatives who drove four hours. you cannot track five vendors' departure times while that is happening.

the people who can are the ones who aren't in the photos. a parent. your point person (planner, coordinator, or the most reliable friend in your group who said yes to a job, not just a title). assign each envelope to one of them, write it down, and tell them the timing out loud.

one rule worth holding onto: tips go at the end of service, not the start. an envelope handed over at 9am sits in someone's bag all day and quietly changes the tone of the working relationship. the exception is the vendor who leaves before anything else begins, which we'll get to.

## the five handoffs, in order

here's the full map, vendor by vendor. assign a name to each line before the week of.

1. **officiant.** who hands it over: a parent. after the ceremony, before photos pull everyone away.
2. **photographer.** who hands it over: your point person. at the end of their coverage, not the start.
3. **catering staff.** who hands it over: your point person. after dinner service, to the catering lead to share out.
4. **DJ or band.** who hands it over: your point person. at the end of the night, once the last song is done.
5. **hair and makeup.** who hands it over: you. before they pack up, so nobody is chasing anyone later.

### 01. officiant

a parent is the right choice here for one reason: the officiant leaves fast. ceremony ends, recessional happens, and within about fifteen minutes the officiant is often out the door while the two of you are being herded toward family photos.

a parent is already near the front, already standing, and already going to say thank you. give the envelope to whichever parent is most likely to actually find the person, not whichever parent you worry about offending. after the ceremony, before photos pull everyone away.

### 02. photographer

photographers have a coverage end time written into the contract, and that's your cue. at the end of their coverage, not the start.

your point person should know that end time (it's in the contract, and it should be in your day-of timeline), and should be watching for the moment the camera comes down. this is also a natural moment for a real thank-you, which matters more than the envelope.

if you have a second shooter or a videographer with a separate envelope, decide now whether they get handed separately or whether the lead distributes. ask the photographer which they prefer. they'll tell you.

### 03. catering staff

this one goes to a person, not to a crowd. after dinner service, to the catering lead to share out.

the catering lead is the captain, banquet manager, or whoever introduced themselves to you during setup. they know who worked the event and how the house splits things. handing out individual envelopes to servers you happen to see creates an uneven mess, and it tends to miss the back of house entirely.

before the day, ask your caterer directly: is gratuity already included in the contract, and who should an additional envelope go to? check your signed contract too. see our [contracts](/blog/category/contracts) posts for how to read a service charge line without guessing.

### 04. DJ or band

at the end of the night, once the last song is done. not during the last song, not at dinner, not when you're saying goodbye at the exit.

for a band, the envelope goes to the bandleader unless they tell you otherwise. your point person should be the one still standing at load-out, because the two of you may well be gone by then. if your exit happens before the music ends, this handoff absolutely cannot be yours.

### 05. hair and makeup

this is the one you do yourself. before they pack up, so nobody is chasing anyone later.

hair and makeup leave early. if you don't hand it over while they're still zipping brushes into a kit, the envelope goes home in your tote and becomes a venmo you'll remember three days later on your honeymoon. you are in the chair, they are right there, and it takes ten seconds. keep those envelopes in the getting-ready room, not with the rest of the stack.

## watch for these

a few things that reliably break the plan:

- **the tote bag problem.** all the envelopes in one bag, and the bag ends up in a car, a suite, or wherever someone set it down. split them: hair and makeup stays with you, everything else goes to your point person at the start of the day in one labeled pouch.
- **assigning a role instead of a name.** "the maid of honor will do it" is weaker than "sarah will do it, and she knows." say it to the person directly and get a yes.
- **double tipping.** if gratuity is built into a contract, an extra envelope is a choice, not an obligation. read the line before you write a name on an envelope.
- **no backup.** if your point person is also in the wedding party and in half the photos, name a second. a parent is usually the most available adult in the room.
- **handing a tip to someone mid-service.** a server carrying plates cannot take an envelope. wait for the lead.
- **leaving before the vendor does.** your exit is often earlier than load-out. anything that happens after your exit cannot be your job.

## how to lock it in this week

1. list every vendor who might get an envelope, and check each contract for whether gratuity is already included.
2. write a name next to each one: you, a parent, or your point person.
3. write a time next to each one, pulled from your actual timeline (coverage end, after dinner service, after the last song).
4. seal and label the envelopes, then split them: yours stay in the getting-ready room, the rest go to your point person in one pouch.
5. send your point person the list the week of, and confirm they have it in writing, not just a conversation.

keeping every vendor, every payment and every due date in one place makes step one take minutes instead of an evening of inbox archaeology.
