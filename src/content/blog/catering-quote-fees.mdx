You get the catering quote back. It says $85 a head. You have 150 guests, so you do the math in your head, feel a wave of relief, and mentally file catering as "handled." Then contract week arrives, you actually read the six-page document, and the number at the bottom is thousands of dollars higher than the one you memorized.

Nothing shady happened. The quote was real. It just was not the total. Your catering quote is not your catering total, and the gap between the two lives in the fine print of a document that is dense, long, and sent to you right when you are most excited. That is exactly when it is hardest to read slowly.

So here are the seven fees that most often close that gap. Save this for contract week, go line by line, and if something is not in writing, ask for it in writing before you sign.

## the fee that dwarfs all the others: service charge

Start here because it is the biggest and the most misunderstood.

The service charge runs 18-24% on food and bar, and it is not the tip. Read that twice, because a lot of couples assume the service charge is the gratuity and stop worrying about tipping. It usually is not. The service charge covers the caterer's operational costs (staffing, coordination, overhead), and a tip for the servers and bartenders is often expected on top of it.

Run the numbers on a real order. Say your food and bar subtotal lands at $18,000. A 20% service charge adds $3,600 before you have tipped a single person. That is not a rounding error. That is a honeymoon.

### how to handle it

1. Find the exact percentage in the contract, not "industry standard" from a phone call.
2. Ask in writing whether any portion of the service charge goes to staff as gratuity.
3. Ask whether the service charge applies to the bar and to rentals, or only to food.
4. Budget the tip separately, as a distinct line, so you are never surprised.

If the caterer will not tell you clearly what the service charge covers, treat that as your first red flag.

## the per-slice and per-table fees that scale with your guest count

These two are dangerous because they look small on paper and then multiply by your headcount.

### cake cutting

The cake cutting fee is $2-6 per slice. Sounds trivial until you multiply. At the high end, 150 guests is $900 just to have staff cut and plate a cake you already paid a separate baker to make. If your venue or caterer charges this, you have a few moves: ask them to waive it if you are already spending a certain amount, serve a sheet cake in the back and display a smaller show cake, or choose a dessert that does not trigger the fee at all.

### linen rental

Linens run $25-75 per table and are rarely in the base price. Picture 20 guest tables at the middle of that range, roughly $50 each, and you have added about $1,000 for tablecloths and napkins. The base quote often assumes the most basic house linen or none at all, so the upgraded color you saw on the mood board is a line item you have not been shown yet.

Ask specifically: are linens included, and if so, which ones, in what color, and for how many tables (guest tables plus cocktail, gift, cake, and sweetheart tables all count).

## the fees tied to your specific choices

Some fees only appear because of a decision you made, which makes them easy to forget you triggered.

### corkage, if you bring your own wine

Bringing your own wine feels like the obvious way to save money. Then you meet the corkage fee: $15-35 per bottle for the caterer to open, pour, and serve it. Do the math against buying through the caterer before you assume BYO wins. If you are opening 40 bottles at $25 corkage, that is $1,000 in service fees on wine you already bought. Sometimes BYO plus corkage still beats their markup. Sometimes it does not. The only way to know is to price both.

### vendor meals

Your contract almost certainly requires you to feed the people working your wedding. Vendor meals run $25-50 per vendor head, and that includes caterers, DJs, photographers, and anyone else on-site for the duration. A typical wedding has a photographer, a second shooter, a videographer, a DJ or band members, and a planner. Six vendor meals at $40 is $240 you did not put in the spreadsheet. Feeding your vendors is the right thing to do, and often contractually required, so budget for it rather than resenting it.

## the two fees you might not trigger at all (and might not avoid either)

The last two are conditional, which is exactly why they slip past people.

### overtime

Overtime is $150-500 per extra hour, charged for every hour past your contract end time. Weddings run long. The dancing is good, nobody wants to leave, and suddenly you are 45 minutes past the hard stop your contract set. That does not round down. Know your exact end time, know the per-hour rate, and decide in advance whether you are willing to pay for one more hour or whether the music simply stops. Assign someone who is not you (a planner, a trusted friend) to watch the clock, because you will not be watching it.

### bar minimum

A bar minimum is a spend floor. If you agree to a $5,000 bar minimum and your guests only drink $4,200 worth, you pay the $800 gap anyway. You pay whether your guests hit it or not. This is not automatically a bad deal, plenty of weddings clear the minimum easily, but you need to know the number and be honest about your crowd. A brunch wedding with a lot of kids and non-drinkers will hit a bar minimum very differently than a Saturday-night party.

## how to actually catch all seven

None of these are hidden on purpose. They live in the fine print of a long document you receive at your most excited, least careful moment. Here is the routine that catches them:

1. Read the contract twice, once fast for the shape of it and once slow with a highlighter.
2. Go fee by fee through this exact list and find (or rule out) each one.
3. Write down the number for every fee that applies to you, in a single running total.
4. For anything a vendor promised verbally, reply by email and ask them to confirm it in writing.
5. Add your separate tip budget on top of the service charge, not instead of it.
6. Compare the true total against your other quotes, not the headline per-head price.

### red flags to watch for

- A quote with a suspiciously low per-head price and a vague "plus fees" note at the bottom.
- A caterer who cannot or will not tell you what the service charge covers.
- "Don't worry, we'll sort that out later" answers about linens, overtime, or the bar minimum.
- No written end time, or a fuzzy one, which almost always leads to an overtime surprise.
- Verbal promises that never make it into the document you sign.

The pattern is the same across all of them: if a number is not in writing, it is not a number you can count on. A great caterer will happily put everything in writing. Reluctance is information.

## do this before you sign

- Confirm the service charge percentage (18-24%) and whether it includes any gratuity.
- Multiply per-guest fees like cake cutting ($2-6 a slice) by your actual headcount.
- Ask whether linens ($25-75 per table) are included and for which tables.
- Price BYO wine plus corkage ($15-35 per bottle) against buying through the caterer.
- Count your vendors and budget vendor meals ($25-50 per head).
- Get your exact end time and overtime rate ($150-500 per hour) in writing.
- Know your bar minimum and whether your crowd will realistically clear it.
