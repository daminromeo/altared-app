You tour a venue on a Tuesday evening, fall a little in love, and leave with a bar package quote in your inbox. Page one says something clean and reasonable, maybe "$45 per person, open bar, four hours." You do the math on your 120 guests, land on a number, and mentally file the bar as "handled."

Then you actually read clause four at midnight three weeks later, and the number you filed away turns out to be the beginning of the sentence, not the end of it.

Here is the thing nobody warns you about: the bar quote is almost never the bar total. The pretty figure on page one is a starting line. The real cost lives in five other places, and most couples don't find them until they're staring at a final invoice that's hundreds (sometimes thousands) higher than the version they budgeted for. None of it is hidden maliciously. It's just buried, and it adds up fast.

Let's walk through the five bar costs that quietly inflate a reception budget, using the dollar ranges you'll actually see in real contracts.

## 1. Per-person pricing is charged on your whole guest count

Per-person bar pricing runs **$30-85 a head**, and here's the part that catches people: it's charged on your full guest count, not just the people who drink.

That means your teetotaler aunt, your pregnant maid of honor, your friend who's a year sober, and the three kids at the family table all get billed at the same per-head rate as your group of college roommates doing tequila shots. Venues price this way because it's simpler for them to sell and impossible for you to audit on the night. You can't exactly hand out wristbands to "verified drinkers."

Run the math on a 120-guest wedding:

1. At the low end, $30 per head x 120 guests = $3,600
2. At the high end, $85 per head x 120 guests = $10,200

That's a $6,600 spread on the exact same headcount, driven entirely by which tier you pick. And if 25 of those guests barely touch the bar, you're still paying full freight for all 120.

### What to ask before you sign

Ask whether the venue offers consumption-based pricing (you pay for what's actually poured) as an alternative to per-person. For a crowd of light drinkers, consumption can come in well under a flat per-head rate. For a crowd that drinks hard, per-person can actually protect you from a runaway tab. Neither is "better." It depends on your guest list, and you can only choose well if they show you both numbers.

## 2. Corkage fees turn "bringing your own wine" into a line item

Bringing your own wine feels like the obvious money-saver, and sometimes it is. But most venues charge a corkage fee of **$15-35 per bottle** for the privilege of opening and serving something you supplied.

That fee covers the glassware, the pouring, and the venue's decision to let outside alcohol through the door at all. On a couple of bottles it's nothing. On a real reception it adds fast.

Say you bring 20 bottles of wine for dinner service:

- At $15 per bottle, corkage is $300
- At $35 per bottle, corkage is $700

So the wine you bought "to save money" now has a $300-700 tax on top of the retail price you already paid. That can still be worth it if you're bringing wine you got at a serious discount or received as a gift. It's rarely worth it if you're buying mid-range bottles at retail and then paying $35 each to have them opened. Do the comparison honestly before you assume BYO wins.

## 3. The service charge lands on top of everything (and it's not the tip)

This is the one that surprises couples the most. A bar service charge of **18-22%** gets added to the whole bar tab, and it is not gratuity. Read that twice, because a lot of people assume the service charge is the tip and end up paying twice.

The service charge is the venue's administrative and staffing fee. It applies to the entire bar total, including the per-person pricing and often the corkage. And because it's a percentage of everything else, it grows every time another cost stacks on.

Here's how it compounds on that 120-guest example at, say, $45 per head:

1. Base bar: $45 x 120 = $5,400
2. Add 20% service charge: $5,400 x 0.20 = $1,080
3. Running total before anything else: $6,480

That $1,080 didn't appear on page one. It's a real, contractual line that most couples read past. And critically, in most contracts, it does not go to your bartenders as a tip. Which brings us to the fee that sits in its own separate line entirely.

## 4. Overtime bills in full hours, never halves

Your reception runs long. Of course it does. The dance floor finally fills up around the time your original bar package was supposed to close, and you decide to keep it going.

Going even one hour late adds **$200-500 more**, and here's the detail that stings: it's billed in full increments, never half. Stay 20 minutes past your window and you're charged for the entire extra hour. There's no prorating your way to a smaller number at 11:40pm.

If your night runs two hours long at the high end, that's $1,000 on top of everything above, decided in the moment, at the exact time you're least likely to be doing arithmetic.

### How to keep overtime from ambushing you

- Build a realistic timeline before you sign, not an optimistic one. If your reception genuinely runs five hours, price a five-hour bar now instead of paying overtime rates later.
- Put the overtime rate in writing so a late decision doesn't turn into a mystery charge.
- Assign one person (a planner, a trusted friend, not you) to make the call on extending, so it's a decision and not an accident.

## 5. Bartender gratuity sits in a line most couples miss

Last one, and it's the sneakiest because it hides in plain sight next to that service charge you already assumed was the tip.

Bartender gratuity runs **$50-100 per bartender, per shift**, and most contracts spell out that it is not included. So after you've paid the 18-22% service charge, you're often still expected to tip the actual humans pouring drinks.

For a wedding with three bartenders working the night, that's:

- Low end: $50 x 3 = $150
- High end: $100 x 3 = $300

Not a budget-breaker on its own, but it's real money that lives on a separate line from every other cost, and it's the one couples forget when they tally the "real" bar number. Add it now so it's not a surprise cash ask on the wedding day.

## Watch for these red flags in the contract

When you're reviewing a bar package, these are the phrases and gaps that should make you slow down and ask questions:

1. **A per-person price with no note about consumption-based options.** If they only quote per-head, ask for the alternative before you assume it's the only way.
2. **"Service charge" and "gratuity" both appearing.** That's your signal you may be paying two separate things. Confirm which one (if either) reaches the bartenders.
3. **No stated corkage fee for BYO events.** Silence isn't free. Get the per-bottle number in writing before you buy a single case.
4. **Overtime described vaguely** ("additional hours billed accordingly"). Pin down the exact per-hour rate and whether partial hours round up.
5. **A quote that only shows page one.** If the number you were given doesn't add up to base + service charge + corkage + overtime allowance + gratuity, you're looking at the pretty number, not the real one.

The pattern across all five is the same: nothing is hidden to trick you. It's buried in a contract you're reading at midnight after a long venue tour, when your eyes glaze somewhere around clause three. Knowing these five lines exist means you can go in and find them on purpose.

## The real number, before you sign

Here's a fast recap of the five costs to check on every bar quote:

1. **Per-person pricing** ($30-85 per head) charged on your full guest count, not just the drinkers
2. **Corkage** ($15-35 per bottle) that stacks fast on 20 bottles of your own wine
3. **Service charge** (18-22%) applied to the whole tab, separate from any tip
4. **Overtime** ($200-500 per extra hour) billed in full increments, never half
5. **Bartender gratuity** ($50-100 per bartender, per shift) that most contracts say is not included

Add those five lines together and you get the number you should actually be budgeting against, not the one on page one.
