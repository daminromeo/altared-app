You get the invitation, you RSVP yes, and then three weeks before the wedding the group chat lights up: "wait, how much are we actually giving?" Someone says $50. Someone says "isn't it supposed to cover your plate now?" Someone else has already spent $180 on a stand mixer and is quietly panicking. Everyone is guessing, and nobody wants to be the person who underpaid or the person who blew their rent money on a coworker's reception.

Here is the thing about the "how much do I give?" spiral: it ends the second you stop guessing and start using rules. There are four of them, they are simple, and they hold up for every wedding on your calendar this year. Save this before you write the check.

## rule 1: start at what your seat costs

Your seat at a wedding is not free. Somebody paid for the chair you are sitting in, the plate in front of you, the drink in your hand, and the slice of cake you may or may not eat. In 2026, per-seat cost runs $150 to $200 at most venues. That number is your baseline.

Notice the word baseline, not ceiling. "Cover your plate" is a starting point that keeps you from accidentally lowballing, not a hard rule you have to hit to the dollar. If you are a broke grad student invited to a $200-a-head vineyard wedding, nobody reasonable expects you to match that. And if you are a well-established family member, $150 might be the floor, not the goal.

Use the seat cost as a gut check. If your gift is way under what it costs to host you, ask whether that is a budget decision (fine) or an accident (fixable). If you have no idea what the venue cost, you can usually estimate from the vibe:

1. Casual backyard or restaurant: closer to the $150 end.
2. Hotel ballroom or popular event space: right around $150 to $200.
3. Destination, vineyard, or luxury venue: at or above the $200 mark.

You are not solving for the exact catering invoice. You are making sure your number is in the neighborhood of the effort and money the couple put into having you there.

## rule 2: layer in your relationship

Once you have the baseline, adjust for how close you actually are to the couple. This is the tier that people overthink the most, so here is the clean version:

- **Coworker or acquaintance:** $50 to $75.
- **Close friend:** $100 to $150.
- **Family:** $150 to $200 and up.

These ranges do a lot of quiet work. The coworker you like but do not text on weekends does not need a $200 gift, and giving one can even feel like a strange amount of intensity for the relationship. A close friend sits comfortably in the middle. Family, especially immediate family or the people who watched you grow up, lands at the top and often above it.

### what if you're in the wedding party?

Being a bridesmaid or groomsman changes the math because you have already spent real money getting there: the outfit, the shower, the bachelorette weekend, the hair and makeup. If you have poured hundreds into being part of the day, it is completely reasonable to give a gift at the lower end of your relationship tier. Your presence and your labor are part of the gift.

### plus-ones and group gifts

Bringing a date? Nudge your number up, since that is a second seat the couple paid for. Going in on a group gift with friends? Totally valid, and it is how a lot of people afford the nicer registry items. Just make sure the per-person share still feels right for your individual relationship to the couple.

## rule 3: your own budget is a rule too

This is the rule people skip, and it is the one that saves the most heartache. A gift you cannot afford helps no one. $50 on time beats $200 late, every single time.

Read that again, because the wedding-industrial complex will try to make you feel otherwise. There is enormous pressure to perform generosity, to hit a number that signals you love the couple "enough." But a couple who is worth celebrating is not sitting there with a spreadsheet judging your contribution against their catering bill. What they will remember is that you showed up, that you were there, and that you sent something thoughtful without imploding your finances to do it.

So set your gift budget before you look at the registry, not after. If you have three weddings this summer, decide what you can spend across all three, then divide. If money is genuinely tight, a smaller cash gift with a real, handwritten card is more meaningful than a maxed-out credit card and a gift you resent. Guests who overextend on wedding gifts are the same guests who feel weird about the whole event afterward, and that is nobody's goal.

If you are the one getting married and reading this from the other side, this rule applies to you too: knowing your real numbers is what keeps your own budget honest. Our [budgeting guides](/blog/category/budgeting) are built for exactly that kind of clarity.

## rule 4: cash and registry items win

Here is the rule people feel most awkward about, so let's just say it plainly: cash and registry items win. 67% of couples say they'd rather have that than something off-list.

That off-list guess, the "I found this gorgeous artisan cheese board and just knew they'd love it," is where good intentions go to die. The couple probably already registered for the exact cheese board they wanted, in the exact color that matches their kitchen. Your surprise version becomes a return trip, a re-gift, or a shelf item they keep out of guilt. Skip the off-list guess.

Cash is not tacky. In 2026 it is the most requested, most useful gift a couple can get, because it goes toward the honeymoon, the down payment, or the wedding debt they are still paying off. If cash feels impersonal to you, that is what the card is for. The card matters. The amount matters. The guess does not.

### how to actually give it

1. Check the registry first. Buy something on it, in your budget range, and let the couple's stated preferences do the work.
2. If the registry is picked over or you would rather give cash, give cash or a digital transfer to their honeymoon or cash fund.
3. Write a real card. Not "congrats!" Write the specific thing you are excited for them about. This is the part they keep.
4. If you are shipping a physical gift, send it to the address on the registry, not the venue. Nobody wants to haul boxes home from a reception.

## watch for these red flags

A few things quietly derail otherwise reasonable gift decisions. Watch for:

- **Treating "cover your plate" as a mandate.** It is a baseline, not a bill. If you cannot afford the seat cost, give what you can and let rule 3 win.
- **Buying off-registry to seem thoughtful.** The thought lands better as a card plus something they actually asked for.
- **Forgetting the plus-one.** Two seats, slightly bigger gift. It is not complicated, but it is easy to overlook.
- **Ignoring what you already spent.** Wedding party members and destination-wedding guests have already contributed a lot. Do not double-punish your bank account out of guilt.
- **Waiting too long.** Etiquette gives you a window (many people say up to a year), but do not let the gift become a source of anxiety for months. On time and reasonable beats late and lavish.
- **Sending cash to the venue.** Envelopes get lost in the chaos of a reception. Use the registry address or a digital fund.

## the quick version to save

If you only remember one thing, remember that the guess is the enemy. Every one of these rules exists to replace a guess with a decision:

1. **Start at seat cost.** $150 to $200 per person at most 2026 venues is your baseline, not your ceiling.
2. **Adjust for relationship.** Coworker $50 to $75, close friend $100 to $150, family $150 to $200 and up.
3. **Respect your own budget.** A gift you can afford, on time, always beats one you cannot.
4. **Give cash or registry.** 67% of couples prefer it. Write the card. Skip the off-list guess.
