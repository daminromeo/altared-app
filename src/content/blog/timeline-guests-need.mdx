say you finish your timeline at 11pm, feel great about it, and send the whole thing to your guest list. by the next morning you have four texts: your aunt wants to know if she should be at the venue for the 2:15 "first look block," your college roommate is asking whether the 5:40 "buffer" means dinner is late, and someone's partner wants to know if they're supposed to be in the 3:00 family photo call. none of those people did anything wrong. you just handed them a document that was never written for them.

the guest timeline and the vendor timeline are two different documents. one is for the people running your day. one is for the people attending it. when you merge them, you get questions, not confidence.

## why two documents, not one

your vendor timeline exists to prevent collisions. it tracks the caterer load-in, the DJ sound check window, the photo call blocks, the buffer gaps you built in because you know the shuttle will be late. it is long, it is granular, and it is meant to be read by people who are being paid to read it carefully: your coordinator, your photographer, your catering lead, your band or DJ.

your guest timeline has one job: make it easy for someone to show up in the right place, at the right time, dressed correctly, and get home safely. that is it. every extra line you add is a line someone can misread.

guests do not need your full vendor schedule. they need five things: when doors open, where to park, where cocktail hour is, what time it ends, and who to text if something goes sideways. the sixth thing on this list is the discipline to cut everything else.

## the 6 things guests actually need

### 1. doors open (and a seated-by time)

give guests a seated-by time, not just ceremony start. "ceremony at 4:30" means different things to different people. some will read it as "arrive at 4:30." some will read it as "be in a seat by 4:20." one of them will be walking in behind you.

write it the way you would say it out loud: "doors open 4:00, seated by 4:30."

that single phrasing does more work than a paragraph of instructions. it tells people the earliest they can arrive, the latest they can arrive, and implies that the ceremony starts promptly after.

### 2. parking notes that name the actual lot

name the lot or street, not just the venue address. a venue address drops people at a pin. it does not tell them that the hotel lot is permit-only after 4pm, or that the main lot fills with another event's guests, or that there is free street parking one block over.

"park on elm street, not the hotel lot."

if there is a shuttle, say where it picks up and the last departure. if there is valet, say whether it is cash only. if there is a fee, write the exact amount your venue or lot quoted you, and if you do not have that number in writing yet, ask for it before you print anything.

### 3. where cocktail hour is, immediately

tell guests where to go after the ceremony, immediately. the gap between "ceremony ends" and "everyone finds a drink" is where a wedding loses its energy. people linger in the aisle, nobody wants to be the first to leave, and your photographer loses light while a hundred people figure out which door to use.

"head to the courtyard, drinks are ready."

name the room. name the direction. if cocktail hour is in a separate building or up a flight of stairs, say that too, because someone in your group is wearing heels on grass or using a mobility aid and deserves to know before they are standing in it.

### 4. the last dance, meaning the hard-out

give the hard-out time so guests arrange rides ahead. this is the line couples most often leave off, and it is the one that causes the most chaos at the end of the night. venues have hard-out times. rideshare availability changes by the hour in a lot of areas. if your guests do not know when the music stops, they find out when it stops.

"we end at 10:30, plan your ride by 10:15."

that phrasing gives them a cue to act on, not just a fact to file away. if there is an after-party, put the address and start time here too, and make it clear whether it is open to everyone or a smaller group.

### 5. a point person who is not you

list the coordinator or day-of contact name and number. not yours. not your partner's. you will not be checking your phone, and you should not be.

"questions on the day? text jamie: 555-0190."

a name plus a number is the whole line. tell that person they are on the card, tell them what kinds of questions to expect (parking, late arrivals, dietary stuff, lost guests), and give them a copy of the vendor timeline so they can answer from the real document. if you are hiring a day-of coordinator, confirm in writing that guest-facing contact is part of the scope. more on that in [contracts](/blog/category/contracts).

### 6. the cut

leave off your internal vendor beats, buffer gaps, and photo call times. guests do not need those.

specifically, cut:

1. caterer and rental load-in windows
2. DJ or band sound check
3. hair and makeup schedule
4. first look, getting-ready, and detail photo blocks
5. family and wedding party photo call times
6. the buffer gaps you padded between every block
7. vendor meal times
8. teardown and load-out

anything a guest cannot act on is noise. if they cannot show up to it, skip it, or be affected by it, it belongs on the internal document only.

## watch for these red flags before you send

- **your guest timeline runs more than a short card or one screen.** if it scrolls, you have included vendor beats. cut again.
- **it lists a time with no instruction.** "5:45 dinner service" is a vendor line. "dinner is served at 5:45, find your table card in the foyer" is a guest line.
- **the contact number on it is yours.** replace it.
- **it promises a time you have not confirmed with the venue.** check your contract for the hard-out before you print "we end at 10:30" on anything.
- **you are sending one PDF to vendors and guests to save time.** this is the original mistake wearing a disguise.
- **it includes a figure you estimated.** parking fees, shuttle costs, after-party covers: use the number your vendor gave you in writing, or leave it off and tell people to ask.

## how to actually write it

write two documents. one for your vendors and coordinator, one for your guests. short, specific, and friendly.

the guest version lives in two places: your wedding website and a small card in the invitation suite or welcome bag. keep the wording identical across both so nobody is comparing versions. the vendor version lives with your coordinator and goes out to every vendor in your final two weeks, along with the contact sheet.

if you want the tone right, read each guest line out loud. if it sounds like something you would text a friend, it is ready. if it sounds like a project plan, rewrite it.

## the short version

1. doors open and a seated-by time, in one sentence
2. the actual lot or street to park on
3. where cocktail hour is, named by room
4. the hard-out time, with a ride cue fifteen minutes earlier
5. a point person's name and number, not yours
6. nothing else from your internal timeline

giving guests too much information creates questions, not confidence. write the short version, confirm the times against your [contracts](/blog/category/contracts), and hand the long one to your coordinator.
