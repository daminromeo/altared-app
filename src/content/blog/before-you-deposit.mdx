You are three vendors deep into booking. The photographer you loved just sent a contract, the deposit is due in 48 hours to "hold your date," and the whole thing feels like a formality. You skim, you sign, you send $1,000. It is only in month seven, when your reception runs long and you get a surprise line item, that you find out what you actually agreed to.

Here is the thing: the deposit is the moment you have the most leverage and use it the least. Before that money leaves your account, you are a prospect they want to win. After it clears, you are a booked client and the conversation changes. So this is the window to ask the uncomfortable questions.

None of these six are unreasonable to ask. The vendors who are worth booking will answer every one without hesitation. The ones who get cagey, deflect, or say "we'll figure that out later" are telling you something too. Save this for your next vendor call.

## 1. What is the refund policy, both ways?

Most deposits are non-refundable. That part is normal and fair. A good vendor turns down other bookings to hold your date, so they keep the deposit if you walk. Fine.

What you actually need to know is the asymmetry. Ask what percentage you keep if YOU cancel versus what happens if THEY cancel. Those are two different clauses, and a lot of contracts only spell out the first one clearly.

Run through these scenarios out loud with them:

1. You cancel four months out. How much of your deposit, if any, comes back?
2. You cancel two weeks out. Same question.
3. They cancel on you (illness, double-booking, going out of business). Do you get a full refund, and do they help you find a replacement?
4. You move your date instead of canceling. Does the deposit transfer, and is there a rebooking fee?

A contract that punishes you heavily for canceling but only offers "we'll refund your deposit" if the vendor bails is lopsided. At minimum, a vendor who cancels on you should return everything you paid. The strong ones go further and help you rebook someone comparable.

## 2. Is the service charge a tip, or is gratuity expected on top?

This is the one that quietly wrecks budgets. A service charge of 18-25% is standard, and it is not a tip. It often covers staffing, overhead, and administration, and in a lot of contracts none of it reaches the person actually serving your guests.

So ask directly: does the 18-25% service charge already cover gratuity, or are tips expected on top?

Play it out with real numbers. Say your catering total is $12,000. A 20% service charge is $2,400. If you assume that money is the tip and it is not, you might add another 15-20% in cash envelopes on the wedding day. That is another $1,800 or so you never planned for. Now you are $4,200 over the food number you thought you had locked.

There is no universal rule here, which is exactly why you ask. Some vendors fold gratuity in. Some do not. Some leave it optional. Get the answer in writing so you can budget the real all-in number instead of the sticker price. If you want a fuller breakdown of the fees that hide behind a "total," our [hidden costs](/blog/category/hidden-costs) posts go deeper.

## 3. What is the overtime rate, in writing?

Weddings run long. The toasts go over, nobody wants to leave the dance floor, and suddenly your photographer's contracted end time was 20 minutes ago.

Most vendors bill $150-300/hr past your contracted end time. That applies to photographers, videographers, DJs, coordinators, and often catering staff. The problem is not the rate itself, it is finding out about it at 10:47pm when someone quietly asks if you'd like them to stay.

Get the overtime rate in writing before you sign. Then ask two follow-ups:

1. Is overtime billed in full-hour blocks or by the fraction? A 15-minute overrun charged as a full $300 hour is a very different deal than a prorated one.
2. How do you approve it in the moment? You do not want your maid of honor authorizing $600 in extra time on your behalf because nobody defined who says yes.

### Do the math before the day

If there is any chance you run over, price it now. Two extra hours of a photographer at $250/hr is $500. Knowing that in advance means you either build it into the budget or set a hard end time you actually enforce.

## 4. What are the travel fees, and where does the free radius end?

Vendors charge $0.67/mile or flat travel day rates once you go past their included service area. That per-mile number sounds tiny. It is not.

Say your venue is 60 miles outside a vendor's free radius. At $0.67/mile, round trip, that is roughly $80. Now imagine they need to arrive the day before for setup, so it is two round trips, plus a flat "travel day" rate on top. You are suddenly looking at a few hundred dollars that never appeared in the headline quote.

Ask two specific things:

1. What is the free radius, measured from where? (Their studio? A city center? It matters.)
2. What is the exact overage cost past that radius, per mile or as a flat rate, and does it include lodging for out-of-town or multi-day events?

Destination and out-of-region weddings are where this stacks up fast. If your venue is remote, put travel in the budget as its own line, not an afterthought.

## 5. Can a substitute show up, and do you get to approve them?

You are not just booking a company. You are often booking a specific person, the photographer whose portfolio made you cry or the DJ whose energy sold you.

So ask: if the person you booked gets sick or has an emergency, can they send a substitute? And critically, do you get to approve who shows up?

There is a real difference between "we guarantee a qualified replacement from our team, subject to your approval" and "the studio reserves the right to assign any available photographer." The first respects that you chose a person. The second means you could get a stranger whose style you have never seen, and you have no say.

This matters most with solo creatives and boutique studios. Ask what their actual backup plan is. A vendor with a real answer (a named associate, a network they trust, a refund option if you decline the sub) has thought about the day you hope never happens. That is a good sign.

## 6. Is the quote locked, or can it creep up?

You budgeted around a number. You assumed that number was the number. But some contracts allow the price to creep up by as much as 10% between signing and your wedding day, citing rising costs, fuel, staffing, or "market adjustments."

On a $12,000 catering contract, a 10% increase is $1,200 you did not agree to spend. Multiply a few of those across your vendor roster and it reshapes the whole budget.

Ask flat out: is the quote I got actually locked, or can pricing change before the wedding? If it can change, ask for the cap in writing and the conditions that trigger it. Better yet, negotiate a locked price as a condition of your deposit. A vendor confident in their numbers can usually hold them.

## Red flags to watch for

While you ask the six, pay attention to how they answer, not just what they say.

- **Vague verbal promises.** "Don't worry, we're flexible on that" means nothing unless it is written into the contract. If it is not on paper, it does not exist.
- **Pressure to sign fast.** "This rate expires tonight" or "someone else is asking about your date" is a tactic. A date hold does not require you to skip reading the contract.
- **Fees that only appear after the deposit.** If travel, overtime, or service charges are missing from the quote and only surface later, that is a pattern, not an oversight.
- **Irritation at your questions.** A vendor who gets defensive when you ask about refunds or subs is showing you how they'll handle problems on the day.
- **A one-sided cancellation clause.** Heavy penalties on you, near silence on what happens if they cancel.

None of these six questions are rude. They are the baseline. The right vendor answers them and probably respects you more for asking.

## Before you sign, run this list

1. **Refund policy** both ways: what you keep if you cancel versus what you get if they cancel.
2. **Service charge**: confirm whether the 18-25% covers gratuity or whether tips are expected on top.
3. **Overtime rate**: get the $150-300/hr number and the billing increments in writing.
4. **Travel fees**: know the free radius and the exact overage at $0.67/mile or the flat day rate.
5. **Substitute clause**: can they send someone else, and do you approve who shows up?
6. **Price lock**: confirm the quote is locked, or cap any increase (some allow up to 10%).
