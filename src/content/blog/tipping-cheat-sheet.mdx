It's usually a Tuesday. The wedding is Saturday. Someone in the group chat (a mom, a maid of honor, a very organized cousin) asks the question that has been quietly sitting in everyone's brain for eight months: "wait, how much cash do we need for tips?"

And then nobody has a clean answer. One person says 20% of everything. Another says you never tip a business owner. Someone Googles it and finds a listicle that says "tip generously!" without a single number. Meanwhile you are trying to remember whether the catering invoice already had a service charge on it, and the contract is a PDF buried in an email thread from March.

This is the tipping question no one answers clearly until the week of the wedding. Here is the actual breakdown, vendor by vendor, plus the one rule that decides most of it before you count a single twenty.

## the rule that answers 80% of it: read the contract

Before you decide who gets an envelope, go find every signed vendor agreement and search for these words: gratuity, service charge, administrative fee, staffing fee.

That search does most of the work for you.

If a service charge is already in there, you are not obligated to add more. That is not being cheap. That is reading what you signed. The problem is that "service charge" is a slippery term, and the two versions of it mean completely different things:

1. **A service charge that goes to the team.** The 18-25% line on your catering invoice is distributed to servers, bartenders, and back-of-house as gratuity. In that case, the tip is handled. You are done.
2. **A service charge that goes to the house.** The same 18-25% covers overhead, insurance, admin, and the venue's operating costs. The staff see none of it directly beyond their hourly wage. If it goes to the house, not the team, that is a different story, and an additional cash tip for the people who actually worked your reception is a reasonable call.

You cannot tell which one you have by reading the percentage. You have to ask. One email, sent in month three instead of on Tuesday of wedding week: "Does the 20% service charge on our contract go to staff as gratuity, or is it an administrative fee? We want to budget correctly for day-of tips."

Every good vendor answers that question without flinching. How they answer tells you a lot.

## 1. your caterer

Check the contract first. An 18-25% service charge usually covers the team. Tip only if it's not going to staff wages, or if a specific person went noticeably beyond what you paid for.

On a $12,000 catering bill, a 20% service charge is $2,400. That is already a significant gratuity pool. Adding another 15% on top because you feel guilty in the moment is $1,800 you did not plan for, on a night when nobody is thinking clearly about money.

If you do want to recognize individuals, the common move is a smaller targeted tip: the banquet captain or maitre d' who ran your floor, or the two bartenders who handled a 150-person cocktail hour without a line. $50-100 each, handed over directly, is meaningful and doesn't blow up your budget.

### what about the chef?

If the chef is also the business owner, tipping is optional and often gently declined. If the chef is staff, a tip passed through the captain is fine. Ask who is who when you ask about the service charge. It's the same email.

## 2. photographer and second shooter

Not required, but $50-200 per shooter is standard.

The detail people get wrong: the second shooter gets the same tip, not half. They worked the same twelve hours, carried the same gear, and shot your entire getting-ready timeline while the lead was with the other partner. Splitting one tip between them turns a nice gesture into an awkward one.

So if you tip $150 each and you have a lead plus a second shooter, that is $300 total. Budget the full amount, not the lead's amount.

Owner-operators are the gray zone here. A solo photographer who runs their own studio sets their own rates and is not expecting a tip. Plenty of couples still give one because the person spent fourteen hours with them and got the shot of their grandfather crying. It's a thank-you, not an obligation. Same logic applies to videographers, who often get overlooked entirely despite being on the same schedule as the photo team.

## 3. hair and makeup

Tip like a salon visit: 15-20% per artist.

This is the most straightforward category because you already know how it works. You are not deciding whether to tip. You are deciding the percentage.

If two artists split the work, tip both separately. A common setup is one artist on hair and one on makeup, each working through six people. If your hair total is $600 and your makeup total is $700, you are looking at roughly $120 on hair and $140 on makeup at 20%, handed to each artist directly.

### who pays for the bridal party's tips?

Decide this early and say it out loud. If your people are paying for their own hair and makeup, they tip on their own services. If you are covering everyone, you are covering the tips too. The awkward version is the one where nobody clarified and four bridesmaids all hand cash to the same artist while you also hand over an envelope.

## 4. band or dj

$25-50 per musician is common. The DJ gets $50-150.

For a band, this adds up faster than people expect. A seven-piece at $40 each is $280. Ask the band leader how tips are handled, because the band leader collects and splits, and the split isn't always even. Some leaders take a larger share, some distribute flat, some pass everything to the players and take nothing. Ask how.

Also ask about sound techs and roadies. A band that brings its own engineer often expects that person to be included in the tip pool, and they are usually the first to arrive and the last to leave.

For a DJ, $50-150 depends on scope. A DJ who played four hours and left is at the lower end. A DJ who also MC'd your ceremony, ran the mics for four toasts, and rebuilt the last hour on the fly when the dance floor went a direction nobody predicted is at the top end.

## 5. venue coordinator

Venue staff are usually salaried. Tipping is optional, and $50-150 if they went well above the contract.

Worth being precise about titles here, because "coordinator" means three different jobs:

- **Venue coordinator.** Works for the venue. Salaried. Their job is the building, the timeline for the space, and the vendor load-in. Tipping optional.
- **Day-of or month-of coordinator.** You hired them. If they're an employee of a planning company, $50-150 is a normal thank-you. If they own the company, they set their price.
- **Full-service planner.** Same logic. Owners set their rates. Employees who worked a fifteen-hour day are worth an envelope.

If your venue coordinator personally fixed a problem you would have never known about (a vendor who showed up two hours late, a rain call made at 3pm that saved your ceremony), that is exactly the "well above the contract" scenario the $50-150 range exists for.

## 6. officiant

Clergy officiants rarely accept tips. A donation to their institution is the right move instead.

Many houses of worship have a set fee or a suggested donation range, and the officiant personally may not be allowed to accept cash. Ask what the standard is. If there isn't one, a donation in the range of the other honoraria you're paying is appropriate, written as a check to the institution rather than the person.

A secular or independent officiant is a different case. They quote a fee, they are running a business, and a tip of $50-100 is a nice gesture if they wrote a custom ceremony and came to your rehearsal. Not required.

## red flags to watch for in the contract

A few things that should make you slow down and ask a question before you sign:

- **A service charge with no explanation of where it goes.** "20% service charge" as a standalone line with no definition anywhere in the document. Ask, in writing, and keep the answer.
- **Both a "service charge" and a "gratuity" line.** Sometimes legitimate (one is admin, one is staff). Sometimes double-dipping. Make them explain both.
- **"Gratuity at client's discretion" plus a mandatory service fee.** This is a soft ask for a second tip on top of a fee you already paid. You are allowed to say no.
- **A tip line on the final invoice you never discussed.** Pre-filled suggested gratuity on a five-figure balance is a big number appearing in a moment when you are unlikely to argue.
- **Vendors who won't put the answer in writing.** A verbal "oh, that all goes to the staff" is worth very little in April when the invoice looks different.

None of these automatically mean a vendor is dishonest. They usually mean the contract is sloppy. But a sloppy contract is exactly what creates a $2,000 surprise during the week you have the least bandwidth to deal with it. ## the quick version

1. Search every contract for "service charge," "gratuity," and "administrative fee" before you decide anything.
2. Caterer: an 18-25% service charge usually covers the team. Confirm whether it goes to staff or to the house.
3. Photographer: $50-200 per shooter. The second shooter gets the same tip, not half.
4. Hair and makeup: 15-20% per artist, tipped separately if two artists split the work.
5. Band: $25-50 per musician, and ask the band leader how the split works. DJ: $50-150.
6. Venue coordinator: usually salaried, tipping optional, $50-150 if they went well above the contract.
7. Officiant: if they're clergy, a donation to their institution instead of cash.
