Nine days after her wedding, a bride we know opened a drawer looking for a phone charger and found the venue contract she'd printed in March. Page three, paragraph two: the $1,500 damage deposit would be refunded within 30 days of the event, "upon written request from the client." Written request. Nobody had mentioned it at the walkthrough, at the rehearsal, or in the five emails after. She sent one paragraph that afternoon and had the money back in eleven days.

That's the whole problem with the post-wedding window. The wedding is over, but the admin isn't. There's real money sitting in deposits, duplicate gifts, and decor you will never use again, and it is easy to miss because nobody tells you the clock is ticking.

Most of these windows are 14 to 30 days. Some are shorter. You will be jet-lagged, behind on work, and writing thank-you notes. So here is the list, in order of how much money is usually on the table, built so you can work through it in about two hours total.

## 01. Security deposits: email every vendor on day one

This is the biggest and the most commonly forgotten. Most contracts release them 14-30 days after the event. Email each vendor day one.

Security and damage deposits show up in more places than couples expect:

1. **The venue.** Damage deposit, cleaning deposit, or both. Often the largest single refund you're owed.
2. **Rental companies.** Tables, chairs, linens, glassware, lounge furniture. Damage deposits here are usually a flat amount or a percentage of the rental total.
3. **Tent and lighting companies.** Frequently separate from the main rental vendor, with their own deposit.
4. **Bar and beverage services.** Glassware deposits, keg deposits, and sometimes a separate cleanup deposit.
5. **Transportation.** Shuttles and party buses often take a damage hold that gets released after the driver reports back.
6. **The city or park district**, if you used a public space, a permit, or a street closure.

The language in your contract matters. Some vendors refund automatically. Many require you to ask, like the "upon written request" clause above. If you don't know which yours is, assume you have to ask. The email takes 90 seconds:

> Hi [name], thanks again for everything on [date]. I'm following up on the $[amount] security deposit listed in our contract. Could you confirm the refund timeline and the method it'll be returned (card, check, or transfer)? Happy to provide anything you need on my end.

Send that to every vendor on the list, from the same thread, the first weekday after the wedding. Then put a calendar reminder for day 31. If the money hasn't appeared, reply in the same thread with the contract clause pasted in. A paper trail in one place is worth more than three phone calls.

### What makes a deposit get withheld

Be honest with yourself before you send the email. Deposits get kept for wax on linens, confetti or glitter that wasn't allowed, a late teardown that pushed the venue into overtime, missing rental items (that one chair nobody can find), and damage from sparklers or candles. If you know something happened, ask what the deduction will be rather than waiting to be surprised. Vendors are much more flexible with couples who bring it up first.

## 02. Unopened bottles: ask before you buy, not after

If you bought your own alcohol, you are probably sitting on a stack of unopened cases in a garage. Some stores take back unopened bottles, if state law allows it. Ask before you buy.

That caveat is doing a lot of work. Alcohol return rules are set at the state level, and plenty of states prohibit retailers from accepting any returned alcohol at all. Where returns are allowed, the store usually still sets its own policy: original receipt, unopened and undamaged, full unbroken cases, within a set number of days, and often only for store credit.

Which is exactly why this is a *before you book* item, not an after item. When you're buying, ask the store two questions and write the answers in your notes:

1. Do you accept returns on unopened wine and spirits, and what does your state allow?
2. What's the window, and does it need to be full sealed cases or will you take individual bottles?

Some retailers run a consignment-style program for events where you buy generously, return what you don't open, and only pay for what was consumed. If that's available in your area, it can change your bar budget significantly, because you can overbuy safely instead of running dry at 10pm.

If returns aren't an option where you live, the leftovers aren't wasted. Keep what you actually like, send bottles home with the wedding party as thank-yous, or save them for your first anniversary and the holidays. You already paid retail. Don't pay for storage anxiety on top of it.

## 03. Duplicate gifts: every store has its own clock

Registry duplicates can usually go back for store credit. Check each return window.

You will get two of the same stand mixer. You will get a set of towels in a color that doesn't match anything. And you will get a few off-registry gifts that are lovely and wrong for your apartment. All of this is normal and none of it requires guilt.

The practical details:

- **Most registries have extended return windows for weddings**, often longer than the store's standard policy, sometimes running months past the event date. Longer than standard does not mean unlimited. Look up the specific window for each store the week you get back.
- **You usually don't need a gift receipt** if the item was purchased off your registry, because the store already has it linked to your event.
- **Expect store credit, not cash.** That's fine. Use it for the things you didn't register for and now realize you need, which for most couples is storage, a better mattress, or replacing the one cheap pan you've had since college.
- **Off-registry gifts are harder.** No receipt usually means lowest recent sale price in credit, if the store accepts it at all.

Make one list of duplicates, sort it by store, and knock out one store per evening. It's genuinely an hour of work for a few hundred dollars in credit, and it clears the pile of boxes in your hallway.

### A note on etiquette

Returning a duplicate is not rude. Telling the person who gave it to you about the return is. Write the thank-you note for the gift as given, warmly and specifically, and leave the logistics out of it. If you want more on handling the awkward parts of gifts and guests, our [etiquette](/blog/category/etiquette) posts cover it.

## 04. Resell decor while other couples are still booking

Signs, vases, and arches resell in local wedding groups. List them while couples are booking.

Timing is the entire strategy here. Decor and dresses resell best while other couples are still booking, which means your buyers are the people planning weddings 6 to 18 months out. If you list in late fall for a fall wedding, you're selling into a slow market. If you list within two weeks of your wedding, you're selling into whoever is building their spring and summer plans right now.

What moves fastest:

1. **Arches, arbors, and backdrop frames.** Bulky to store, expensive new, and everyone needs one.
2. **Vases and candleholders in bulk.** Sell as a set with a photo of them all together, not as singles.
3. **Custom-free signage.** Welcome signs, bar menus, and seating charts that don't have your names on them. Acrylic sells better than wood.
4. **Linens and table runners**, if they're clean and you can say so honestly.
5. **Neon signs, letter boards, and card boxes.**

Price at roughly 40 to 60 percent of retail, post real photos (daylight, plain background, not just your professional wedding photos where the arch is covered in florals you're not including), and be clear about pickup only. Local wedding resale groups, neighborhood marketplaces, and campus listservs near you are where these actually sell. Shipping a seven-foot arch will eat your profit.

**Watch for these red flags when you sell:**

- Buyers who want you to ship an item you listed as local pickup, then send an "overpayment" and ask for the difference back. That's a scam, every time.
- Anyone pushing you off-platform to a payment app immediately, before they've asked a single question about the item.
- Requests to hold something for weeks with no deposit. Decor buyers are planning months out, so a hold is reasonable only with money attached.
- On the deposit side, a vendor who won't put the refund timeline in writing after the event when it was in the contract before it. Go back to the signed [contract](/blog/category/contracts) and quote it.

## 05. The dress: list it sooner than feels normal

Consignment and resale sites take wedding dresses, and the sooner you list, the more current it looks.

A dress from this season photographs as "current." The same dress two years later photographs as "a few seasons ago," and the price drops accordingly. If you already know you're not preserving it, the most expensive thing you can do is wait a year to decide.

Your options, roughly in order of effort:

1. **Resale marketplaces for wedding dresses.** You set the price, you handle shipping, you keep the most.
2. **Consignment shops.** They take a cut (often 40 to 50 percent) but they handle buyers, fittings, and cleaning standards.
3. **Local wedding groups.** Best for dresses under a certain price point where shipping and authentication aren't worth it.
4. **Donation.** Several organizations take gowns for military families, teen formals, or charitable resale. You're not getting cash, but you may be able to document it.

Get it cleaned first, keep the original receipt or order confirmation if you have it, and list with the designer name, style number, size as purchased, and every alteration that was made. Alterations are the thing buyers ask about most and the thing most listings leave out.

The same logic applies to suits bought rather than rented, bridesmaid dresses still in bags, and shoes worn for four hours.

## Your two-hour post-wedding money list

Do this in the first week back, before honeymoon brain fully sets in:

1. Pull every signed contract and highlight the refund clause and the number of days.
2. Email every vendor about the security deposit on day one, with the amount and the contract language quoted.
3. Call the liquor store about returning unopened bottles, and check what your state allows.
4. Sort duplicate gifts by store, look up each return window, and do one store per night.
5. Photograph and list decor this week, while other couples are still booking.
6. Decide on the dress now. Clean it, list it, or donate it, but don't let it sit for a year.
7. Set a calendar reminder for day 31 to chase anything that hasn't landed.
