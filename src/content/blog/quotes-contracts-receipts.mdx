say it is a Tuesday in month three of planning. one vendor sends the contract as a pdf attachment. another emails a link. a third texts a quote screenshot. and suddenly you have four browser tabs, two inboxes and a camera roll full of documents you can only hope you can find again when you need them.

nothing has gone wrong yet. that is the tricky part. the disorganisation does not hurt on the day it happens, it hurts eight months later when the caterer asks about the balance due and you cannot remember whether the number you are thinking of included service charge, or whether that was the quote from the vendor you did not book.

wedding contracts deserve a system before something goes missing. here is the one that works, in three places.

## why loose documents cost you more than time

a wedding is maybe a dozen vendor relationships running in parallel, each with its own paperwork rhythm. a quote, then a revised quote, then a contract, then a deposit receipt, then an invoice, then a final payment, then (hopefully) a receipt for that too. multiply that by a dozen and you are managing a filing problem, not a memory problem.

when documents live in scattered places, three specific things go wrong:

1. **you lose leverage.** if you cannot find the original quote, you cannot point to what was promised when the final invoice looks different.
2. **you miss due dates.** deposits and balances are scattered across pdfs you have not opened in months, which means the date arrives before you do.
3. **you become the bottleneck.** if everything is in your inbox, nobody else can answer a vendor question, chase a receipt or confirm an amount without going through you.

that last one matters more than people expect. the system you build is not only for you.

## 01. a shared cloud folder, named by vendor

one shared google drive folder, named by vendor, so your partner can pull anything too.

the structure is deliberately dull. one parent folder called something obvious. inside it, one subfolder per vendor, named with the vendor's business name exactly as it appears on their emails, because that is how you will search for it later. inside each vendor folder goes everything: quote, contract, deposit receipt, invoices, final receipt, and any email thread you exported because it contained a promise that is not written into the contract.

### a naming habit that saves you

name files so they sort themselves. date first, then what the document is. a folder where everything reads `2026-03-14 contract` and `2026-04-02 deposit receipt` tells you the sequence of events at a glance, which is exactly what you need when a vendor says "as agreed in our last conversation."

### share it on day one, not later

the whole point is shared access. give your partner edit access, and consider giving read access to whoever your point person is on the day. if you set it up now you will not have to do an emergency handover at the worst possible moment.

### screenshots count

a texted quote is still a quote. screenshot it, drop it in the vendor folder, rename it with the date. if a vendor only ever communicates by text or dm, consider asking them to confirm the key numbers by email so you have something that is not sitting in a messaging app you might lose access to.

## 02. an email label that catches threads as they land

create a label called "wedding vendors" and filter every vendor thread into it on arrival.

a folder is where documents rest. a label is how you find the conversation around them, which is often where the actual agreement lives. the sentence "yes, that includes setup and breakdown" is rarely in the contract. it is in an email reply at 9pm.

set up the filter so it works without you:

1. create the label "wedding vendors."
2. add a filter rule for each vendor's email domain as you book them, applying the label automatically.
3. add a catch-all rule for keywords that show up in wedding threads, so new inquiries get labelled before you have added a specific rule.
4. do a five-minute pass through your existing inbox and apply the label retroactively to anything already there.
5. once a month, skim the label and move anything with an attachment into the right cloud folder.

the filter should run on arrival. if labelling is a thing you have to remember to do, it will stop happening in the busy stretch, which is precisely when it matters.

## 03. a quote tracker where the numbers live

add each quote manually so totals, deposits and due dates all live in one view.

this is the piece people skip, and it is the piece that actually does the work. a folder full of pdfs does not tell you what you have committed to. a label full of threads does not tell you what is due next week. you only get that by entering each quote manually, as a row, with the numbers pulled out of the document and into a view you can scan.

for each quote, record the vendor, what the quote covers, the total, the deposit, the deposit due date, the balance due date, and whether the total includes tax, service charge, travel and overtime. that last column is the one that prevents nasty surprises, and it is worth going back to the contract to answer it properly rather than guessing.

entering it manually feels like duplicated effort. it is not. reading a contract closely enough to pull its numbers out is how you catch the things you would otherwise skim past, and it leaves you with a single view of what you are actually spending instead of a vague feeling about it. if you want somewhere to do this that already has the fields, you can [add your own quotes free](/get-started) and keep every number in one view from the first inquiry to the final receipt.

## red flags to watch for in the paperwork

as you file and enter each document, a few things should slow you down:

- a quote and a contract with different totals, and no written explanation of the change.
- a contract with no payment schedule, or dates written as "30 days before" with no actual calendar date attached.
- a vendor who will only send a quote as a text or screenshot and resists putting it in an email.
- line items described vaguely ("packages as discussed") instead of specifically.
- totals that do not say whether tax, service charge, travel or overtime are included. ask for the all-in number in writing.
- cancellation and postponement terms that are missing entirely. if they are not in the document, they are not an agreement.
- a signed contract you never received a countersigned copy of.

none of these mean a vendor is acting in bad faith. most are ordinary admin gaps. but they are all easier to fix in month three than in the final month, and you will only spot them if you are handling each document once, properly, as it arrives. if contract language is the part that makes you uneasy, [our contracts posts](/blog/category/contracts) go deeper on what to ask for.

## make it openable on a bad day

you do not need a perfect system. you need one that your partner, your mum or your point person can also open on a bad day.

that is the real test. if you got sick the week of the wedding, could someone else find the florist's contract, see what was already paid and know what was still owed, without calling you? if yes, you are done. if no, the fix is usually access rather than more structure.

### set it up this week

- make one shared cloud folder, with a subfolder per vendor, and give your partner edit access today.
- create the "wedding vendors" email label and a filter that applies it on arrival.
- back-label the vendor emails already sitting in your inbox.
- enter every quote you have so far into one tracker, with total, deposit and due dates.
- note for each quote whether the total includes tax, service charge, travel and overtime, and ask in writing where it is unclear.
- book a fifteen-minute monthly tidy to file attachments and update the tracker.

save this and set it up this week. the system is boring and it works, which is the only review a wedding admin system ever needs.
