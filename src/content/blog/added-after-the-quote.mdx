You got the quote. It was a clean, round number, the kind you can say out loud to your partner without wincing. You signed. Three months later the invoice lands in your inbox and it is somehow 20% heavier, stacked with lines you have never seen before. Service charge. Admin fee. Corkage. You scroll back through your email looking for where you agreed to any of this, and you cannot find it, because you did not agree to it in the quote. You agreed to it in the contract, in the fine print, in the paragraph nobody reads at 11pm.

Here is the thing worth saying up front: none of this is vendors being dishonest. These are standard line items that live in the fine print, not the proposal. The quote is one document. The invoice is another. They were never the same number, and the gap between them is where most couples blow their budget without realizing it was avoidable.

So before you sign anything, here are the six line items that get added after the quote you already said yes to, and how to budget for the real total instead of the pretty one.

## the six fees hiding between the quote and the invoice

Let's go through them in the order they tend to bite. Each one is standard. Each one is negotiable, at least a little. And each one is easier to plan for when you see it coming.

1. **Service charge (18-24% on top of the catering total)** and no, this is not the tip.
2. **Admin fee (1-3% of contract value)** billed just for processing paperwork.
3. **Overtime rate ($150-300 per hour, per vendor)** after your contracted end time.
4. **Vendor meals ($25-50 per vendor head)** and most contracts require it in writing.
5. **Setup and strike ($200-800)** for the venue to flip the room or clear it out.
6. **Corkage ($15-35 per bottle)** when you bring your own wine or champagne.

### 1. the service charge (18-24%)

This is the big one, and it is the one people confuse with gratuity most often. The service charge is 18-24% added on top of your catering total, and it is separate from any tip you leave. On a $20,000 catering bill, a 22% service charge is $4,400 you did not see in the quote. That is not a rounding error. That is a honeymoon.

Ask your caterer directly: does the service charge include gratuity for staff, or is tipping expected on top? Sometimes it covers the staff. Sometimes it covers "the cost of service" (whatever that means) and the servers still expect cash. You want that answer in writing, because "service charge" and "gratuity" are two different animals wearing the same coat.

### 2. the admin fee (1-3%)

The admin fee is 1-3% of your contract value, billed for processing paperwork. Yes, paperwork. On a $40,000 total wedding contract, a 2% admin fee is $800 for the privilege of being invoiced.

This one feels the most like a made-up charge, and it is the one you have the best shot at getting waived or reduced, especially if you ask before you sign rather than after. It rarely appears in the initial quote because it is small enough to feel invisible and standard enough that vendors assume you know. You do not have to accept it silently. A polite "can we remove the admin fee?" during negotiation works more often than you would think.

### 3. the overtime rate ($150-300 per hour)

Your contract has an end time. Your reception does not always agree with it. When the party runs long, the overtime rate kicks in at $150-300 per hour, per vendor. Read that again: per vendor. If your photographer, DJ, and coordinator all stay an extra hour, you are not paying one overtime fee. You are paying three.

Say the band, photographer, and venue staff all run 90 minutes over at $200 an hour each. That is $200 x 1.5 hours x 3 vendors, which is $900 added to your invoice from one decision made on the dance floor. Nobody plans for this because in the moment it feels like "just one more hour." Build a buffer, or decide in advance that the party ends when the contract says it ends.

### 4. vendor meals ($25-50 per head)

You are feeding your vendors, and in most cases your contract requires it in writing. Vendor meals run $25-50 per vendor head. Photographers, videographers, the band, your planner, the assistants nobody mentioned. On a team of ten people at $40 a plate, that is $400.

The trap is the catering headcount. You count your guests, you forget your vendors, and then your caterer charges you for ten extra covered plates (which are sometimes billed at full guest price if you did not arrange a discounted vendor meal). Ask your caterer whether they offer a reduced vendor meal rate. Many do. Then ask each vendor how many people they are actually bringing, because "the photographer" is frequently two people.

### 5. setup and strike ($200-800)

Someone has to flip the room from ceremony to reception, and someone has to clear it all out at the end of the night. The venue charges $200-800 to do it, and this setup and strike fee almost never appears in the headline rental number. The rental price gets you the room. It does not always get you the labor to make the room usable twice in one evening.

Ask what the rental actually includes. Is the flip on you? Is strike (breakdown and cleanup) billed hourly or as a flat fee? Is there a penalty if your rentals are not out by a certain time? A $500 strike fee is very different from a $500 strike fee plus a $150-per-hour penalty when your florist runs late collecting arrangements.

### 6. corkage ($15-35 per bottle)

If your venue lets you bring your own wine or champagne, they will usually charge corkage of $15-35 per bottle to open and pour it. The math sneaks up on you. If you bring 60 bottles for a 120-person reception at $25 corkage, that is $1,500 just to have your own wine served.

Bringing your own alcohol can still save money over a full open bar, but only if you run the corkage math first. Sometimes the corkage fee eats so much of the savings that the venue's own package is cheaper and less hassle. You cannot know which is true until you put both numbers next to each other.

## watch for these red flags before you sign

You are not trying to catch anyone in a lie. You are trying to catch the gap between the quote and the invoice before it catches you. Here is what to look for:

- **A quote with no line-item breakdown.** One big number and no detail means the fees are living in a contract you have not read yet.
- **"Service charge" and "gratuity" used interchangeably.** These are different. Get clarity in writing on whether tipping is expected on top.
- **No stated overtime rate.** If the contract says nothing about overtime, that does not mean it is free. It means the rate is undefined and you will find out the expensive way.
- **A guest headcount that ignores vendors.** If nobody has mentioned vendor meals, your catering count is probably wrong.
- **"Setup included" with no mention of strike.** Setup and breakdown are two separate jobs. Confirm both.
- **A BYO alcohol policy with no corkage number.** Great, you can bring wine. At what per-bottle cost?

If a vendor gets defensive when you ask about any of this, that is information too. The good ones will walk you through their fees line by line, because they would rather you budget correctly than get sticker shock at invoice time. ## how to budget for the real total, not the pretty one

The fix is not distrust. The fix is math you do before you sign, not after. Knowing all six of these before you sign means you can plan for the real total from the start.

The practical move is to take every quote and rebuild it with the hidden lines added in. That $20,000 catering quote is really closer to $24,400 once the service charge lands, before you have touched vendor meals or overtime. When you compare vendors, compare the real numbers, not the headline ones, or you will pick the "cheaper" caterer who quietly charges more once the fees stack up.
