say your future mother-in-law mentions a brunch at the rehearsal dinner. everyone nods. two weeks later someone adds "join us for brunch Sunday!" to the bottom of the wedding website, and nobody has said the word "paying" out loud. forty guests now have it on their calendar and a restaurant needs a card on file by Friday.

that's the whole problem with the day-after brunch. the day-after brunch is optional, but once it's on the invite someone is paying. the invite is the moment it stops being a nice idea and becomes a line item.

here's who pays for what, in the order it actually comes up.

## 01. hosting it

**who pays: the host.**

a parent or relative may offer. if nobody does, it is yours to host or skip.

that's the full rule and it's cleaner than most wedding etiquette. the day-after brunch has no default sponsor. the rehearsal dinner has a traditional host, the wedding itself usually has an obvious one, but brunch is a bonus event that exists only because somebody decided to pay for it.

so before you plan a single thing, have the direct conversation. not "we were thinking about a brunch?" but "are you offering to host it?" the vague version is how families end up with a brunch nobody claimed and a bill that gets passive-aggressively split three ways in a group chat at the airport.

### if someone offers

great. let them host it their way. that means they pick the venue, set the headcount, and decide the menu. if you start redirecting the guest list and upgrading the food, you've turned their gift into your event and the money question gets murky again.

### if nobody offers

you have two honest options: host it yourselves, or skip it. a wedding weekend does not require a brunch. nobody has ever gone home disappointed because there wasn't a third event. if your budget is already tight, skipping is a real answer, not a failure. (if you're deciding where the remaining money goes, our [budgeting posts](/blog/category/budgeting) are a better place to spend an hour than a brunch menu.)

## 02. the food

**who pays: the host.**

whoever sends the invite covers the meal.

this is the part people try to negotiate around and shouldn't. there is no version of a hosted invitation where guests arrive and discover they're buying their own eggs. if you invite people to a meal, you're buying the meal.

if the full sit-down version is out of reach, change the format instead of changing who pays:

1. **a hotel lobby coffee and pastries** instead of a restaurant brunch. still hosted, far smaller bill.
2. **a backyard or airbnb spread** with bagels, fruit, and a coffee urn, if someone in the family has the space.
3. **a shorter window.** two hours, not four. the per-person cost usually tracks with how long people sit.
4. **a smaller list.** immediate family and the wedding party instead of all 140 guests. a smaller hosted brunch is better etiquette than a large unhosted one.
5. **skip it entirely** and send people off with a good sendoff at the reception.

what you cannot do is invite everyone to a restaurant and hand them separate checks. if you genuinely want a no-host gathering, it has to be worded as one from the start (more on that below), and it is a different kind of event.

### about headcounts

ask the venue what number they need and when they need it, and ask for the all-in number including service and tax before you confirm. the gap between the menu price and the final invoice is where day-after brunch budgets go sideways. this is the same question you should be asking every vendor, and we've written more about it in [hidden costs](/blog/category/hidden-costs).

## 03. drinks

**who pays: the host.**

mimosas on the table are hosted. anything a guest orders on their own is theirs.

this is the cleanest line in the whole event and it's worth saying out loud to your venue. if you put carafes on the table, you're buying them. if a guest flags down a server for a bloody mary or a second espresso, that's on their own tab.

tell the restaurant exactly where the line is before service starts. the phrase you want is something like: coffee, juice, and the mimosa carafes are on our bill, everything else goes on individual checks. write it into the contract or the email confirmation so the staff isn't improvising on a Sunday morning.

### watch for

a few things that quietly turn a modest brunch into an expensive one:

- **an open bar that nobody defined.** "drinks are covered" means something different to you and to the guest ordering a third round.
- **a room minimum you didn't notice.** some private spaces require a spend minimum, and if you don't hit it you pay the difference anyway. ask whether there is one.
- **service charges stacked on top of gratuity.** ask whether the service charge goes to staff or to the house, and whether a tip is expected on top.
- **an open-ended end time.** if the restaurant doesn't have a hard stop, people linger and the tab grows.
- **a headcount that was never confirmed.** you are usually charged on the guaranteed number, not the number who show up.

## 04. hotel rooms

**who pays: each guest.**

guests already cover their own rooms. the brunch is the thank-you.

this one gets confused because the brunch often happens at the hotel where everyone is staying. the proximity doesn't change anything. guests booked and paid for their own rooms, same as they would for any wedding weekend, and hosting brunch does not obligate you to cover a night.

think of the relationship in reverse: your guests spent real money to be there. the brunch is the thank-you. that framing also helps you right-size it. a thank-you doesn't need to be elaborate, it needs to be warm and it needs to be free for the people you're thanking.

what you can do is block rooms at a negotiated rate so guests have an easier booking experience. that's a courtesy, not a cost you're absorbing.

## 05. the invite

**who pays: the host.**

put it on the wedding website so guests can book travel around it.

the invite does two jobs. it tells people a brunch exists, and it tells them who's hosting. get both into the wording.

### wording that makes hosting clear

- hosted: "please join us for brunch Sunday morning before you head home." no price, no RSVP-with-a-card, no mention of cost. silence on money reads as hosted, which is exactly why you can't stay silent if it isn't.
- not hosted: "we'll be at the hotel restaurant from 9 to 11 if you'd like to stop by, order off the menu." that's an open invitation to gather, not an invitation to a meal, and the difference needs to be obvious before anyone sits down.

### put it on the website early

guests book flights around the end of the weekend. if brunch shows up two weeks before the wedding, half of them have already booked a 7am Sunday departure and your headcount collapses. put it on the wedding website with a date, a time, and an end time, so guests can book travel around it. you can add it to your tasks and budget in [Altared](/get-started) alongside the rest of the weekend so the brunch gets a real owner and a real budget line instead of living in someone's head.

## the short version

1. nobody owes you a brunch. a parent or relative may offer. if nobody does, it is yours to host or skip.
2. whoever sends the invite covers the meal. full stop.
3. mimosas on the table are hosted. anything a guest orders on their own is theirs. tell the venue where the line is, in writing.
4. guests cover their own rooms. the brunch is the thank-you.
5. put it on the wedding website early, with an end time, so guests can book travel around it.
6. ask the venue for the all-in number, and ask whether there's a room minimum, before you confirm.

save this before you send the invites.
