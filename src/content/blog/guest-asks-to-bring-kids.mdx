say you send your save the dates on a Sunday night, and by Monday morning there's a text sitting in your phone that starts with "quick question!!" and ends with your cousin asking whether her two kids can come. you haven't finalized your guest list. you haven't confirmed your venue's capacity. you have no idea what your caterer charges per head. and now you're expected to answer a yes-or-no question that is not actually a yes-or-no question.

the kids question is coming. it always comes. the only variable is whether you've already decided what you're going to say.

this is the text you didn't know you needed to have drafted. six versions, one for each flavor of that conversation. screenshot the one that fits, swap the name, send it.

## decide the policy before you answer a single text

the reason the kids question feels so hard is that it often gets asked before they've made the call. you end up making policy live, over text, with one person, which is how you end up with three different answers circulating in a group chat.

the answer depends on a lot of things:

1. **your guest count.** how many seats you actually have, not how many you wish you had.
2. **your venue rules.** some venues cap headcount hard. some have rules about minors on site, or about late-night hours.
3. **your budget per head.** every added seat is a real number, and you should know what that number is before you say yes to anyone.
4. **whether you made a nursing-baby exception.** decide this once, in advance, not in the moment when someone's asking.
5. **how much energy you have to explain it.** genuinely a factor. some days you have a paragraph in you. most days you don't.

write the policy down somewhere you and your partner can both see it. one sentence. "adults only, nursing babies welcome" or "immediate family kids only" or "no exceptions." then every text you send after that is just a delivery problem, not a decision problem.

if you're still mapping out seats and costs, that's worth doing before the questions start rolling in. you can [get started](/get-started) and track every seat count and per-head cost in one place.

## the six texts

these six cover every version of that conversation. none of them require a long explanation, because you don't owe anyone one.

### 01. the gentle no

> "we kept it adults-only to keep numbers tight, but we'd love to have you there."

this is your default. it's warm, it's final, and it reframes the message around wanting *them*. the "to keep numbers tight" clause does quiet work: it tells the guest this is a structural decision, not a verdict on their specific children.

don't add more. the instinct to soften it with three follow-up sentences is the instinct that gets you negotiated with.

### 02. the yes, one child

> "we have a spot for [name], just let us know so we can add a seat."

sometimes the answer is yes. flower girl, ring bearer, a godchild, the one kid who was always going to be there. the key phrase is "a spot for [name]." naming the specific child makes it clear this is a single seat, not an open door for siblings.

"just let us know so we can add a seat" also does something practical: it puts the confirmation back on them and gives you a clean reason to follow up if they go quiet.

### 03. the under two exception

> "nursing babies are totally welcome, just no older kids at this one."

if you've made a nursing-baby exception, say it plainly and define the edge in the same breath. "just no older kids at this one" is doing the real work there. without that clause, "babies are welcome" becomes "kids are welcome" by the time it reaches the third person in the chain.

"at this one" is also a small gift. it implies the policy is about this event, not a lifelong position on their family.

### 04. the venue rule out

> "our venue has a strict guest count limit, so we can't add anyone, we're so sorry."

when the constraint is external, use it. venues with hard capacity limits give you an answer that nobody can argue with, because arguing means arguing with the fire code.

only use this one if it's true. a guest who finds out later that there were empty chairs will remember.

### 05. the budget reason

> "each seat adds real cost for us, so we had to draw a hard line at adults only."

some people respond better to honesty about money than to vague etiquette language. this text works especially well with close family and with anyone who's planned an event themselves.

"a hard line" is the phrase that matters. it signals that this isn't the opening position in a negotiation.

### 06. the loop-in text

> "i'm passing this to [point person], they're handling all our guest questions."

for when you have no energy left. a parent, a sibling, a maid of honor, a planner. give one person the policy sentence you wrote down, and route everything through them.

this is not avoidance, it's delegation, and it's the single best protection against answering the same question nine times in nine slightly different ways.

## watch for these

a few things that turn one text into a week-long thing:

- **the exception that gets quoted.** the second you make a one-off allowance without explaining why, it becomes precedent in the group chat. if you say yes to one child, be ready to name the reason (in the wedding party, immediate family) or keep it quiet.
- **the follow-up negotiation.** "what if they just come to the ceremony?" is the most common one. decide now whether partial attendance is a yes or a no, so you're not improvising.
- **the second ask through a different person.** a guest who hears no from you sometimes asks your mom. this is exactly what the point person text prevents.
- **the plus-one slide.** the kids question often comes bundled with a plus-one question. answer them separately, or you'll accidentally approve one by approving the other.
- **saying yes because the question was sweet.** you don't have to say yes just because the question was sweet. someone sending a photo of their toddler in a tiny suit is not a binding contract.
- **the RSVP that adds names anyway.** some guests will write extra names on the card regardless. catch it early by comparing returned RSVPs against your invited list as they come in, not the week of.

## how to say it without a paragraph

you don't owe anyone a long explanation. the longer the message, the more surface area there is to push back on. a good no has three parts and stops: the policy, a one-clause reason, and a line that says you want them there.

if you're being asked over and over, that's a sign your wording needs to travel further than your texts. put "adults only" on your wedding website and address envelopes to named adults only. clear information up front means fewer individual conversations later. more on that over in [etiquette](/blog/category/etiquette).

## the short version

- decide your kids policy with your partner before anyone asks, and write it in one sentence
- know your guest count, your venue's limit, and your cost per head first, because every added seat has a number attached
- pick the text that fits, swap the name, send it, don't elaborate
- name specific children when you say yes, and define the edge when you make an exception
- route repeat questions to one point person so the answer stays the same every time
- save this before you need it, because the kids question is coming
