someone shares a rental house link in the group chat. it's beautiful. it's also $4,200 for four nights. one bridesmaid replies "omg obsessed," two go quiet, and one texts the maid of honor privately to ask if there's a cheaper weekend. nobody has said a number out loud yet, and now everyone is negotiating around a house they can't all afford.

the shower and the bachelorette are the two events most likely to quietly stress out your whole bridal party, mostly because nobody talked about money first. not because people are cheap, and not because anyone is being difficult. it's because the rules feel unspoken, and unspoken rules get expensive.

here's the short version before we get into it: the host covers the shower, the bridesmaids split the bachelorette, and the bride pays her own travel. activity add-ons are opt-in only. whoever puts down the deposit owns the risk if plans change. and tips on bachelorette services run 15-20%, split evenly across the group.

six rules. read them, screenshot them, send them to your group chat before anyone books anything.

## rule 1: the host pays for the shower. the bride pays nothing.

this one is the oldest and the clearest. whoever hosts the bridal shower covers it. traditionally that's the maid of honor, the bridesmaids as a group, or a family member (an aunt, a future mother-in-law, a close family friend who offered). the shower is a gift to the bride. asking her to chip in defeats the entire premise.

what "covering it" actually includes:

1. the venue, whether that's a restaurant private room, a rented space, or someone's living room
2. food and drinks
3. decor, florals, and any rentals
4. invitations and postage
5. favors, games, and prizes if you're doing them
6. the cake or dessert

the bride's only job is to give a guest list and show up.

### what if multiple people want to host?

split it. two or three hosts sharing a shower is normal and it takes the pressure off any one person's budget. what matters is that the split is agreed on in writing (a note in a shared doc counts) before anyone books a venue. "we'll figure it out" is how one person ends up eating a $600 catering bill.

### the bride can decline

if the shower being planned is clearly beyond what the hosts can comfortably afford, the bride gets to say "please make it smaller." that's not ungrateful, that's kind. brunch at someone's house with good coffee and a bunch of people who love you is a real shower.

## rule 2: bridesmaids split the bachelorette. the bride pays her own travel.

the standard is that the bridesmaids and attendees split the shared costs of the bachelorette: the house or hotel, group meals, the boat, the party bus, whatever the group activity is. the bride's portion of those shared costs gets covered by the group.

what the bride pays for herself: her travel. her flight, her gas, her ride to the airport. that's it, and that's the part people get wrong in both directions. some groups assume they're flying the bride out. some brides assume they're paying their share of the house. neither is standard.

if the group wants to cover the bride's flight too, that's a generous choice, not an obligation. it should be offered, not expected, and it should be offered before tickets are booked, not after someone already put a $340 flight on their card.

### if you're a bridesmaid who can't swing it

say so early, privately, and without apologizing for a paragraph. "i can do the shower but i can't do the bachelorette weekend" is a complete sentence. a maid of honor who is any good at her job will make it painless. and a bride who is any good at hers will not hold it against you.

## rule 3: set a per-person cap before anyone sends a single venue link

this is the rule that prevents the other five from mattering. $150-300 is the realistic range for most bridesmaids, and putting a number in the group chat early saves a lot of awkward conversations later.

that range is per person for the shared bachelorette costs, and it's a useful anchor because it forces real decisions. at $200 per person with six bridesmaids, you have $1,200 to work with. that's a two-night rental, groceries, one nice dinner out, and one activity. it is not a four-night beach house with a private chef. knowing that on day one changes what gets proposed.

how to actually set the cap:

1. the maid of honor sends one message: "before we look at anything, what's a comfortable number for you? i'm thinking somewhere in the $150-300 range for the shared costs."
2. everyone replies with their real number, privately to the MOH if that's easier
3. the cap is set at the lowest comfortable number, not the average
4. the MOH posts the cap in the chat as a decision, not a suggestion
5. every option proposed after that has to fit inside it

setting the cap at the lowest number is the part people skip, and it's the part that matters. averaging means the person with the tightest budget quietly pays more than she can afford or drops out entirely.

### build in a buffer

whatever your cap is, plan to about 85% of it. gas, an extra grocery run, a cleaning fee nobody read, a cab home at 1am. the costs that show up on the trip itself are the ones that blow past the number people agreed to.

## rule 4: whoever books it owns the deposit risk

this is the rule nobody thinks about until it's a problem. one person puts their card down for the rental deposit. two people drop out six weeks later. the deposit is nonrefundable. who eats it?

the honest answer: the person who booked it, unless the group agreed otherwise in advance.

that's not a punishment, it's just how financial risk works. if you're the one holding the reservation, you're the one the cancellation policy applies to. so:

- **if you're the one booking**, collect money before you pay the deposit, not after. get everyone's share in hand (or at least half of it) first. and read the cancellation window out loud to the group before you commit.
- **if you're not the one booking**, understand that your friend is taking on real exposure on your behalf. pay her the day she asks, not three follow-ups later.

the cleanest version: the group agrees in the chat, in writing, that if someone drops out after the deposit is nonrefundable, that person forfeits her share. it feels awkward to type. it is much less awkward than one bridesmaid quietly absorbing $400 because a plan changed.

### watch for these red flags

- someone books a house "to lock in the rate" before the group has agreed on a budget
- a nonrefundable deposit on a date that isn't confirmed with the bride yet
- venmo requests going out with no itemized breakdown of what the total covers
- a group total that keeps creeping up after the cap was set ("it's only $30 more each")
- one person paying for everything up front with a vague promise to "settle up after"
- add-ons being booked for the whole group without asking the whole group

that last one is rule five.

## rule 5: activity add-ons are opt-in only

the shared costs (lodging, group meals, the main group activity) get split evenly. everything else is optional, and each person opts in and pays her own.

that means the spa afternoon, the second dinner out, the tattoo, the boat upgrade, the matching outfits, the photographer for an hour of content. if half the group wants it and half doesn't, it doesn't get folded into the group total. the people who want it pay for it.

nobody should feel like she has to explain why she's skipping the $95 add-on. structuring it as opt-in from the start means she doesn't have to. and when you're collecting quotes for any of this, keep them somewhere the whole group can see, not buried in one person's dms. that's most of what [/get-started](/get-started) is for: every quote and cost in one place instead of scattered across four apps.

## rule 6: tip 15-20% on bachelorette services, split evenly

this is the line item that gets forgotten every single time, and it's not small.

tips on bachelorette services run 15-20%, and the group splits it evenly. that applies to:

1. the private chef
2. the bartender
3. the party bus or shuttle driver
4. the boat captain and crew
5. hair and makeup artists
6. the house cleaner, if one is booked separately

run the numbers before the trip. a $900 private chef at 20% is $180 in tips. with six people, that's $30 each, and it should be inside your cap, not a surprise venmo on the drive home. same with the party bus: if the driver's tip is $120 and nobody planned for it, someone is covering it alone at 2am.

decide who's carrying cash and how the reimbursement works before you leave. the person handing over the tip should not be the person absorbing it.

## the short version to send to your group chat

1. the host pays for the shower. the bride pays nothing.
2. bridesmaids split the bachelorette. the bride pays her own travel.
3. set a per-person cap first. $150-300 is realistic for most bridesmaids.
4. whoever puts down the deposit owns the risk. collect money before you pay it.
5. activity add-ons are opt-in only. each person pays her own.
6. tip 15-20% on bachelorette services and split it evenly.

none of these rules are about being rigid. they're about making sure the four people who love you most aren't quietly resenting a weekend that was supposed to be fun. the conversation takes ten minutes at the start. skipping it costs a lot more later.
