You get engaged on a Saturday. By Wednesday you're on a venue tour, and by the following week someone is emailing you a catering minimum with a number you have to read twice. Nobody sits you down first and shows you how the whole thing is supposed to split. You just start booking, one vendor at a time, and hope it adds up.

It usually doesn't. Not because couples overspend on purpose, but because they book in the wrong order and find out what everything actually costs one quote too late. The fix is boring and it works: know the percentages before you book a single vendor. Here is the split that keeps couples out of the panic zone, line by line, with the real ranges the pretty planning checklists tend to leave out.

## The split that actually works

Before we go section by section, here is the whole thing in one place. Save this part.

1. **Venue and catering:** 45–50%
2. **Photography:** 10–12%
3. **Florals:** 8–10%
4. **Music and bar:** 5–8% (add 3–5% more for an open bar)
5. **Attire and beauty:** 5–8%
6. **Buffer fund:** 5–8%, held back and untouched until the day of

That adds up to roughly 85–95% of your total budget. The remaining slice covers stationery, transport, the officiant, and whatever else your specific venue requires. Every wedding is different, and your ranges will shift based on where you're getting married and what matters most to you. But these keep the math honest.

The reason this order matters is that the first two categories quietly take almost half the budget before you've booked anyone else. If you don't know that going in, you'll commit to a venue that eats 60%, then wonder why there's no room left for a photographer you actually like.

## Venue and catering: the 45–50% that disappears first

This is the big one. Venue and catering eats 45–50% of most budgets, and it does it quietly, because the two costs are often tangled together. Your venue quote might include catering, might require a specific caterer, or might be venue-only with a separate food minimum on top. Read which one you're getting before you fall in love with the space.

Here's a concrete example of how the math works. If your total budget is $40,000, then 45–50% means you have roughly $18,000 to $20,000 for venue and catering combined. That sounds like a lot until you price a plated dinner for 120 guests, add the service charge, add the rentals the venue doesn't include, and add the tax that lands on all of it.

### Watch for

- **Food and beverage minimums** that you have to hit whether your guest count reaches them or not.
- **Service charges of 20–25%** applied on top of the subtotal, which is not the same as gratuity and often surprises couples.
- **Required rentals** (tables, linens, chairs) that aren't in the headline quote.

If venue and catering are creeping past 50%, that's your signal to either trim the guest list or find a space that leaves more room for everything else. More on where costs hide in our [hidden costs](/blog/category/hidden-costs) posts.

## Photography: budget 10–12%, and don't cut it

Photography gets cut first and regretted most. It is the most common regret we hear, and the reason is simple: the flowers wilt, the food gets eaten, the band packs up, and the photos are the only thing left the next morning. Budget 10–12% and protect it.

On a $40,000 wedding, 10–12% is $4,000 to $4,800. That is a real photographer with a real portfolio, a second shooter for larger weddings, and enough coverage hours to actually capture the day from getting-ready to the last dance.

When couples cut here, they usually do it by trimming hours or skipping the second shooter, and then the album has a gap right where the ceremony exit or the reception should be. If you have to trim photography, trim add-ons like a physical album you can order later, not the core coverage.

## Florals: 8–10%, and the $2k quote that becomes $6k

Florals have a way of doubling between the first call and the final quote. This is the category where 8–10% is realistic, and where $2k quotes become $6k fast if you're not tracking it.

Why the jump? The first conversation is aspirational. You describe the look, the florist sketches the dream, and the early number reflects the simple version. Then you add centerpieces for every table, upgrade to in-season-but-pricey blooms, add an arch installation, boutonnieres, corsages, a ceremony-to-reception move for the arrangements, and suddenly the quote has quietly tripled.

### How to keep florals in range

1. Give the florist a real number up front, not a vibe. Say "this is my floral budget" out loud.
2. Ask what's driving the price. Installations and out-of-season flowers are usually the culprits.
3. Reuse arrangements. Ceremony pieces can move to the reception if you plan the logistics.
4. Get the itemized quote, not the lump sum, so you can see what to cut.

## Music and bar: 5–8%, plus 3–5% more for an open bar

A band or DJ typically runs 5–8% of the budget. A DJ sits at the lower end, a live band at the higher end, and the choice is mostly about the energy you want for the reception.

The part that catches people is the bar. An open bar adds 3–5% more on top of your music line, so if you're planning to cover drinks all night, budget for it as its own category rather than assuming it's folded in somewhere. On a $40,000 wedding, that 3–5% is another $1,200 to $2,000 for alcohol, bartenders, and often a per-head or per-consumption charge from the venue.

### Watch for

- **Corkage fees** if you bring your own alcohol to a venue that allows it.
- **Bartender labor** charged separately from the alcohol itself.
- **Overtime for the band or DJ**, which is exactly the kind of end-of-night surprise your buffer fund exists to cover.

## Attire and beauty: 5–8%, and the costs that aren't in the quote

Attire and beauty run 5–8%, and this is the sneakiest category on the list because alterations, hair, and makeup are almost never in the first quote you see. The dress price on the tag is not the dress price you pay.

Alterations alone can run several hundred dollars, sometimes more for a heavily beaded or structured gown. Then add hair and makeup for the day, which often includes a trial run and, if you're covering the wedding party, multiplies by the number of people in your chairs that morning.

On a $40,000 wedding, 5–8% is $2,000 to $3,200 for both partners' attire, alterations, shoes, accessories, and beauty combined. Budget it as a full category from the start so the alterations bill doesn't feel like it came out of nowhere.

## The buffer fund: 5–8% you do not touch

This is the one most couples skip entirely, then scramble for overtime and gratuity at the end of the night. Hold 5–8% back as a buffer you do not touch until the day of. Overtime and gratuity live here.

On a $40,000 wedding, that's $2,000 to $3,200 sitting in reserve. It sounds like a lot to set aside and not spend, which is exactly why people raid it early. Don't. The final two weeks of planning are when the small costs pile up: the extra hour of photography, the tip envelopes for every vendor, the delivery fee nobody mentioned, the last-minute rental you didn't know you needed.

### What the buffer typically covers

- Vendor overtime (music, photography, venue)
- Gratuity across all your vendors
- Last-minute rentals and delivery fees
- The one thing your specific venue requires that isn't on this list

Couples who fund the buffer coast through the last two weeks. Couples who skip it spend those weeks moving money around and stressing. Treat it as non-negotiable, not optional. If you want more on planning the timeline around these costs, our [planning](/blog/category/planning) posts go deeper.

## What the last 5–15% covers

The six categories above add up to 85–95% of your total. The rest covers stationery (save-the-dates, invitations, day-of signage), transport (shuttles, the getaway car), the officiant, and whatever else your specific venue requires. These are small individually and easy to forget collectively, so give them a real line even if it's a modest one.

## Red flags your budget is off track

Run this quick check against your own quotes:

- **Venue and catering is over 50%.** You're squeezing everything else. Trim the guest list or the space.
- **Photography is under 10%.** You're cutting the thing couples regret most.
- **Your floral quote jumped and you can't say why.** Ask for the itemized version.
- **You have no buffer line at all.** You'll feel it in the final two weeks.
- **Hair, makeup, and alterations aren't written down anywhere.** They're coming whether you budgeted them or not.

## The short version

- Venue and catering take 45–50%, so book them first and know the number before you fall in love with a space.
- Protect photography at 10–12%. It's cut first and regretted most.
- Expect florals to climb. Hold the line at 8–10% and get the itemized quote.
- Music runs 5–8%, and an open bar adds 3–5% on top.
- Attire and beauty are 5–8%, and alterations, hair, and makeup are rarely in the first quote.
- Keep a 5–8% buffer you don't touch until the day of. Overtime and gratuity live there.
