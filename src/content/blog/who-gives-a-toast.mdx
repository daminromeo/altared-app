say the salmon goes out at 7:40 and your uncle stands up at 7:38 with a folded piece of paper nobody knew about. he's warm, he's funny, he's fifteen minutes long. by the time he sits down the plates are cold, the catering team is standing against the wall holding coffee carafes, and your photographer has burned a third of their remaining coverage on the back of a chair.

this is the toast rundown nobody gives you when you get engaged: there's an order, there's a time limit, and if you don't set it in advance someone will go for fifteen minutes while the salmon gets cold.

so here are the six slots, in order, with the exact caps. save this before you need it, and send it to whoever is writing a speech right now.

## the six slots, ranked

1. **host open** (emcee or venue coordinator), 60 seconds
2. **best man**, 4 minutes
3. **maid of honor**, 4 minutes
4. **parent slot** (one parent or one parent set), 3 minutes
5. **couple close** (one or both of you), 3 minutes
6. **the hard limit**: 20 minutes total, full stop

slot 6 isn't a speaker. it's the rule that holds all of it together, and it's the one that gets skipped.

## slot 1: host open, 60 seconds

your emcee or venue coordinator goes first. not a speech, a door opener. welcome everyone, raise your glass, tell the room what's about to happen and in what order.

that last part matters more than it sounds. when the host says "we'll hear from four people over the next twenty minutes, then we're straight into first dance," every other speaker in the room recalibrates. the person who was planning to wing it suddenly remembers they're one of four. the public announcement of the structure is half of the enforcement.

if you don't have a dedicated emcee, your coordinator or dj can do this. confirm who owns it in writing before the day. a slot with no assigned owner is a slot that quietly becomes someone's surprise.

## slot 2: best man, 4 minutes

slot 2 goes right after dinner is called, capped at 4 minutes. the formula that actually lands: one story, one roast line, one sincere close.

one story, not a career retrospective. one roast line, not a set. the sincere close is what people remember, so it should be about both of you, not just the friend he's known since middle school.

### why it comes second

the best man goes early because the room is still fresh and the roast energy plays best before anyone's had a third glass. putting the funniest speech later in the block tempts everyone to one-up it.

## slot 3: maid of honor, 4 minutes

also 4 minutes max. the job here is to shift the focus to her. the best man's speech was largely about one person, so slot 3 balances the room.

a line that does a lot of work in very few words: *she always knew, and now we all do.* that's the whole shape of a good maid of honor speech, a thing she believed about this person before anyone else saw it, and the moment it became obvious to everyone.

four minutes is genuinely enough. it's roughly three pages read slowly, out loud, with pauses for laughing. tell both speakers to time themselves out loud at home, not in their head. in-head reading is always faster than the real thing.

## slot 4: parent slot, 3 minutes

one parent or one parent set speaks. not one from each side, not each stepparent, one slot. 3 minutes.

the content is simple: welcome them to the family, then sit down. this is the slot that balloons if you leave it open, because parents have the longest relationship with the day and the most people in the room they feel obligated to acknowledge.

if both sides want to speak, the options are: pick one set and have the other give a welcome at the rehearsal dinner, or have one person from each side split the 3 minutes between them. what you don't do is add a seventh slot. the second you make an exception, the 20 minute number stops being real.

## slot 5: couple close, 3 minutes

one or both of you speaks last, 3 minutes. thank the room, then cue the first song.

speaking last is the move because it's a natural transition out of the formal block and into the party. you're the only people who can legitimately end the toasts, so your final line should hand off directly to the band or dj. "thank you for being here, now come dance with us" is a cue, not just a sentiment. tell your dj that line is their trigger.

three minutes covers: thank you for traveling, thank you to the people who built this (parents, wedding party, anyone who did real labor), one thing about each other, done.

## slot 6: the 20 minute hard limit

total toast window: 20 minutes, after that guests stop listening and the food gets cold.

you'll notice the slots add up to under 20 with room to spare. that's on purpose. the buffer absorbs applause, the pause while someone finds the mic, the laugh that runs long. a plan with zero slack isn't a plan.

### how to actually enforce it

this is a job, and it belongs to a specific person, usually your coordinator or emcee.

1. send every speaker their slot number and time cap in writing, at least two weeks out.
2. have your emcee announce the structure during slot 1 so the room knows.
3. ask speakers to time themselves out loud once.
4. give the emcee permission, in advance, to stand up and transition if someone runs over. permission granted ahead of time is what makes it not awkward in the moment.
5. put the toast block on the printed timeline your caterer and photographer both get, so everyone's clock matches.

## watch for these

- **the surprise speaker.** someone asks the dj for the mic between courses. the fix is telling your dj and emcee that the mic goes to the five named people, no exceptions, no matter who asks or how charming they are.
- **"i'll just say a few words."** this is always more than a few words. an unassigned speaker is an untimed speaker.
- **toasts scheduled before food hits the table.** hungry rooms are bad audiences and the kitchen's timing gets wrecked. call dinner, then start slot 2.
- **no mic plan.** if the handoff is a scramble, you lose a minute per speaker to logistics alone. one person holds the mic and hands it directly to the next speaker.
- **speeches spread across the whole reception.** splitting toasts between courses sounds considerate and in practice means the room never settles into dancing. keep the block together.
- **nobody told the photographer.** if toasts drift later than planned, your coverage window may not stretch. check your contract language for how overtime is handled before you assume there's flexibility. more on what to read twice in [contracts](/blog/category/contracts).

## the short version

- slot 1: emcee or coordinator, 60 seconds, welcome everyone and raise your glass.
- slot 2: best man, 4 minutes, one story, one roast line, one sincere close.
- slot 3: maid of honor, 4 minutes, shift the focus to her.
- slot 4: one parent or one parent set, 3 minutes, welcome them to the family then sit down.
- slot 5: you two, 3 minutes, thank the room then cue the first song.
- slot 6: 20 minutes total, full stop.
- assign the enforcement to a named person and tell them in advance they have permission to transition.
