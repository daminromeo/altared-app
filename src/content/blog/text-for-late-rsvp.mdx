say it is a Tuesday night, your caterer has emailed you asking for a number, and your RSVP spreadsheet has three rows with nothing in them. not a yes. not a no. not a "sorry, checking with work." just silence since the invites went out.

the caterer needs a headcount. the seating chart needs names. and three people on your list have not said a word since the invite went out.

so here are the three messages, in order. the first is a friendly check-in, the second names the actual deadline, and the third is the close. short, warm where it can be, and honest about the consequence. save this before you need it.

## first, get your actual deadline straight

before you send anything, work backwards from the number your vendors need, not from the date printed on your reply card. those are usually different dates, and the gap is where couples lose a week of chasing.

1. ask your caterer for their final headcount cutoff date in writing, and ask whether the number can go up after that date or only down.
2. ask your venue the same question if they handle rentals, chairs, or place settings.
3. ask your stationer or calligrapher when they need final names for escort cards or place cards.
4. take the earliest of those dates. that is your real cutoff.
5. set your printed RSVP date comfortably before it so you have room to chase.

once you know the real cutoff, the three messages below have somewhere to land. without it, you are nudging people toward a deadline you have not actually confirmed, and they can tell.

## message 01: the nudge

**send 3 weeks before your deadline.**

> "hey! just making sure you got our invite."

that is the whole message. resist the urge to add three sentences of apology around it.

the nudge exists because mail gets lost, emails land in promotions, and people genuinely open an invitation, think "obviously yes," and then never physically reply. a huge share of your non-responders are in that category. they are not snubbing you. they filed it mentally as handled.

### why short works better here

a long message makes the recipient feel accused, which makes them defensive, which makes them slower. a seven-word text reads as logistics, not conflict. it also gives them an easy out that preserves the relationship: "oh my god, I thought I did that."

send it as a text or a DM, individually. not in a group chat, not as a mass email where everyone can see who is on the late list. one person, one message, their name at the top if you want to add it.

### what not to do at this stage

do not send a passive-aggressive story to your whole audience. do not post "some people still have not RSVPed 🙃". the people who need to see it will not, and the people who already replied will feel vaguely scolded.

## message 02: the deadline

**send 1 week before caterer cutoff.**

> "we need your yes or no by friday."

this one names the actual date. that is the entire job of message two.

the first message assumed good faith and gave them an easy path. this one removes the ambiguity. people do not respond to "soon" or "whenever you get a chance." they respond to a day of the week. use the real day, and if it helps, add the reason in one clause: the caterer needs the number.

### a yes or a no, not a maybe

be explicit that a maybe is not an answer you can use. your caterer cannot cook a maybe and your seating chart cannot seat one. if someone genuinely does not know (work travel, a sick parent, a newborn), ask them to give you their best guess now and tell them you will count that guess as final. that is a kinder trade than letting them hold a seat open indefinitely.

if they reply to this one, update your tracker immediately, before you put the phone down. the most common way couples lose track of a headcount is collecting answers across texts, emails, DMs and a paper pile, then trying to reconcile them at 11pm.

## message 03: the close

**send on cutoff day, then count them out.**

> "no response today means no seat. love you."

the "love you" is doing real work in that sentence, and so is the consequence. you are telling them exactly what happens and you are telling them you are not angry. both are true.

then the important part: actually count them out. a final notice you do not act on teaches everyone that your deadlines are suggestions, and it leaves you re-opening the question with your caterer the week of the wedding, usually at a higher price than whatever you were quoted for the original number.

### what to do if they surface afterward

if someone comes back two days later, you are allowed to say "we already submitted the number, I am so sorry, we would have loved to have you." you are also allowed to call the caterer and ask whether one more is possible, and what it would cost. just make that a decision you choose, not a default you fall into for every straggler.

## watch for these red flags

a few patterns are worth catching before they turn into a seating chart problem at midnight.

- **the serial maybe.** someone who replies warmly every time without ever answering the question. treat the second non-answer as a no and tell them so.
- **the plus-one that appeared out of nowhere.** a reply that adds a name you did not invite is not an RSVP, it is a new negotiation. answer it directly and in writing.
- **a reply card returned with no names on it.** more common than you would think with large households. match it to the envelope or call them.
- **"we'll let you know closer to the date."** closer to the date is after your cutoff. say that out loud.
- **relatives routing replies through a parent.** every message that travels through a third party loses detail. get the yes or no from the person who is eating the dinner.
- **a vendor who will not give you a firm cutoff date.** push until you have one in writing. ask what happens to the price if the number moves after that date, and get that in writing too. if you are unsure how to phrase it, our notes on [contracts](/blog/category/contracts) cover what to ask for.

## you are not being rude

worth repeating, because this is the part that stalls people for weeks: you are not being rude by following up. you are managing a real logistical deadline, and your guests who love you will understand that.

nobody who cares about you is going to remember that you texted twice about a dinner count. they will remember whether there was a chair with their name on it.

## the short version

- get the real cutoff date from your caterer, venue and stationer, and use the earliest one.
- **three weeks out:** "hey! just making sure you got our invite."
- **one week out:** "we need your yes or no by friday."
- **cutoff day:** "no response today means no seat. love you." then count them out.
- send every message individually, never in a group thread.
- log each answer the minute it arrives instead of reconciling later.
- treat a maybe as a no unless they give you a usable best guess.

your guest count is not just a seating chart problem, it is a budget problem, because almost every per-person line in your wedding moves with it. keep the number and your quotes in the same place so you can see what one more yes actually does.
