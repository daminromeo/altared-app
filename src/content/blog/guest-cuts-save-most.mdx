You sit down with the catering quote, feeling good about your number. Then you actually count the list. There's your partner's coworker you've met twice. There's a "plus-one" for your cousin who is very single. There are four kids under ten who each get a chair, a plate, and a line item. And there's the b-list you already mailed because you got impatient. By the time you add it up, you're forty people over the number you budgeted for, and at $150 per head, that's $6,000 you never planned to spend.

your guest list is the single biggest lever in your entire wedding budget. not the flowers. not the band. the number of people you feed. the math is simple once you see it: $150 per head times 10 guests is $1,500. most couples have at least two or three of the cuts below sitting in their list right now, and they don't realize it until the invoice lands.

this is the version of the conversation your planner doesn't always have with you before you set the number. here are the six cuts that save the most, in rough order of how much they hurt (spoiler: less than you think) and how much they save (more than you think).

## 1. plus-ones you feel obligated to offer

Capping plus-ones saves $150 to $250 per head on average. That's not a rounding error. That's a real cost attached to a person you may never see again.

The obligation is mostly imagined. Nobody comes to a wedding for the plus-one policy. The standard etiquette is straightforward and defensible: offer a plus-one to anyone married, engaged, or in a long-term partnership, and to members of the wedding party. Everyone else can come solo and will be seated with people they know.

If you want a cleaner rule, use one of these:

1. Ring on it: married, engaged, or living together gets a plus-one.
2. Wedding party gets a plus-one regardless.
3. Everyone else is invited by name, no "and guest."

Address the envelope to the specific people invited. "Ms. Jordan Lee" is not "Ms. Jordan Lee and Guest." That single choice on the envelope quietly sets the expectation before anyone has to have an awkward conversation.

## 2. work friends and the "obligatory" invite

Obligatory invites average 12 people. At a typical per-head cost, that's $1,800 or more, gone on people who would honestly be relieved not to have to buy a gift and give up a Saturday.

The test here is simple. Would you get dinner with this person, just the two of you, if there were no wedding? If the answer is "not really, we just sit near each other," they're a work acquaintance, not a wedding guest.

### the all-or-nothing office rule

The trap with coworkers is precedent. Invite one person from your team and you've quietly told the other five they were snubbed. So pick a lane and hold it:

- Invite the whole team, or none of the team.
- Invite people you'd stay friends with if you changed jobs, and no one else.
- Keep it out of the office entirely and tell curious coworkers, warmly, that you're keeping it small.

"We're keeping it small" is a complete sentence. You do not owe anyone a spreadsheet of your reasoning.

## 3. kids' seats still cost a full head

This is the one that surprises people most. Each child seat still costs a full head count at most venues. Your caterer and venue price per body in a chair, not per adult appetite. A four-year-old who eats three chicken nuggets still occupies a $150 place setting.

You have a few honest options here, and the key is to be consistent so no one feels singled out:

1. Adults-only, across the board. Note it on your details card and spread the word through family early.
2. Immediate family kids only (nieces, nephews, the flower crew), and no one else's.
3. All kids welcome, but book a separate kids' menu and a sitter zone if your venue allows a lower per-child rate. Confirm the price in writing first, because "kids' meal" does not automatically mean "cheaper" on every contract.

Ten kids removed from a $150 list is $1,500 back, plus a quieter ceremony. If you go adults-only, tell the parents you love directly and early, so they have time to arrange care. That phone call is worth more than a line on a card.

## 4. b-list timing (the free cut most people fumble)

The b-list isn't rude. Sending it wrong is what causes the damage. Send wave two only after declines come in, not before the rsvp deadline.

Here's the mechanics that keep it invisible:

1. Mail your a-list with an rsvp date roughly six to eight weeks before the wedding.
2. Set an internal rsvp deadline for your b-list that's earlier, so wave two still has time to reply comfortably.
3. As "no" replies land from the a-list, release b-list invitations in small batches to fill those exact seats.
4. Never let a b-list guest see a save-the-date that predates their invitation, and keep the two groups off the same group chat.

Done right, this cut costs you nothing and adds nobody who isn't backfilling a real cancellation. It's the single most efficient tool on this list because it lets your final count follow your actual acceptances instead of your optimism. ## 5. the vendor meals you didn't count

Here's a per-head cost hiding in plain sight. 10 vendors at $35 to $50 each adds $350 to $500 that is not in your catering quote. Your photographer, videographer, band or DJ, planner, and their assistants all need to eat, and most contracts require you to feed them.

This isn't a cut in the "uninvite people" sense. It's a cut in the "stop being surprised by it" sense, and it's real money either way. Two moves help:

1. Count your vendor heads on purpose. Get the exact number of people each vendor is sending, in writing, before you sign.
2. Ask your caterer for a vendor meal rate. Many will do a boxed or plated crew meal well below the guest per-head price. Get that number in the contract, not in a "we'll figure it out" email.

The difference between paying a $50 full guest plate and a discounted vendor meal across ten people is exactly the kind of gap that only shows up on the final invoice. ## 6. set a hard cap and let it do the work

Every 10 guests cut saves roughly $1,500 on a $150 per-head budget. So instead of trimming names one painful conversation at a time, set the number first and build the list to fit it.

A hard cap flips the psychology. Now every invite is a trade-off against a fixed total, not an open-ended "sure, why not." When you're at your cap and someone new comes to mind, you have to remove someone to add them. That's a much clearer decision than the vague guilt of "should we?"

Pick your cap from your budget, not the other way around. If you can comfortably spend on 100 heads at $150, your list is 100. Full stop. The venue, the bar, the rentals, and the invitations all scale off that one number, which is exactly why it's the biggest lever you control.

## red flags to watch for in your own quotes

Before you finalize anything, scan for these:

- A per-head price quoted without service charge or gratuity, which can add 18 to 24 percent on top.
- "Estimated" guest counts on a contract with a final-count deadline you haven't clocked. Know the date you can't lower the number.
- Kids and vendors billed at full guest rate with no separate line to negotiate.
- Bar minimums that assume a higher head count than you're actually inviting.
- A b-list that's already seen your save-the-dates. If wave two got the early card, the timing cut is gone.
- Plus-ones written into your rsvp cards as a default ("number of guests: __") instead of by name.

Any one of these can add four figures you never budgeted for. Two or three together is how couples end up thousands over on catering alone.
