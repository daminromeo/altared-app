Two months before the wedding, the family group chat goes quiet for a full day. Then someone types, "wait, I thought your side was handling the rehearsal dinner?" Nobody was being difficult. Nobody was cheap. It just turned out that four different people had four different assumptions, and none of them had ever been said out loud. That is how most wedding money fights start. Not over the seating chart, but over six quiet line items that no one puts in writing until the invoice shows up.

Here is the short version, plus what to actually do about each one so your group chat survives to the wedding day.

## the six who-pays questions, in plain numbers

Before we break them down, here is the whole list at a glance. Save it, print it, drop it in the chat.

1. **Rehearsal dinner** — groom's family traditionally covers it, avg $3,000-$8,000.
2. **Bar tab** — venue or couple pays, not the caterer, unless your contract says otherwise.
3. **Bridesmaid costs** — bridesmaids pay their own way, avg $1,800 total per person.
4. **Vendor tips** — the couple pays, 15-20% per vendor, on top of the final invoice total.
5. **Guest upgrades** — whoever invites the extra guests covers their plate, avg $85-$175 each.
6. **Second shooter** — the couple pays, $400-$900 extra, almost never in the base photo package.

None of this is a gotcha. It is just the part of wedding finance that stays invisible until someone gets a bill. Let's take them one at a time.

## 1. the rehearsal dinner: whose bill is it, really?

Tradition says the groom's family hosts the rehearsal dinner, and the average lands between $3,000 and $8,000 depending on headcount and venue. That range is wide for a reason. A backyard taco spread for twelve is not the same as a plated dinner for forty at a restaurant with a private room minimum.

The problem is not the tradition. It is that "the groom's family covers it" gets treated as a fact when it is really a starting point for a conversation. Plenty of couples split it, host it themselves, or skip the formal dinner entirely. What causes tension is when one side assumes the other has it handled and nobody confirms.

Say the number out loud early. "We're expecting around 30 people, which puts the rehearsal dinner near $4,500. Are you still comfortable hosting, or should we plan to split it?" That one sentence prevents the day-of scramble where a $5,000 assumption evaporates.

## 2. the bar tab: it is not the caterer's problem

Here is the one that surprises people most. The bar tab is almost always the couple's or the venue's cost, not the caterer's, unless your contract specifically says otherwise. People assume "food and drink" travels together in one line. It usually does not.

Bar arrangements come in a few flavors, and knowing which one you signed changes everything:

- **Open bar, flat per-person rate.** You pay a set price per guest for unlimited service over a fixed window.
- **Consumption bar.** You pay for exactly what gets poured, which can swing high with a thirsty crowd.
- **Corkage.** You supply the alcohol and pay a per-bottle fee for the venue to serve it.

Each of these lives in a different spot in your paperwork, and the person who signs is the person who pays. If your venue quote says "bar not included," that is your money, full stop. Read that section twice before you assume anyone else has it.

## 3. bridesmaid costs: what "pay their own way" actually adds up to

Bridesmaids traditionally cover their own expenses, and when you add up the dress, alterations, travel, the shower, and the bachelorette, the average is about $1,800 per person. That number tends to shock people on both sides of the aisle. The couple underestimates what they are asking of their friends, and the bridesmaids underestimate what they said yes to.

This one causes tension because it is rarely stated as a total. Someone agrees to be in the wedding picturing a $150 dress, then the destination bachelorette lands and the real figure is closer to that $1,800.

You do not have to cover their costs, but you owe them honesty about the size of the ask. Give your party a rough all-in estimate up front. If travel is unavoidable, consider trimming somewhere else, a lower-cost dress they can rewear, or a local celebration instead of a flight. ## 4. vendor tips: the number people forget to budget for

Tips are the classic invisible line item. The couple pays them, they run 15-20% per vendor, and here is the part people miss: that percentage is calculated on top of your final invoice total, not baked into it.

Multiply that across a full vendor list and it adds up fast. Consider a rough example:

- Catering final invoice: tip at 15-20%
- Photographer
- DJ or band
- Hair and makeup team
- Coordinator

If your catering final total is $12,000, a 15-20% tip is $1,800 to $2,400 on that vendor alone. Stack the rest and you are easily looking at a four-figure tipping budget you never saw coming. Not every vendor expects a tip the same way (owners versus hired staff differ), but you should plan as if most do, then adjust down rather than get caught short. Build a tip line into your [budgeting](/blog/category/budgeting) plan from the start instead of scraping it together the week of.

## 5. guest upgrades: whoever adds the name covers the plate

Here is a rule that saves marriages before they start: whoever invites the extra guests covers their plate. Average plate cost runs $85 to $175 each, and every added name multiplies fast.

The classic flashpoint is a parent who wants to invite ten more of their friends or extended relatives. That is not a small favor. Ten guests at $130 a plate is $1,300, and that is before you add their share of the bar, rentals, and larger cake. If a parent wants those names on the list, the fair and increasingly common approach is that they cover the cost of the additions.

Frame it as math, not a boundary. "We'd love to include them. At about $130 a plate, ten more guests is $1,300. Are you able to cover that?" It turns a loaded conversation into a simple yes or no, and it usually shrinks the list on its own.

## 6. the second shooter: the photography line nobody reads

The second shooter is almost never in the base photography package. Expect $400 to $900 extra, and the couple pays it. Couples routinely assume "two photographers" is standard because they saw two people at a friend's wedding. It was an add-on there too.

A second shooter is genuinely useful (getting-ready shots of both partners at the same time, a second angle during the ceremony, candids while the lead shoots portraits). It can be worth every dollar. But it should be a decision you make on purpose, not a surprise on the final invoice. Ask directly: "Is a second shooter included, or is that additional? What does it cost?" Then decide whether the coverage is worth $400 to $900 to you.

## watch for these red flags in your contracts

The pattern across all six items is the same: the money is real, but the responsibility is vague. These are the phrases and gaps that should make you pause before you sign or before you assume.

- **"Bar not included"** buried in a venue quote. That is your tab, and it can be your single biggest surprise.
- **"Base package"** anywhere in a photography contract. Base means extras exist. Ask what they cost.
- **Tips described as "gratuity may apply"** with no number. Get the percentage in writing.
- **A verbal promise** from a family member about the rehearsal dinner or guest additions. If it is not confirmed with a number, treat it as unconfirmed.
- **A headcount that keeps creeping** without anyone claiming the added plates. Every name has an owner.
- **Two quotes that look identical** but one folds in service charges and the other adds them later.

If a line in your contract does not clearly say who pays, that is the line most likely to blow up your group chat. Flag it now, while you still have leverage to ask.

## the actionable summary

Here is what to do this week, before any invoice forces the conversation:

1. **Name the rehearsal dinner number** ($3,000-$8,000 range) and confirm who is hosting, in writing.
2. **Reread your bar clause.** If it says "not included," that money is yours.
3. **Give your bridesmaids an honest all-in estimate** (around $1,800) so nobody says yes blind.
4. **Add a tips line to your budget** at 15-20% per vendor, calculated on final invoices.
5. **Assign every extra guest to whoever invited them** at $85-$175 a plate.
6. **Ask if the second shooter is included.** If not, decide on purpose whether $400-$900 is worth it.

The families who get through planning without a money fight are not richer or calmer. They just said the numbers out loud early.
