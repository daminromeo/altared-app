Six weeks out from her date, a bride adds one bridesmaid to the hair and makeup lineup. Just one. The salon says "no problem," sends an updated invoice, and the single slot she thought would run the same as everyone else's comes in 40% higher. Then her mom's cousin RSVPs late (three of them, actually), and the caterer quietly adds $200 per head to the final bill. Nothing went wrong. No one made a mistake. This is just how vendor pricing works when your date is close and their schedule is locked.

Here's the thing nobody warns you about: the quote you signed six months ago is not the number you'll pay, especially if anything changes close to the date. Rush florals, a bigger guest count, an extra cake tier, reprinted stationery, a venue hour extension, one more bridesmaid in the chair. Every single one of these carries a last-minute markup, and most contracts bury it in the fine print.

Below are the six costs that reliably double (or close to it) when you make changes near the wire, with the actual markups and the clauses to check before you sign anything else.

## The six costs that double when your date gets close

### 1. Rush florals

Florists order stems on a lead time. When you change your order inside that window, they either pay a premium to get flowers fast or reallocate from another job, and that cost lands on you.

Changes within 60 days typically add a 20-40% rush fee. So if you decide six weeks out that you want to swap your greenery-heavy centerpieces for something with more peonies, you're not just paying the difference in flowers. You're paying the difference plus a markup on the whole affected order.

What triggers it: adding installations, upgrading stem counts, switching to out-of-season or imported blooms, or increasing the number of arrangements. What usually doesn't: swapping a ribbon color or a vessel you already own.

### 2. Guest count

This is the one that catches almost everyone, because late RSVPs feel outside your control. But adding 10 guests late can cost $150-$250 per head, and that number is often higher than your original per-plate quote.

Why? Late additions mean the caterer is re-costing after they've already locked their staffing and food orders. Ten extra guests at the top of that range is $2,500 you didn't budget for, and that's before you count the rentals: more chairs, another table, extra linens, additional place settings.

A quick way to see how fast this compounds:

1. Base per-plate cost you signed: figure your original quote.
2. Late-addition surcharge: often $150-$250 per head instead.
3. Rental add-ons: chair, linen, and place setting per new guest.
4. Bar minimum shifts: more guests can push you into a higher tier.
5. Favors and stationery: place cards, menus, and welcome items you now need more of.

One late RSVP touches five line items. That's why guest count is the single biggest lever in your whole [budget](/blog/category/budgeting).

### 3. Cake tiers

A cake seems fixed once you've tasted and ordered it, but tiers are a common last-minute add when the guest count creeps up or someone decides the dessert table looks thin.

One extra tier added late runs $200-$500 more. Bakers price by serving, and adding a tier close to the date means rescheduling their build time and re-sourcing ingredients on short notice. If you're already bumping your guest count (see above), the cake is often the second domino: more people, more servings, more cake.

If you suspect your numbers might grow, ask your baker up front what a tier costs to add and how much notice they need to avoid the rush rate. Some will hold the standard price if you decide two months out instead of two weeks.

### 4. Rush print

Stationery is deceptively risky because the reprint math is brutal. Reprinting invites or menus last-minute doubles the base cost. Printers set up per job, and a reprint means paying for that setup a second time on top of expedited turnaround and shipping.

The usual culprits:

- A venue or time change after invites are ordered.
- A name spelled wrong on menus or place cards.
- Guest count growth that leaves you short on printed pieces.
- A last-minute detail card (shuttle times, a welcome party) you forgot to include.

If your base invitation order was, say, several hundred dollars, a rush reprint doesn't add a little. It roughly doubles it. Proof everything twice, confirm your venue details are final before you hit order, and pad your quantity by 10-15% so you're not reprinting over three place cards.

### 5. Venue hours

Extending by 1 hour the week of adds $500-$2,000. That's a wide range, and where you land depends entirely on your contract. Some venues charge a flat overtime rate. Others make you extend everything with the extra hour: security, the coordinator, cleaning staff, sometimes the bar.

That last point is the trap. A "one more hour" decision at 9pm on your wedding night isn't just the venue's hourly rate. It can cascade into overtime for every vendor still on site. Your band or DJ, your photographer, your catering staff. Each of those has its own overtime clause, and each one bills separately.

The week-of extension is the most expensive version because there's zero negotiating leverage left. Decide your true end time when you sign, and build in the buffer then, when the venue actually wants your business.

### 6. Hair and makeup

Adding a bridesmaid to the beauty lineup within 30 days? Expect 25-50% more per slot. Beauty teams book artists by the head and by the hour. A late add can mean bringing in a second artist for a single face, or extending the morning timeline, which pushes everyone's start earlier.

So the slot that would have been the standard rate for your other bridesmaids comes in noticeably higher for the late one. If your standard bridesmaid slot was, for example, $120, a 30-day add could put that same slot at $150 to $180. Same service, same chair, different timing.

The fix is boring but it works: confirm your exact beauty headcount at your trial, not the week of. If there's any chance a bridesmaid, a mom, or a flower girl's mom wants in, tell your artist early so they can staff for the max.

## Watch for these clauses before you sign

The markups above aren't scams. They're standard. What catches couples off guard is that the triggers are written into contracts they never fully read. Before you sign anything, or before your next vendor call, look for:

- **A "changes within X days" clause.** This is where the rush percentages live. Florals flag it at 60 days, beauty often at 30. Know your windows.
- **Overtime language that stacks.** If your venue overtime clause references "and all associated vendor staffing," one extra hour is not one line item.
- **Per-head vs. flat late fees.** A flat $250 change fee is very different from $250 per head. Read which one you agreed to.
- **Setup fees on reprints.** Confirm whether a reprint restarts the setup charge. It usually does.
- **A hard RSVP deadline with a penalty date.** Vendors set final-count deadlines for a reason. The day after that deadline is where per-head jumps live.

### Red flags on a vendor call

If a vendor won't give you a straight answer on change fees, that's information. Watch for these:

- "We'll figure that out closer to the date." Translation: at the rate we set later.
- No written change policy at all, just a verbal "it depends."
- An overtime rate that's quoted but not printed in the contract.
- Vague RSVP deadline language with no penalty date attached.

None of these mean the vendor is dishonest. They mean the expensive details are undefined, and undefined always favors the person sending the invoice. Ask for every fee in writing before you sign. Good vendors expect this.

## The move that saves you the most

The single most protective habit is deciding your variables early, while you still have leverage. Two months out, a florist will often hold the standard rate. A baker will slot your extra tier at cost. A venue wants to sell you the fifth hour when you're still shopping, not when you're already booked. The markup isn't punishment. It's the price of urgency, and urgency is the one thing you can plan around.
