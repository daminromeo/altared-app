say it's a Tuesday night, eleven weeks out, and you've got a browser with fourteen tabs open: kraft bags in three sizes, custom stickers with a minimum order of 100, a "bridal welcome bag starter kit" that costs more than your rehearsal dinner centerpieces. welcome bags are one of those things that feel simple until you're standing in a party supply aisle at 11pm holding a tiny lotion and questioning all your choices.

here's the thing nobody tells you: a welcome bag is not a gift. it's logistics with a bow on it. the bags that guests remember are the ones that answered a question they had (where do i catch the shuttle? is there anything to eat before 7?) or solved a problem they were about to have (a headache at 9am). everything else is a souvenir of your wedding that they will quietly leave in the hotel room.

so: five things worth packing, and three to leave out entirely.

## the five that actually land

### 01. an itinerary card

this is the single most useful thing in the bag, and it's the one people cut first because it feels less special than a candle. it isn't.

put three things on it: shuttle times, ceremony start, and one contact number for the whole weekend. one number. not yours, not your partner's, not a group chat. pick your planner, your coordinator, or a very calm friend who is not in the wedding party and will have their phone on them. when fourteen people need to know whether the 3:15 shuttle is the last one, you want all fourteen texts going to the same place.

a few lines that earn their space on the card:

1. friday: welcome drinks, location, start and end time
2. saturday: shuttle departure times (plural, with the last one bolded)
3. ceremony start time, and what time guests should be seated
4. sunday: brunch window, checkout time
5. one contact number, labeled clearly ("weekend questions: [name], [number]")

if your weekend has anything unusual (a venue with no cell service, a ten-minute walk on gravel, a dress code that genuinely matters), that goes on the card too. this is also the place to tell people about parking.

### 02. a water bottle

single-serve bottles are inexpensive in bulk, and guests actually use them every time. that's the whole pitch. nobody has ever thrown out a bottle of water.

it does double duty if your guests are traveling, drinking at a welcome party, or sitting through an outdoor ceremony. buy in bulk, skip the custom label if the budget is tight (the label does not make anyone drink it more), and if your hotel will chill them, even better.

### 03. one good snack

a granola bar or local treat covers late arrivals before the rehearsal. "one" is doing real work in that sentence. three snacks is a gift basket, and gift baskets are where welcome bag budgets go to die.

pick something that survives a day in a hot car and doesn't melt into the itinerary card. if you want the local-treat version, call the bakery or candy shop directly and ask about bulk pricing and lead time before you fall in love with it. small shops often need more notice than you'd guess for a hundred of anything.

### 04. a hangover kit

ibuprofen, antacid, electrolyte packet: the trio that earns you thank-you texts. that's it. you don't need the cutesy pre-assembled version with a printed pouch, though if you find one at a price you like, fine.

check the packaging rules where you're buying: individually sealed single-dose packets are the move, both because they're cleaner and because handing out loose pills from a bulk bottle is not something you want to do at scale.

### 05. a two-line personal note

two lines is enough: "we're so glad you're here. see you tomorrow at 4." that's it. you do not need to write a paragraph about the journey of your love. you will be writing these at a kitchen table at midnight and you will be grateful you kept them short.

if you have the energy, hand-write them. if you have 120 guests and a full-time job, print them and hand-sign. nobody is measuring.

## the three to skip

### skip 01: candles

many hotels don't allow open flames in rooms, so they can go straight in the trash. check your hotel's policy before you buy. if you love the idea of scent as a welcome, skip it anyway. it's a strong personal preference and a guaranteed percentage of your guest list will find it too much.

### skip 02: mini toiletries

hotel rooms already stock these, so you're paying for duplicates. a travel-size lotion feels thoughtful in the aisle at 11pm and reads as clutter when it's sitting next to the identical bottle the hotel already left on the counter.

the exception is a genuine gap: sunscreen for a beach weekend, bug spray for a backyard reception near water, a hair tie and bobby pins if your ceremony is outdoors and windy. those are problem-solvers, not duplicates.

### skip 03: bulky keepsakes

ornaments, frames, mugs: guests flew in, they cannot fit one more thing in a bag. this one stings because keepsakes are the most personal thing on the list, and it feels like the item that proves you cared. it isn't. the itinerary card proves you cared. the mug becomes a decision your guest has to make at 6am on Sunday with a suitcase that already doesn't close.

if you want guests to leave with something physical, put it at the reception where they can choose to take it, not in a bag they have to carry home.

## watch for these when you're ordering

a few things that quietly inflate the cost or create a problem the week of:

- **per-bag delivery fees at the hotel.** a lot of properties charge to distribute bags to rooms, and some charge per bag. ask for the number in writing before you commit, and ask whether handing bags out at the front desk is free.
- **custom minimums you don't need.** if the printed bag has a minimum order well above your guest count, you're paying for extras you'll throw away. ask the vendor for their actual minimum, not the one on the website.
- **bags for every guest instead of every room.** two guests sharing a room do not need two bags. count rooms, not heads, and you'll usually order noticeably fewer.
- **"welcome bag kits" priced as a bundle.** get the itemized cost. a bundle can hide the exact items on the skip list.
- **assembly time.** a hundred bags with five items each is a real evening. recruit people and give them a start time, or you'll be doing it alone at midnight.
- **shipping windows that land after you've left for the venue.** if it's a destination weekend, ship early or ship to the hotel and confirm someone will sign for it.

## price it before you order

the fastest way to blow past your number is to price items one at a time and never total them. do it the other way: decide your per-bag ceiling first, multiply by room count, then shop backward into it. when a vendor quotes you, ask for the all-in number including shipping, delivery and any hotel distribution fee, because the per-unit price is almost never the real price.

## the short version

save this for when you're putting your list together:

1. pack an itinerary card with shuttle times, ceremony start, and one contact number
2. pack water, one good snack, a hangover kit, and a two-line note
3. skip candles, mini toiletries, and bulky keepsakes
4. count rooms, not guests
5. ask the hotel what it costs to distribute bags before you order anything
6. get the all-in number from every vendor, then compare them side by side

build the bag around what a tired traveler needs at 9pm and again at 9am. that's the whole formula.
