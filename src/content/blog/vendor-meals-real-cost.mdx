The catering invoice arrives, and there is a line you did not expect: 11 vendor meals. You budgeted for guests. You did not budget for the photographer, the second shooter, the day-of coordinator, the dj, the videographer, and the six people in the band who all need to eat somewhere between the ceremony and the last dance. That one line just added a few hundred dollars you never wrote down.

This is one of the most under-budgeted lines in a whole wedding budget, and it is not because couples are careless. It is because vendor meals sit in a weird gap. They are not a guest cost, they are not a vendor deposit, and they show up late, usually right when your catering headcount gets finalized. Most couples under-count by 4 or more. Here are the six rules that fix that, and what each one actually costs.

## rule 1: everyone working your wedding counts

The first mistake is counting too narrow. You think "vendor meals" and picture your photographer. But the real list is longer than that, and every person on it eats.

Here is who typically counts as a vendor meal:

1. Photographer
2. Second shooter
3. Day-of coordinator or planner
4. DJ (or each dj if there are two)
5. Videographer (and their assistant, if they bring one)
6. Every member of your band, individually

That last one is where the math gets away from people. A "band" is not one meal. A six-piece band is six humans who each need a plate. Same with your photo and video teams: a single company on your contract can arrive as three or four separate people on the day.

Write the list out by name and role, not by company. When you count bodies instead of businesses, the number jumps, and that is the number your caterer is going to bill you for.

## rule 2: vendor meals cost real money, often at guest price

Caterers typically charge $25 to $75 per vendor meal. The range is wide because it depends on your caterer and, importantly, on whether you negotiate.

Here is the part that catches people: unless you negotiate, a vendor meal comes in at the same rate as a guest plate. Caterers do not automatically give you a discount for feeding the crew instead of Aunt Carol. So if your guest plate is $75, your default vendor meal is $75, times however many bodies you counted in rule 1.

Some caterers do offer a reduced "vendor meal" rate, often a simpler hot dish served without the full plated-dinner presentation. It is worth asking directly: "Do you have a vendor meal rate, and what does it include?" A yes can meaningfully drop this line. But do not assume it. Assume guest price until your contract says otherwise.

### the negotiation ask, in one sentence

When you are reviewing your catering quote, ask: "Can vendor meals be billed at a reduced rate instead of full guest price, and can that be written into the contract?" Getting it in writing matters more than the verbal yes, which brings us to the next rule.

## rule 3: your venue may require hot meals in writing

This is the clause that is easy to miss because it does not live in your catering quote. It lives in your venue contract.

Many venues require hot meals for vendors working six or more hours. Not a boxed sandwich, not the leftover appetizers, an actual hot meal. And it can be written into the contract as a requirement, which means it is not optional even if you were hoping to cut corners there.

The logic behind it is real: your photographer is on their feet for ten hours, and a fed vendor is a functioning vendor. But the budget impact is that a "we'll just grab them something" plan does not fly if your venue mandates hot plated meals for a six-plus-hour crew. That mandate pushes you toward the higher end of that $25 to $75 range.

Read your venue contract specifically for language about vendor catering, staff meals, or crew provisions. If you find a hot-meal clause, factor the higher meal cost in now, not when the caterer reconciles your final count.

## rule 4: do the band math before you sign

Let's make this concrete, because a range on a page never lands the way a number does.

Say you booked a six-piece band. That is six meals. At $50 each (right in the middle of the $25 to $75 range), that is $300. And that is $300 before service charge, before tax, and before the tip we are about to talk about.

Now stack the rest of the day on top: photographer plus second shooter is two more meals, coordinator is one, dj is one, videographer is one. That is five more, and if they are also billed at $50, that is another $250. You are at $550 in vendor meals for a very normal vendor lineup, and we have not left the catering line yet.

Run this math for your actual vendors before you sign your catering contract. It is a five-minute exercise that stops a several-hundred-dollar surprise. ## rule 5: the meal does not replace the tip

This is the rule that surprises people the most, and it is worth saying plainly: feeding a vendor is not the same as tipping a vendor.

Vendor meals do not replace gratuity. Providing a hot dinner is a courtesy (and sometimes a contract requirement), but it is not a substitute for the 10 to 20 percent per vendor that is still expected on top. These are two separate lines in your budget, and they get charged in two separate ways: the meal shows up on your catering invoice, and the tip goes out in envelopes on or after the wedding day.

If you are working with 8 to 12 vendors on the day, that gratuity is a real number that needs its own budget line from the start. Not every vendor expects the same thing (tipping norms vary by role and by whether someone owns their business), but the mistake to avoid is assuming "well, we fed them" covers it. It does not. ## rule 6: add the vendor meal line early

The reason this cost feels like an ambush is timing. Most people add the vendor meal line too late, after the catering invoice already spelled it out for them. By then you are reacting to a number instead of planning for it.

Add a vendor meal line to your budget early, ideally the same day you start booking vendors. A realistic starting count is 8 to 12 meals. That covers the common lineup: photographer, second shooter, coordinator, dj, videographer, and a band. Adjust up if you have a larger band or bigger photo and video teams, and adjust down if you are working with a leaner crew.

Here is a clean starting estimate you can drop into your budget today:

1. Count your vendors by body, not by company (start with 8 to 12).
2. Multiply by your caterer's vendor meal rate ($25 to $75; use $50 if you do not know yet).
3. Add a separate gratuity line at 10 to 20 percent per vendor.
4. Check your venue contract for a hot-meal requirement and adjust the rate if needed.

## watch for: the red flags that mean you're under-counting

A few signals that your vendor meal line is about to bite you:

- **Your budget lists companies, not people.** "Band" instead of six musicians. "Photo/video" instead of four humans. This is the number one cause of under-counting.
- **Your catering quote is silent on vendor meals.** Silence usually means guest price by default. Ask before you assume you got a deal.
- **Nobody has read the venue contract for a hot-meal clause.** If it is in there and you did not budget for hot plates, your rate just went up.
- **You have a gratuity line but not a vendor meal line, or vice versa.** They are two separate costs. Having one does not cover the other.
- **You are finalizing catering headcount and only now adding vendors.** If this line appears for the first time on the final invoice, you added it too late.

If any of those sound like your spreadsheet, you are probably budgeting for fewer vendor meals than you actually owe, and now is the time to fix it.

## the quick version

Vendor meals are small on their own and significant in aggregate. Here is what to lock in before you sign your catering contract:

- **Count people, not companies.** A six-piece band is six meals. Photo and video teams are often more than one plate each.
- **Assume guest price unless you negotiate.** Caterers charge $25 to $75 per vendor meal, billed at guest rate by default.
- **Read the venue contract.** Many venues require hot meals for vendors working six or more hours, and it can be a written requirement.
- **Budget the tip separately.** Meals do not replace gratuity. Expect 10 to 20 percent per vendor on top.
- **Add the line early.** Start with 8 to 12 meals and refine as you book.
