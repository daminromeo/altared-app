Two weeks before her wedding, a bride we heard from sat down with a stack of cash and a list of vendors, ready to fill envelopes. She got to her caterer and paused. The invoice already had a line that said "service charge, 20%." Did that count? Was she supposed to tip on top of it? And wait, her photographer was bringing a second shooter. Was that one envelope or two?

She didn't have answers, because nobody had written them down for her. That's the whole problem with wedding tipping. Every vendor category has a different norm, some contracts already build gratuity in, and no one puts it in writing. So couples end up guessing, either over-tipping out of guilt or accidentally stiffing someone who worked a 10-hour day.

These are the five rules that catch most couples off guard. Save this now. You'll want it two weeks before the wedding when you're building your tip budget and stuffing envelopes at the kitchen table.

## Rule 1: the service charge on your catering invoice is not a tip

This is the one that trips up almost everyone. Your catering (or venue) invoice has a line that reads something like "service charge, 18-22%." It looks like gratuity. It sounds like gratuity. It is not gratuity.

A service charge is a fee the company keeps to cover operational costs: coordination, staffing logistics, overhead. In many cases the servers and bartenders working your reception see none of it. That 18-22% goes to the business, not the people carrying trays for four hours.

Here's how to handle it without double-paying:

1. Read the line item carefully. Look for the words "gratuity included" specifically, not "service charge."
2. If the contract doesn't say gratuity is included, ask your caterer directly: "Does the service charge go to the staff, or should I tip them separately?"
3. If staff aren't covered, budget a cash tip for the on-site team. A common approach is $20 to $50 per server or bartender, handed to the captain to distribute.
4. If gratuity truly is built in, you're done. You don't need to tip again.

The point is to ask, not assume. On a $10,000 catering bill, a 20% service charge is $2,000, and it's completely reasonable to want to know whether the humans who served you are getting any of it.

**Watch for:** vague contract language. "Service charge" and "gratuity" are not interchangeable words, even though vendors sometimes use them loosely. Get the answer in writing before the wedding so you're not making decisions with a cocktail in your hand.

## Rule 2: the second shooter gets their own envelope

Photographers usually aren't required to be tipped, especially if they own their studio. But it's become common and appreciated, and here's the part people miss: if two people showed up to shoot your day, that's two envelopes.

The standard is $50 to $150 per shooter. Your lead photographer and your second shooter are often different people with different roles, and the second shooter is frequently a contractor who doesn't set the rate or take home the full booking fee. They just worked your entire day too, catching the moments the lead couldn't be in two places for.

So when you're building envelopes, count heads, not companies. Same logic applies to a videographer working alongside your photographer. If a separate person did a separate job all day, they get their own envelope.

A quick example. If your lead photographer brought one second shooter, and you tip $100 each, that's $200 total, in two envelopes, labeled with two names. Not one envelope of $200 handed to the lead and hoped to be split.

## Rule 3: hair and makeup get tipped on the full rate, each

Hair and makeup follows the beauty-industry norm, which means 15-20% per artist, the same way you'd tip at a salon. Two rules inside this rule.

First, each artist gets tipped separately. If one person does hair and another does makeup, they each get 15-20%. If one person does both, they get one tip on the combined total.

Second, and this is the sneaky one: tip on the full rate, not the discounted one. Say your makeup artist normally charges $200 but gave you a bridal-party package deal that brought your personal rate down to $150. The tip is calculated on the $200, because 15-20% of the artist's real rate is the standard their income is built around. Discounts are a kindness they extend to you. The tip shouldn't shrink because they did you a favor.

Here's the math laid out:

- Makeup artist, full rate $200, tip at 20% = $40
- Hair stylist, full rate $180, tip at 20% = $36
- Total in beauty tips: $76, in two envelopes if two artists

## Rule 4: DJ versus band changes the whole calculation

Music tipping depends entirely on whether you booked a DJ or a live band, and the difference is bigger than people expect.

**A DJ** is typically one person (sometimes with an assistant). The norm is $50 to $150 total. One envelope, one performer, simple.

**A band** is a group, and the norm is $25 to $50 per musician. That per-person number is where couples get blindsided. A five-piece band at $30 each is $150. An eight-piece band at $40 each is $320. Splits add up fast, and if you budgeted "some tip for the music" without counting the people on stage, you'll be short.

So before you decide music is a small line, count the band members:

1. Ask your bandleader how many musicians will actually perform at your wedding.
2. Multiply by $25 to $50 depending on the quality of the experience and your budget.
3. Decide whether you're handing one envelope to the bandleader to split or tipping individually (most couples give one envelope to the leader).
4. If you booked a DJ instead, budget the flat $50 to $150 and move on.

**Watch for:** the sound tech or MC who came with the package. If a separate person ran audio or hosted the reception all night, that's another person who did a distinct job. Ask who's coming so nobody gets forgotten.

## Rule 5: someone else holds the envelopes, because you won't have time

This is the rule that makes all the other rules work. You can build the perfect tip budget, label every envelope, and calculate every percentage correctly, and none of it matters if the envelopes stay in your bag while you're getting married.

You will not sit down for four hours straight on your wedding day. You'll be taking photos, greeting guests, eating three bites of dinner, and dancing. You will not be tracking down the second shooter to hand them cash at the exact moment they're leaving. It just won't happen.

So delegate it. Give the stack of labeled envelopes to your wedding planner, your maid of honor, or a trusted family member the morning of, along with a simple list of who gets what and roughly when. Vendors who leave early (photographer, hair and makeup, sometimes the caterer) get tipped as they go. Vendors who stay to the end (DJ, band) get tipped at the end of the night.

A clean handoff looks like this:

- Envelopes labeled with each vendor's name and role
- A one-page list: vendor name, envelope amount, when they typically leave
- One designated person (planner or MOH) holding the stack
- A quick text or note to that person the night before so it's not a surprise

That's it. The person holding the envelopes becomes the reason your tipping actually happens instead of becoming an awkward Venmo the following week.

## Quick recap: your tipping cheat sheet

Here's everything in one place to save before you stuff the envelopes:

1. **Service charge is not a tip.** That 18-22% on the invoice may go to the business, not the staff. Ask, don't assume.
2. **Photographers get $50-$150 per shooter.** The second shooter gets their own envelope.
3. **Hair and makeup get 15-20% each, on the full rate,** not the discounted one.
4. **DJ: $50-$150 total. Band: $25-$50 per musician.** Count the people on stage.
5. **Hand the envelopes to your planner or MOH.** You won't have time day-of.
