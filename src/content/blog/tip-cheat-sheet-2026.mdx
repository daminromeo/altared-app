Picture the Thursday before your wedding. The seating chart is finally done, your dress is steamed, and then someone (usually your mom, sometimes your maid of honor) asks the question no one prepared you for: "wait, did you get cash for tips?" Suddenly you're at the ATM at 9pm, guessing at amounts, stuffing bills into envelopes you're labeling with a Sharpie, hoping $100 is enough for a photographer you paid $4,000.

No one hands you a tipping guide at the venue tour. So here it is, the 2026 version, with exact numbers you can budget for now instead of scrambling for the week of.

The reason this matters isn't just etiquette. It's money. Tips can quietly add up to several hundred, sometimes over a thousand dollars, and most couples don't account for them until it's too late to plan around. Knowing the numbers now means you fold them into your budget from the start, not pull from somewhere else at the last minute.

## The 7 vendors and exactly what to tip

Here's the short version you came for. We'll break each one down below.

1. **Caterer staff:** 15-20% of the food total, separate from any service charge
2. **Photographer (and second shooter):** $100-$200 each
3. **Florist:** 10-15% of the floral invoice, if they styled on-site
4. **DJ:** $50-$150 flat, more if they ran the whole room
5. **Hair and makeup:** 15-20% per artist, in separate envelopes
6. **Transportation:** 15-20% per driver, but check the contract first
7. **Officiant:** $50-$100 if they personalized your ceremony

Save that. Now let's talk about the parts that trip people up.

## The percentage vendors (and the service charge trap)

### Caterer

Your caterer staff get 15-20% of the food total. The trap here is the service charge. A service charge is not a tip. It's a line item that covers the caterer's operational costs (staffing logistics, event coordination, overhead), and in most cases very little or none of it lands in your servers' pockets. So when you tip, calculate 15-20% of the food total, not the service charge, and treat them as two separate things.

Quick math on a real number: if your food total is $8,000, a 15-20% tip lands between $1,200 and $1,600. That's not a rounding error. That's a real line in your budget, and it's exactly the kind of thing couples miss when they only look at the contract's bottom line.

Always ask your caterer directly: "is gratuity for the service staff already included, and if so, at what percentage?" Sometimes it genuinely is. Sometimes the service charge is doing double duty. You want that in writing before you decide what to hand over on the day.

### Florist

Florists earn 10-15% of the floral invoice, but only if they styled on-site. There's a real difference here. If your florist and their team show up, install an arch, fluff every centerpiece, and hang something from the ceiling, that's labor and artistry worth tipping. If they drop the arrangements at the door and leave, you can skip it. A drop-and-go delivery doesn't carry the same on-site effort.

On a $2,500 floral invoice with full on-site styling, 10-15% is $250 to $375. Reasonable, and easy to plan for once you know whether they're installing or just delivering.

### Hair and makeup

Hair and makeup artists get 15-20% each, same as you'd tip at a salon. The key detail: give each artist their own envelope. If you've got one person on hair and another on makeup, that's two tips, calculated separately on each person's service total. Don't lump it into one envelope and assume it'll get split fairly. It might not, and it's awkward for everyone.

### Transportation

Drivers get 15-20% of the bill per person. But read the contract first, because transportation companies bake gratuity into the invoice more often than any other vendor on this list. If it's already in there (and it frequently is), you're not obligated to tip again. Double-tipping a driver because you didn't read the fine print is a common, avoidable mistake.

## The flat-tip vendors

### Photographer and second shooter

Your photographer and second shooter each get $100-$200. This one surprises people because photography is often the single biggest vendor line in the whole budget, and the tip is a flat amount rather than a percentage. That's the standard in 2026, and it holds even on a $5,000 package.

The part couples forget: the second shooter. If two people are working your wedding, that's two envelopes, same $100-$200 range each, handed to each shooter individually. Don't hand one lump sum to the lead and assume it trickles down.

### DJ

Your DJ gets a $50-$150 flat tip as the norm. Bump it to $200 if they MC'd the whole night, read the room, kept the dance floor packed, and basically ran the reception. A DJ who just presses play is different from one who runs the whole room, and the tip should reflect that. If your DJ owns the company, tipping is optional but still appreciated, especially for an all-night MC performance.

### Officiant

Your officiant deserves $50-$100 if they made the ceremony feel like you. A generic ceremony read off a card is one thing. An officiant who met with you, wove in your story, and made the room laugh and cry is another. That personalization is worth the tip.

One important note on clergy: if a religious officiant is marrying you, they often can't accept a personal cash tip, but they will gladly accept a donation to their church, synagogue, mosque, or organization. Ask what's appropriate rather than guessing.

## Watch for these red flags

Tipping is where couples lose money to confusion. Keep an eye out for:

- **A service charge disguised as gratuity.** If the caterer's invoice has a 22% "service charge," ask point-blank whether any of it goes to the staff. If the answer is no or vague, plan to tip your servers on top.
- **Gratuity already baked into a bill.** This is most common with transportation, but it shows up in catering and hair/makeup contracts too. If it's already included, don't pay it twice.
- **One envelope for a two-person team.** Second shooters and separate hair/makeup artists each get their own envelope. Assuming it gets split is how people go undertipped.
- **"Tipping is optional" as a reason to skip it entirely.** Optional means not contractually required. For someone who owned the room all night, it's still the right move if they went above and beyond.
- **Waiting until the week of.** The single biggest red flag is not budgeting for tips at all. This is the list most couples figure out the week of the wedding, scrambling for cash envelopes. Do it now instead.

## How to actually plan for this

You don't need to memorize percentages. You need to read your contracts before you sign them, so there are no surprises in the last week. Here's the workflow:

1. Pull every signed contract into one place.
2. For each vendor, find the total, then find any service charge or gratuity line separately.
3. Note whether gratuity is already included (transportation and catering especially).
4. Calculate the tip on the correct base (food total, floral invoice, or per-person service), or use the flat amount for photographers, DJs, and officiants.
5. Add the total tip pool as its own line in your budget now, not the week of.
6. Prep and label the cash envelopes a few days early, and hand them to a trusted person to distribute on the day.
