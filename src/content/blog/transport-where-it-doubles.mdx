You get the limo quote back and it says $650. That feels fine. It fits the budget line you set months ago, so you sign, you move on to the seating chart, and you forget about it. Then the invoice arrives the week of the wedding and it says $1,180. Nothing on it is technically wrong. Every line was in the contract you signed. You just never read it as a total, because the contract was written to be read one line at a time.

Wedding transport quotes are almost never the real number. Not because anyone is trying to trick you, but because these contracts are written for the company, not the couple. The base rate is the headline. The six lines that actually move your bill sit underneath it, quiet, standard, and easy to skim past. Here is every one of them, what it does to your total, and how to negotiate from the real number instead of the pretty one on the front page.

## 1. Minimum hour blocks

Most limos and coaches carry a 3 to 5 hour minimum. That is the single biggest gap between what you think you are paying and what you actually pay, because your real transport need is usually short.

Think about the actual job. You need the car to get the wedding party from the hotel to the ceremony, maybe a loop for photos, then to the reception. That is often 90 minutes of driving. But you cannot book 90 minutes. You book the minimum, and the minimum is three, four, sometimes five hours. So you pay for four hours when you needed 90 minutes, and the two and a half hours of "idle" time in the middle is billed at the same rate as the time the car is moving.

There is a way to work with this instead of against it:

1. Ask what the minimum block actually is before you fall in love with a vehicle. It varies wildly by company.
2. Stack your needs inside the block you are already paying for. If you are stuck with four hours, use them. A second photo stop, a shuttle for the parents, a mid-reception cigar run.
3. Consider two shorter separate bookings versus one long one, and price both. Sometimes a ceremony car plus a separate reception car beats one vehicle sitting idle for hours.
4. Book the block to end when you actually need it to, not when the reception ends on paper.

## 2. The gratuity clause

Here is the one that quietly costs couples the most, because they pay it twice. A 15 to 20% tip is often pre-baked into the contract. It is already in there, added to your total, before you have met the driver.

Then the wedding day arrives, the driver is lovely, they held the door and helped grandma up the step, and you hand them cash at the curb. That is a completely reasonable instinct. It is also a second tip on top of the one you already paid in the contract.

On a $1,000 transport bill, a pre-baked 18% gratuity is $180. If you tip another $100 at the door, you have paid $280 in gratuity on a $1,000 service and you did not decide to. Before you book anything, find the word "gratuity" or "service charge" in the contract and confirm whether it is already included. If it is, you are free to tip a small extra amount for genuinely great service, but you are doing it by choice, not by accident.

## 3. Deadhead miles

Deadhead miles are the ones nobody expects because you never rode in the car for them. This is the charge for the driver's trip to you. The vehicle lives at a depot somewhere, and getting it from the depot to your venue costs the company time and fuel, so they pass it to you, sometimes at $1 to $3 per mile each way.

If your venue is 40 miles from the transport company's base, that is 80 round-trip miles of deadhead, and at $2 a mile that is $160 added to your bill before a single guest gets in the car. The further your venue sits from a city, the bigger this line gets, which is exactly backwards from what most couples assume (they think a rural venue means cheaper everything).

### What to ask

Ask where the vehicle is dispatched from and whether deadhead is charged one way or both. Then ask if there is a company closer to your venue. A slightly higher base rate from a local operator can beat a lower rate plus 80 miles of deadhead from across the state.

## 4. Guest shuttles

This is the line venues rarely flag as expected, and it is a big one. A single guest shuttle loop runs $600 to $1,200. One loop. If you have guests at multiple hotels, or you need continuous service so people can leave the reception whenever they want, you are running several loops, and the number climbs fast.

The trap is that guest shuttles feel optional right up until they are not. If your venue has no parking, or a long private drive, or a bar and a rural highway between it and the hotels, a shuttle stops being a nice-to-have and becomes a safety requirement. Nobody puts that in the venue brochure.

Budget it as a real line from the start. If your ceremony and reception are both remote, assume at least one shuttle loop at $600 to $1,200 and price out whether you need more than one loop for the departure rush. It is far better to see this number in your planning spreadsheet in month two than on your invoice in month eleven. If you are still building that spreadsheet, our [budgeting guides](/blog/category/budgeting) are a good place to slot this in early.

## 5. The overtime rate

Overtime kicks in the second you go past your booked block, and it does not tick up gently. Going 30 minutes past your block can trigger a full extra hour, billed at 1.5x.

Read that again, because it is the part that stings. If your block ends at 11:00 and the last dance runs to 11:20, you have not been charged for 20 minutes. You have been charged for a full hour at time and a half. On a vehicle with a $200 hourly rate, that 20 minutes of dancing costs you $300.

Weddings run long. They almost always run long. So plan for it on purpose:

1. Build your booked block to end after your actual planned end time, not right at it, with a 30 to 60 minute buffer.
2. Confirm the exact overtime rate and the increment (full hour versus 15-minute) in writing.
3. Assign one person, not you, to watch the clock and make the call on extending. You will be dancing.
4. Know the number ahead of time so an on-the-day extension is a decision, not a shock.

## 6. The fuel surcharge

The last one is the quietest. A 5 to 10% fuel line shows up at invoicing, buried after the base rate. It is rarely mentioned in the quote conversation because it is calculated on the final total, so it cannot be a clean number until everything else is added up.

On a $1,000 bill, a 7% fuel surcharge is another $70. Not huge on its own, but it is the last brick on a stack that started with a "fine" quote and ended somewhere much higher. Ask whether a fuel surcharge applies and what percentage, and add it to your mental total for every quote you compare, so you are comparing real numbers against each other.

## Watch for these red flags

When you are reading transport contracts, a few things should make you slow down and ask questions:

- A quote that lists only a base rate with no mention of gratuity, fuel, or minimums. The lines still exist, they are just not shown to you yet.
- A minimum block that is far longer than your actual need, with no willingness to discuss it.
- Vague overtime language like "additional time billed at prevailing rates." Get the exact rate and increment in writing.
- A venue that cannot tell you whether guests will need a shuttle. That usually means yes, and they just do not want to be the one to say it.
- Deadhead charges from a company based far from your venue, quoted as a flat "travel fee" with no per-mile breakdown.

None of these mean the company is dishonest. They mean the contract is doing its job, which is to protect the company. Your job is to read it as your total.

## Add it up before you sign

Here is what the real math can look like. A $650 base becomes $650 plus $180 gratuity plus $160 deadhead plus $70 fuel, and that is before a single shuttle loop or a minute of overtime. The quote said $650. The real number was over $1,000, and every dollar of it was in the contract you already had.

Before you book any wedding transport:

1. Find the minimum block and stack your needs inside it.
2. Confirm whether gratuity is pre-baked, so you do not tip twice.
3. Ask where the vehicle dispatches from and whether deadhead is one way or both.
4. Budget guest shuttles at $600 to $1,200 per loop from day one.
5. Get the exact overtime rate and increment, and build in a buffer.
6. Add the 5 to 10% fuel surcharge to every quote before you compare.
