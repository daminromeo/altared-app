say the bar opens at six, cocktail hour is in full swing, and at seven-fifteen someone taps your shoulder mid-conversation to ask what they should do about a guest who is three drinks past fine. you are holding a drink in one hand and your dress in the other. you have not eaten. your photographer is waiting for golden hour. and now you are the one making a judgment call about a family member in front of forty people.

that moment is avoidable. not because you can control how much anyone drinks, but because the decision can be made in advance by someone who is not you.

you already know who this guest is. so does every family member who quietly looked away when you said you were having an open bar. nobody needs to say the name out loud at the rehearsal dinner. you just need to write it down once, hand it to your point person, and stop carrying it.

## why this goes to a point person, not to you

your point person is whoever is running the day: a coordinator, a venue manager, a day-of hire, or a very organized friend who is not in the wedding party and not related to the guest in question. that last part matters more than people expect. a sibling or a parent has history with this guest. history makes people hesitate, negotiate, or soften a boundary at exactly the wrong moment.

what you are doing here is a vendor handoff. you already brief your photographer on the family members who should be in the formal photos. you already tell your dj which songs are banned. this is the same category of prep, just less fun to write down. none of this is dramatic. it's the same kind of prep you'd do for any vendor handoff.

the handoff happens before the ceremony. not during cocktail hour, not in a text at nine pm. before you are unreachable.

## the three things

### 01. the name

give the actual name and table number before the ceremony. not a description, not "you'll know him when you see him," not a vague gesture toward the groom's side of the family.

"uncle dave, table 4, left side."

a point person who has never met your family cannot work from vibes. they are managing a room of people they do not know, in low light, while also tracking the cake delivery. a name plus a table plus a seat position is something they can actually use in under five seconds.

if there is more than one person on the list, that's fine. write all of them. nobody is being punished, and this list does not get shared with anyone else.

one more useful detail: tell your point person who this guest came with. a spouse, a sibling, a ride-share buddy. that's the person most likely to be part of the solution, or part of the problem.

### 02. the signal

agree on one word that means "step in now, no questions."

"weather" is a good one. simple, public-safe.

the point of a signal is that it survives a loud room and a crowded dance floor. you can say it to your point person while standing next to the person you're talking about, and nothing escalates. you are not announcing anything. you are not starting a conversation about whether it's really that bad yet.

a signal works in both directions. your point person can use it on you, too. if they come up mid-reception and say "hey, weather," you nod, and they handle it. you do not ask follow-up questions on the dance floor.

set two rules with the signal:

1. anyone in the briefed group can use it, not just you.
2. once it's said, the plan runs. nobody re-litigates it in the moment.

### 03. the exit

name one person allowed to call them a ride, no debate needed.

this is the piece couples skip, and it's the piece that turns a small moment into a scene. without a named person, what happens is five people standing in a hallway negotiating about whether someone should drive, while the guest in question insists they are fine, and somebody eventually gives up because the first dance is starting.

pick one person. tell them. tell your point person who it is. the authority is pre-assigned so the argument never has to happen in the middle of your first dance.

write it down. verbal-only plans get forgotten by hour two.

a few things to include with the name:
- how the ride gets paid for and by whom
- where the guest is staying, if you know it
- whether there is a second person who can back them up if the first is unreachable

## what to watch for

a plan can look complete on paper and still fall apart. these are the parts that usually break:

1. **briefing the wrong person.** a parent or sibling of the guest will almost always hesitate. pick someone with no stake in the relationship.
2. **a signal that is too clever.** if the code word is a long phrase or an inside joke, it will not survive a dance floor at eleven pm. one word, easy to say, boring in public.
3. **"we'll figure it out if it happens."** this is the version where you figure it out, because you are the only one with authority nobody will argue with.
4. **telling the bar staff and stopping there.** bartenders can cut someone off, and good ones will. they cannot get that person home, and they are not responsible for your guest list.
5. **a verbal-only handoff.** say it and write it. your point person is holding a hundred details that day.
6. **briefing after the ceremony.** by then you are in photos, in receiving lines, in a dress you cannot move quickly in. do it before.

also worth a quick conversation with your venue at the walkthrough: ask what their own policy is for a guest who has had too much, who on their staff handles it, and whether that changes after a certain hour. venues have seen this before. they usually have a procedure, and it is better to know it than to assume it matches yours. if you are mapping out the rest of your bar decisions, our [budgeting](/blog/category/budgeting) and [planning](/blog/category/planning) posts cover the rest of the reception timeline.

## the actual handoff

keep it short. a note on your phone, a line in a shared doc, or a printed card you hand over with the rest of the day-of paperwork. three lines is enough:

1. **name and table.** "uncle dave, table 4, left side."
2. **signal.** one word that means step in right now.
3. **exit.** the one named person pre-authorized to call a ride, written down, with a backup.

then let it go. that is the whole point of handing something off. you briefed someone capable, you gave them authority, and you are not the person managing it anymore.
