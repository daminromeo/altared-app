say it's a Tuesday night three weeks after the wedding. you have a stack of blank cards, a good pen, and a list in a notes app. the first fifteen notes take forty minutes, because the registry gifts are easy. you write the thing, you name the thing, you sign the thing. then you hit the sixth name on the list and the pen stops moving.

that is normal. it's the other ones that sit in your drafts for weeks. the cash gift where naming the number feels crass. the aunt who RSVP'd yes and never came. the photographer who worked a twelve hour day. your parents, who are divorced, and who sat four tables apart and were kind to each other all night. the gift that went back to the store.

here are the five hardest notes to write, and what to actually say in each one.

## 01. the cash gift

the instinct is to go vague. "thank you so much for your generous gift." it reads like a form letter, and the person who gave it knows exactly what they gave.

cash gifts feel awkward to name out loud, but naming them is exactly what makes the note land. name the amount and say how it lands. not "your generosity," but the number and the destination:

> "your gift is going straight to our first home."

the reason this works is that a cash gift is abstract until you give it a shape. the giver wrote a check into a void. you are handing it back to them as a kitchen, a down payment fund, a flight to somewhere. if you have not decided what it's going toward yet, say that honestly. "we're putting it aside while we figure out the house thing, and it's going to matter when we do" is a real sentence that beats "thank you for your generous gift" every time.

### if naming the number still feels like too much

name the use instead, specifically. the rule is specificity, not arithmetic. "the flights are booked and your gift covered them" does the same job.

## 02. the guest who didn't show

someone RSVP'd yes, sent a gift, and then wasn't there. maybe they got sick. maybe something happened and you still don't know what. maybe they just didn't come.

the trap here is writing a note that makes them feel worse. the "we missed you so much, it wasn't the same without you" note reads as warm in your head and as a bill in theirs. thank the gesture, skip the guilt trip:

> "we felt you with us, and we mean that."

no-show guests still deserve warmth, without you making them feel worse about it. if you know the reason and it was serious, you can acknowledge it in one clause and move on. if you don't know the reason, don't ask for it in a thank you card. the card is not the place to open an investigation. mention the gift, mention them, close it.

## 03. your vendor team

most couples skip this one entirely, which is exactly why it lands so hard when you don't. your vendor team worked a full shift on your wedding day. the planner, the photographer, the band, the catering lead, the person who flipped the room in twenty minutes while you were taking portraits.

a single specific sentence about their actual work means more than a generic thank you:

> "the way you handled the lighting change, we noticed."

one line specific to their work goes far. not "everything was beautiful." name the save. name the thing that could have gone sideways and didn't. vendors trade in referrals and portfolios, and a note that proves you saw the work is the kind of thing that gets screenshotted and kept.

### what to actually write to each vendor

1. name the moment you noticed. the rain plan, the reset, the hair fix, the one song they read the room on.
2. say what it changed for you. "we didn't have to think about it" is high praise to a working vendor.
3. if you mean it, offer the review or the referral, and then actually leave it. a review is a thank you note with a longer tail.

if you're still mid-planning and building your vendor list, keeping every contact and quote in one place makes this part much easier later. you can [add your vendor quotes free](/get-started) and track every cost, contact, and detail in one place, which means you'll have names, emails, and who-did-what when the cards come out.

## 04. divorced parents

this is the one that sits longest. divorced parents who held it together for you need separate notes, each one only about them.

> "watching you hold it together meant everything to us."

separate cards. separate envelopes. no "thank you both." no mention of the other parent in either note, even generously, even in passing. a line about the other one, however kind, turns your thank you into a comparison. keep each one only about them: what they did, what they gave, what you noticed about how they showed up.

if a stepparent was part of it, same rule. their own note, their own contribution, their own sentence.

## 05. the gift you returned

it didn't fit the apartment. it was a duplicate. it broke. it was, politely, not you.

if a gift got returned for any reason, you still write the note. acknowledge the thought:

> "the fact that you thought of us is the part that stays."

you do not have to lie about keeping it, and you do not have to confess the return either. the thank you is for the gesture, which was real and which you received. thank the thought, name something true about the giver, sign it.

## watch for

a few things that quietly turn a good note into a bad one:

- **the mass-produced feel.** if the same sentence appears in thirty cards, people notice when they compare at the next family thing.
- **apologizing in a thank you note.** "sorry this is so late" makes the note about you. just write it and send it.
- **the guilt sandwich** in a no-show note. warmth, then a jab, then warmth.
- **"thank you both" to divorced parents.** one card, two names, a lot of damage.
- **naming the registry item wrong.** check the list before you write. guessing is worse than being generic.
- **silence to vendors.** you were their whole day. ten minutes of writing buys a lot of goodwill.

## the short version

save this for when you are staring at a blank card at midnight and cannot find the words.

1. **cash gift:** name the amount and say how it lands.
2. **no-show guest:** thank the gesture, skip the guilt trip.
3. **vendor team:** one line specific to their work goes far.
4. **divorced parents:** separate notes, each one only about them.
5. **returned gift:** still write the note, acknowledge the thought.

work through the easy ones first to build momentum, then do the hard five in one sitting while you still have the pen in your hand. for more on handling the awkward stuff gracefully, there's more in [etiquette](/blog/category/etiquette), and if your vendor list is still a mess of screenshots and email threads, [start there](/get-started) so the names are ready when the envelopes are.
