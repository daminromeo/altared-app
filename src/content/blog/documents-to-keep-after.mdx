Eleven months after her wedding, a bride gets an email from a vendor's new office manager saying there is a $1,400 balance outstanding on the account. She knows she paid it. She remembers writing the check. But the folder with the paperwork went into a drawer the week after the honeymoon, and somewhere between the move and the holidays it stopped existing in any findable form.

That is the whole problem. The confetti settles and every folder gets tossed in a drawer, but six of those documents need to live somewhere you can actually find them.

None of this is dramatic. It is just the admin layer that most planning guides skip because it happens after the pretty part is over. Here is what to keep, why it matters, and how long it stays relevant.

## 1. your certified marriage license copy

The certified copy ($10-30 each, varies by county) is the one that actually changes your name.

People confuse three different pieces of paper here, so it is worth being precise:

- **The marriage license** is the permission slip you get before the wedding.
- **The signed license** is what your officiant files after the ceremony.
- **The certified marriage certificate copy** is what your county issues afterward, with a raised seal or official stamp. That is the document institutions accept.

A photo of the signed license on your phone will not move anything forward. The Social Security Administration wants the certified copy. So does the DMV, your passport agency, most banks, and usually your employer's benefits team.

### order more than one

Order two or three certified copies while you are already at the counter. At $10-30 each, the extra copies are cheap compared to what it costs in time to request one later, especially if you need to mail a form to a records office and wait on processing while your new passport application sits in limbo.

Then put the originals somewhere that is not a wedding folder. A fireproof box, a safe deposit box, or the same place you keep your birth certificate and Social Security card. Scan one and store the digital version too, but know that the scan is for your reference, not for official use.

## 2. every signed vendor contract

Keep every signed contract for at least 3 years, in case a deposit dispute or billing error comes up.

Your signed vendor contracts are your paper trail if a billing dispute comes up years later. Three years sounds excessive until you consider what actually happens in that window. A photography studio changes ownership and the new owners cannot locate your file. A venue's accounting system migrates and your deposit disappears from the ledger. A vendor sends a collections notice for a balance you settled in cash.

In every one of those scenarios, the signed contract is the only document that establishes what was agreed to. It tells you what the deposit amount was, whether it was refundable, what the cancellation terms said, what the overtime rate was, and whether that second shooter was included or billed separately.

### what to keep with the contract

The contract alone is only half the record. Store it alongside:

1. Every amendment or addendum you signed, including the ones sent as a quick email confirmation.
2. The email thread where you negotiated anything that differs from the standard terms.
3. The final headcount confirmation you sent to your caterer and venue.
4. Any change order for a date, time, or package change.

If you negotiated something verbally and the vendor confirmed it by text, screenshot the text and file it with the contract. A text message that says "yes, we will waive the cake cutting fee" has saved couples real money when the final invoice arrived with the fee on it anyway. If you want more on reading contracts before you sign them, the [contracts section of our blog](/blog/category/contracts) goes deeper on the specific clauses worth arguing over.

## 3. your wedding insurance declarations page

Wedding insurance claims can be filed weeks out, so hold the declarations page until the claim window closes.

Your insurance declarations page is live until the claim window closes. The declarations page is the one-page summary at the front of your policy: policy number, coverage limits, effective dates, deductible, and the carrier's claims contact. It is what you reach for when something has gone wrong and you need to act fast.

The timing matters more than people realize. Most wedding-day problems that trigger a claim do not announce themselves on the wedding day. The rental company invoices you three weeks later for a damaged linen order. The videographer stops responding and you realize the footage is not coming. The venue sends a bill for a scuffed floor. By the time you learn about the issue, you may be well into the claim window already.

Note two dates when you file this document away: when the coverage period ends, and the deadline for filing a claim. Those are usually not the same date. Once the claim window has genuinely closed, this is the one document on the list you can comfortably let go.

## 4. your paid final invoices

The paid invoice is proof that a balance is settled, critical if a vendor ever claims you still owe.

Your paid invoices prove a zero balance if a vendor ever says otherwise. This is the document that solves the $1,400 problem from the top of this post. A contract proves what you agreed to pay. A paid invoice proves you paid it.

What you want is an invoice that clearly shows a zero balance, or a receipt tied to the payment. Not a quote. Not an estimate. Not a payment schedule showing what is due. Those are different documents and they will not help you.

### match the payment method to the proof

For each vendor, keep the paid invoice plus whatever documents the payment:

- Credit card statement line showing the charge
- Bank transfer confirmation or Zelle receipt
- Canceled check image from your bank
- Venmo or payment app receipt with the date and amount

Cash payments are the risky category. If you paid a vendor in cash, get a written, signed receipt before you walk away. Without it, there is no record on either side, and you are relying entirely on the vendor's bookkeeping being perfect a year from now.

## 5. your venue permit

Some venues pull event permits in your name, keep a copy in case a noise or liability question surfaces later.

Your venue permit protects you if a noise or liability question surfaces post-wedding. This is the one most couples do not even know exists, because it gets handled quietly during the planning process.

Depending on where you married, there may be a special event permit, a noise variance, a temporary alcohol permit, a tent permit, or a parking authorization attached to your wedding. Backyard weddings, barn and farm venues, park and beach ceremonies, and private estate rentals are the most likely to involve permits pulled in a client's name rather than the venue's.

That matters because if a neighbor files a noise complaint after the fact, or a municipality follows up on a permit condition, the person named on the permit is the one who gets the letter. Having the actual document (with the permitted hours, the conditions, and the issuing authority on it) is what lets you respond in five minutes instead of five weeks.

Ask your venue directly: "were any permits pulled for our event, and whose name is on them?" Then ask for a copy. Most venues will send it without hesitation.

## 6. a written gift log

A written gift log protects you if a large cash gift is ever questioned for tax or estate purposes.

A written gift log matters more than people expect, especially for large cash gifts. Most couples track gifts informally while writing thank-you notes, then delete the list once the last card goes out. Keep it instead.

The log does not need to be sophisticated. A spreadsheet with four columns works:

1. Name of the giver
2. Date received
3. Amount or item
4. Format (cash, check, transfer, registry item)

The reason to hold onto it: if a large amount of cash lands in your account around your wedding date, documentation of where it came from is what distinguishes a gift from income or an undocumented loan. That question comes up with mortgage lenders reviewing your deposits, in estate conversations years later, or if a family member's finances ever get reviewed. A dated log written at the time is worth significantly more than a reconstructed memory.

## red flags to watch for

Some of these are easy to miss in the post-wedding blur.

- **A vendor who will not send a paid invoice.** If you have settled the balance and they are vague about sending confirmation, push until you have it in writing.
- **"We'll just handle it on the day."** Anything agreed to verbally and never documented is the thing most likely to reappear as a line item on a final bill.
- **A final invoice with charges you never saw in the contract.** Overtime, delivery, travel, cake cutting, and corkage fees have a habit of appearing late. Compare the final invoice against the signed contract line by line before you pay.
- **A venue that gets cagey about permits.** If nobody can tell you whose name is on an event permit, assume it might be yours and get a copy.
- **Only digital copies of the marriage license.** A scan is not a certified copy and will be rejected.
- **Deleting the gift list after thank-you notes.** The one moment you will want it is years out, long after it is reconstructible.

## how to actually store all of this

Two places, one physical and one digital.

Physical: a single labeled folder or envelope with your certified marriage license copies and any original signed documents. Keep it where you keep other vital records, not in the box of wedding keepsakes.

Digital: one cloud folder named something searchable, with subfolders per vendor. Each vendor subfolder holds the signed contract, the paid invoice, and the payment confirmation. Add your insurance declarations page, your venue permit, and your gift log at the top level. The whole thing takes about 40 minutes to set up and it is the difference between resolving a dispute in one email and spending a weekend reconstructing a year of transactions.

## the short version

1. Order two or three certified marriage license copies at $10-30 each, and store them with your vital records, not your wedding keepsakes.
2. Keep every signed vendor contract for at least 3 years, along with amendments and the email threads behind them.
3. Hold your insurance declarations page until the claim window closes, and note that date somewhere.
4. Save the paid final invoice for every vendor, matched with the payment confirmation.
5. Ask your venue whose name is on any event permits, and get a copy.
6. Keep the gift log after the thank-you notes are done, especially for large cash gifts.
