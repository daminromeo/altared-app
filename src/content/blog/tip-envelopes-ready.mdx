Picture the morning of the wedding. The venue deposit cleared months ago. The photographer is booked, the caterer signed, the band confirmed. You are getting your hair done, someone hands you a coffee, and then your coordinator leans over and asks, quietly, "Do you have the tip envelopes ready?"

And you freeze. Because no one told you about the tip envelopes.

This is the line nobody puts in their budget. You've got the venue deposit handled, the photographer booked, the caterer signed. But did anyone tell you to set aside $800 to $2,000 in cash envelopes for the day itself? Probably not, because it never shows up on a single contract. It just lands, all at once, on the one day you have the least attention to spare.

## Why tipping catches almost everyone off guard

Tipping at weddings isn't optional. It's expected, and it lands all at once. That combination is what makes it dangerous for your budget.

Every other big cost has a paper trail. The venue sends an invoice. The photographer sends a contract with a payment schedule. The caterer gives you a per-head number and a due date. You can see those coming from a mile away, and you plan for them.

Tips don't work that way. They don't appear in the original quote. There's no invoice, no due date, no reminder email. The expectation just exists, quietly, in the background of the whole industry, and it becomes real the moment your vendors show up to work.

So the couple who budgeted carefully for twelve months suddenly needs $1,500 in cash they never accounted for. That money has to come from somewhere, and by the day of the wedding, there's nothing left to trim. You can't renegotiate the caterer the morning of. The only options are scrambling for an ATM or shorting people who worked hard for you. Neither feels good.

## Eight envelopes, minimum

Here's the part that surprises people most: it's not one tip. It's a stack of them.

Think about everyone touching your day. Your photographer, second shooter, caterer staff, band or DJ, hair and makeup team, day-of coordinator, florist delivery crew. That's a lot of envelopes, and nobody lists it in the original quote.

Plan on eight envelopes minimum, cash, day-of. Here's a realistic breakdown of who gets one and the range you're looking at, generally $50 to $500 each depending on the role and how they were paid.

1. **Photographer** — often $50 to $200 if they own the business, more if you want to. Owners don't strictly need a tip, but many couples give one for standout work.
2. **Second shooter** — $50 to $100. They worked the same long day for a fraction of the pay.
3. **Caterer / banquet staff** — this is often the biggest line. Check your contract first, because many caterers build a service charge or gratuity in. If they don't, plan on $20 to $50 per server, which adds up fast at a big reception.
4. **Bartenders** — $20 to $50 each, unless a tip jar or gratuity is already covered.
5. **Band or DJ** — $50 to $150 for a DJ, and $25 to $50 per band member.
6. **Hair and makeup team** — 15% to 20% of that service, the same way you'd tip at a salon.
7. **Day-of coordinator** — $50 to $200. They are the person keeping the whole thing from falling apart.
8. **Florist delivery and setup crew** — $10 to $50 for the people hauling and arranging, especially for big installs.

Add officiant, transportation drivers, and the venue's setup or cleanup crew, and you can easily push past eight. Every one of those is a separate envelope, a separate name, a separate amount you have to figure out in advance. That's how you get from "a tip" to $800 to $2,000 in one shot.

### The service charge trap

Read your contracts closely before you plan a single tip, because this is where couples double-pay or feel guilted into paying twice.

A "service charge" or "administrative fee" is not the same as a gratuity, and it often does not go to the staff at all. A 20% service charge on a $10,000 catering bill is $2,000, and if you assume that covers the servers, you might skip their tips entirely, or you might tip on top without realizing you already paid a hefty fee. Neither is what you want. If the contract says "gratuity included," your staff are covered. If it says "service charge," ask the vendor point blank where that money goes and whether the team still expects a tip.

## Red flags and things to watch for

A few patterns tend to blow up the tip budget or catch people at the worst possible moment. Watch for these:

- **"Gratuity not included" buried in the fine print.** This is the phrase that turns a $6,000 catering quote into a $7,200 real cost. Search every contract for it before you sign, not the week of.
- **Vendors who only take cash tips.** Most do. You cannot Venmo the banquet staff at 10pm, and the ATM at the venue may be empty or nonexistent. Get the cash days ahead.
- **No one assigned to hand out envelopes.** You will be busy getting married. Give the stack to your coordinator or a trusted family member with a written list of names and amounts, sealed and labeled.
- **Assuming the quote is the final number.** The quote covers the contract. It does not cover the line items that don't show up until the day of. A "$25,000 wedding" with tips is often a $26,500 wedding, and that gap is exactly the kind of thing that wrecks a budget you thought was locked.
- **Forgetting the small crews.** The florist's delivery driver, the rental drop-off team, the shuttle driver. They're easy to overlook and awkward to skip when they're standing right there.

## How to actually plan for it

The fix is simple, it just requires doing it early, while you still have room to adjust.

**A worked example.** Say you're planning a mid-size wedding. Your caterer contract has no gratuity, so you set aside $600 for the banquet and bar staff. Photographer and second shooter, $200. DJ, $100. Hair and makeup at 18%, roughly $130. Coordinator, $150. Florist crew, $40. Officiant, $100. That's about $1,320 in cash, right in the middle of the $800 to $2,000 range, and none of it appeared on a contract. Now imagine finding that out the night before instead of six months out.

Here's the order of operations:

1. **Pull every contract and search for "gratuity" and "service charge."** Mark which vendors are already covered so you don't double-tip.
2. **List every human who works your day**, including the ones not on a contract (drivers, setup crews).
3. **Assign a dollar amount to each** using the ranges above, and total it.
4. **Add that total to your master budget now**, as a real line item, not a maybe.
5. **Get the cash a few days early**, split it into labeled envelopes, and hand the stack to your coordinator with a list.

The whole thing takes an afternoon, and it moves the tip line from a day-of ambush to a number you decided on calmly, months in advance.
