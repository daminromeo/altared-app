You found the dress. The consultant clipped it at the back, you turned toward the mirror, and everyone in the room got a little teary. The tag says a number you talked yourself into. You sign, you put down the deposit, you tell your group chat.

Then the invoices start.

Here is the thing nobody says out loud on the pickup day: the dress tag is not the final number. Not even close. Between the day you say yes to the gown and the day you walk down the aisle, five separate charges show up, and none of them are in the original quote. They land later, usually when your budget spreadsheet is already locked and you have mentally moved on to flowers.

So before you book a single fitting, here is exactly what to expect, with real dollar ranges, so nothing catches you off guard.

## 1. Alterations: budget $500 to $800 separately

Almost no gown quote includes alterations, and almost no bride finds this out until the seamstress hands over the estimate at the first fitting.

Alterations alone run $500 to $800 at most boutiques. That is a standard range for a standard gown, and it can climb higher fast if your dress has:

1. A hem that needs shortening (especially with lace or horsehair trim)
2. A bustle, which is often priced per point (some gowns need 6 or more points)
3. Taking in or letting out the bodice at multiple seams
4. Adding cups, boning, or a modesty panel
5. Reworking straps, sleeves, or a neckline

Each of those is usually a separate line item. A simple hem and a bustle might land near the bottom of the range. A heavily beaded gown that needs the seams opened and reset by hand can push you past the top of it.

Why so much? Because a wedding dress is not a dress you buy in your size and wear. It is a garment ordered a size up (or two) and then sculpted to your body over several appointments. That labor is real, and it is skilled, and it is almost never bundled into the price on the tag.

### plan the timeline, not just the money

Alterations also cost you calendar space. Most boutiques want your gown in hand three to four months before the wedding, with fittings spaced a few weeks apart. If you order late or lose or gain weight during planning (common, no shame), expect rush fees on top of the base cost. Ask about the rush policy the day you order, not the week you panic.

## 2. Veil and accessories: the veil alone is $150 to $600

The veil feels like an afterthought until you price one. A veil alone runs $150 to $600, and cathedral-length or custom-edged veils sit at the top of that.

And the veil is only the start of the accessories line. Once you are standing in the boutique, the consultant will start layering things on, because it genuinely completes the look, and because that is the job. So plan for:

- **Shoes** you will actually wear (and possibly a flat backup for the reception)
- **Jewelry**, whether that is earrings, a bracelet, or something borrowed
- **Hairpieces**, combs, or a headband
- A **belt or sash** if your gown takes one

Individually these feel small. Together they are a category, not a footnote. A $300 veil, $120 shoes, and $150 in jewelry is $570 you did not write down when you signed for the dress.

The move here is to give accessories their own line in your budget before you shop, so the number is a decision and not a surprise. You can add each quote as you go and [track every attire line for free](/get-started) instead of keeping receipts in a text thread.

## 3. Bridal undergarments: $80 to $300, and required

Here is one that gets people every time. Corsets, bras, boning, shapewear, and slips add $80 to $300, and your seamstress will ask for them at every single fitting.

That is not a suggestion. The undergarments change how the gown sits, so alterations done without them are alterations done wrong. If you show up to a fitting in the wrong bra, the seamstress cannot pin accurately, and you will pay to redo the work.

So this cost is doubly locked in: you have to buy them, and you have to buy them early enough to wear them to the first fitting. Buy the real pieces before appointment one, not the week of the wedding.

### what your seamstress actually needs

Ask your boutique or seamstress for a short list at your first appointment. It usually includes the exact bra style, any shapewear, and the shoes you plan to wear (heel height changes the hem). Bring all of it to every fitting. A single reusable garment bag with your fitting kit inside saves you from the "wait, where are my shoes" scramble three times over.

## 4. Pressing and steaming: $75 to $200 the week before

Your gown will not arrive from alterations ready to wear. It will have been handled, hung, folded into a garment bag, and possibly boxed. It needs pressing and steaming, and venues rarely steam on-site.

Plan $75 to $200 the week before the wedding. That covers professional pressing or steaming of the gown, and often the veil too, which wrinkles worse than the dress.

A few things people get wrong here:

- **Do not steam it yourself the night before.** A single water spot or a scorched panel is a disaster with no time to fix it. This is not the DIY moment.
- **Confirm who handles it.** Some boutiques offer steaming as part of pickup, some charge for it, and some hand you the box and wish you luck. Ask when you order.
- **Coordinate the timing.** The gown should be steamed close to the wedding date so it does not re-wrinkle hanging in a hotel closet. Build it into the week-of schedule.

If your gown ships to your venue city or you are traveling, factor in that you may be paying a local pressing service you have never met, sight unseen. Get that quote in advance too.

## 5. The tip for the people who built your gown

Months of work went into your dress, and two people carried most of it: the bridal consultant who guided you through it and the seamstress who fit it to your body. A 15 to 20% tip is standard for both.

This is the cost brides forget most, because tips do not appear on any invoice. There is no line item that says "gratuity." It is just expected, and it is genuinely earned. On $600 of alterations, a 15% tip is $90. On the consultant's service, plan a similar percentage or a flat thank-you amount depending on your boutique's norms.

If cash tipping feels awkward or you are unsure of the etiquette, a good rule is: the more custom and hands-on the work, the more a tip matters. A seamstress who reworked a beaded bodice by hand deserves the full 15 to 20%. When in doubt, ask the boutique directly what is customary. ## Red flags to watch for before you sign

None of these five costs are scams. They are normal. What is not normal is a boutique that hides them or gets cagey when you ask. Watch for:

- **"We'll figure out alterations later."** Push for a rough range in writing before you commit to the gown.
- **No rush policy explained upfront.** If they will not tell you the rush fee schedule, assume it is high.
- **Pressure to buy every accessory the same day.** You can love the dress and still take the veil home to think about it.
- **Vague answers on steaming.** "The venue will handle it" without confirmation is how gowns show up wrinkled.
- **A quote that only lists the gown price.** Ask, "what else will I pay between now and the wedding?" A good boutique answers honestly.

The pattern is simple: the more a boutique volunteers about the extra costs, the more you can trust the number on the tag.

## Add it up before you fall in love

Here is what the dress actually costs, in add-ons, before you touch the gown price itself:

- Alterations: $500 to $800
- Veil: $150 to $600 (plus shoes, jewelry, hairpieces)
- Undergarments: $80 to $300
- Pressing and steaming: $75 to $200
- Tips: 15 to 20% of the service

That is real money stacked on top of a number you already stretched for. The couples who stay sane are the ones who wrote these lines down before boutique shopping, not after.

So do this before your first fitting:

1. **Add a separate "attire extras" line** to your budget, aside from the gown price.
2. **Get alterations, steaming, and rush policies in writing** the day you order.
3. **Buy your real undergarments early** and bring them (plus shoes) to every fitting.
4. **Give accessories their own number** so the veil and jewelry are decisions, not surprises.
5. **Set aside 15 to 20% for tips** for the consultant and seamstress now.
