say you send one message to the group chat: "hair and makeup starts at 9, here's the artist's link." within an hour you have four replies, two of them private. one person already booked her own stylist. one wants to know if she has to do makeup too. one is quietly worried about what this costs her. and one is waiting to see what everyone else says before she answers.

none of that is drama. it's just a question nobody answered first.

hair and makeup is where who-pays gets awkward fast. decide it before the group chat does. the good news is that the rule underneath it is simple and it holds up in almost every situation: if you require it, you pay for it. everything else is a variation on that.

## the rule that settles most of it

the fastest way to think about wedding party beauty is to ask one question before you send any link or any time slot: am i asking for a specific look, or am i offering a convenience?

those are two completely different asks, and they come with two completely different invoices.

asking for a specific look is a requirement. you are directing how someone shows up in your photos, which means you've moved it from their personal grooming into your production. offering a convenience means the artist is already on site, the chairs are already booked, and anyone who wants in can have a slot.

the mistake most couples make is sending a message that sounds like the second one but functions like the first. "everyone's getting their hair done at 9 :)" reads as an invitation. it lands as an instruction. be clearer than that, in writing, early.

## 01. if you require it, you pay

asking for a specific look makes it your cost, not theirs.

this covers more than you'd think. a uniform style across the group. a specific artist. "no bold lip." a particular hair direction so the veil sits right. an 8am call time that rules out doing it themselves. any of those are you deciding what someone's head looks like on your wedding day, and that's a line item on your budget, not theirs.

if the number gives you pause, you have two honest options and they are both fine:

1. keep the requirement and pay for it. budget it with your other day-of costs, not as an afterthought.
2. loosen the requirement so it's genuinely optional, then say so plainly. "do whatever you like with your hair, i just love you in it" is a real answer.

what doesn't work is keeping the requirement and hoping nobody adds up the cost. that's how resentment shows up at the rehearsal dinner.

## 02. if it's optional, each person pays

offer the booking, let them opt in, and they cover their own.

this is the version most couples land on, and it works well when the opt-in is real. you hold the slots, you share the artist's pricing and the time grid, and people choose. someone who wants hair but not makeup takes hair. someone who does her own face beautifully does her own face. someone who can't swing it this month says no without having to explain why.

a few things make the opt-in real instead of theoretical:

1. share the artist's actual pricing before you ask for a yes, not after.
2. give a deadline that isn't the week of the wedding, so nobody's deciding under pressure.
3. let people take part of the service rather than all of it, if the artist allows it.
4. make the no-thanks option visibly fine by saying it out loud.
5. collect answers individually, not in the group chat, where people copy whoever replied first.

### the quiet pressure problem

a group chat is a terrible place to opt out of spending money. the first person to say yes sets the norm, and everyone after her is now the one person being difficult. if you want honest answers, ask for them one at a time.

## 03. the trial is yours

trials are for your look. a wedding party trial is yours to cover if you want one.

your own trial is non-negotiable. you're testing longevity, photos, how the hair holds under a veil, and whether you and the artist communicate well. that's your cost, and nobody expects otherwise.

the part people skip: if you want a wedding party trial, because you're matching a look or want to see the whole group together, that's your request and your cost too. you initiated it. you're paying for the certainty.

if someone in the wedding party wants her own trial just for her own peace of mind, that's hers. the dividing line is the same as everywhere else here: whoever asked for it, pays for it.

## 04. the tip follows the booking

if you booked and paid for it, the tip comes from you too.

tips are where the awkwardness quietly reappears at the end of a long morning, usually while someone is zipping a dress. whoever books, tips. if you paid for the full group, you handle the full tip. if everyone paid for their own service, each person handles her own.

decide this in advance, put cash in a labeled envelope, and hand the envelope to someone who is not getting married that day. the point is that nobody is doing arithmetic in a hotel room with wet hair.

### check the contract first

some artists build gratuity into the contract or the travel fee. read before you assume. ask for the all-in number so you know exactly what's already covered and what isn't. if you want more on reading beauty and vendor paperwork closely, our [contracts posts](/blog/category/contracts) go deeper.

## 05. touch-ups are a booking extra

an artist staying through photos is a booking extra, so it goes on your invoice.

if you want an artist to stay past the getting-ready window, through portraits, through the ceremony, through to the first dance, that's additional time you are buying. it isn't a favor the artist throws in because she's still around. it's hours, and it's billed as hours.

this is your cost because it's your timeline that requires it. your wedding party didn't ask the artist to wait for the golden hour photos. you did.

if you don't want to pay for stay-on time, the fix is cheap and works: build a small touch-up kit, hand it to one person, and tell her where it lives. blotting papers, the lipstick, a few pins. done.

## red flags to watch for

- a quote that only covers the couple, with the wedding party listed as "additional" and no pricing attached. ask for the all-in number before you book.
- a group chat message that implies a requirement but never says who pays. that gap gets filled by whoever assumes hardest.
- travel, early start, or parking fees that only surface on the final invoice. ask what triggers each one.
- an artist who won't put the schedule in writing. a time grid is part of the service, not a bonus.
- silence from one person in the wedding party. that's usually a budget worry, not indifference. ask privately.
- assuming trial pricing applies to the wedding day rate. confirm which is which.

## the short version

1. if you require a specific look, you pay.
2. if it's genuinely optional, offer the booking and let each person cover her own.
3. your trial is yours. a wedding party trial you requested is also yours.
4. whoever books, tips.
5. stay-on touch-up time is a booking extra, so it goes on your invoice.
6. say all of this in writing before the first slot gets claimed.
