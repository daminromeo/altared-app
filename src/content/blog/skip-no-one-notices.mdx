Picture the end of your reception. The band is packing up, someone's aunt is dancing to a song only she can hear, and the tables are covered in half-finished drinks and abandoned little boxes of almonds. Those boxes cost you $4-12 each. You bought 120 of them. Do the math and that's a chunk of money sitting there, uneaten, about to get swept into a trash bag by the venue's closing crew.

Nobody remembers the favors. Nobody remembered the printed menu either, the one that cost $3-8 a card and got read exactly once before it became a coaster. These are real line items on real quotes, and the vendors who sell them to you will never suggest you cut them. That's not their job. Their job is to fill the quote.

Your job is different. Your job is to figure out which costs your guests actually register and which ones they don't, so you can move money toward the stuff people remember: the food, the music, an open bar that actually stays open. Here are five costs you can skip, roughly in the order they'll show up on your invoices.

## the five costs guests never register

Save this list for your next vendor meeting. Each one is a genuine line item, and each one is invisible to the people you're throwing the party for.

1. **Printed menus, $3-8 per card.** Guests read them once, then ignore them. If you're doing a plated dinner, the server tells everyone their options anyway. A single framed menu at the entrance or one per table (not per person) does the same job for a fraction of the cost.
2. **Guest favors, $4-12 each.** Most get left on the table by the end of the night. The custom koozies, the tiny jars of honey, the personalized matchbooks: they feel thoughtful in the planning spreadsheet and get abandoned in practice.
3. **Cocktail hour florals, $800-2,000.** During cocktail hour, your guests are talking, drinking, and finding their friends. They are not looking up at arrangements. This is the least-watched hour of your entire wedding, floral-wise.
4. **Extra cake tiers, $200-500 each.** Tiers that exist purely for the photo. You can get the exact same look for less (more on the dummy-tier trick below).
5. **Charger plates, $3-6 each to rent.** These are the decorative plates your actual plate sits on. They get removed before the first course arrives. Guests interact with them for approximately the length of time it takes to sit down.

Let's break down why each of these is safe to cut, and what the money looks like when you add it up.

## printed menus and favors: the paper-and-trinket trap

### menus

Printed menus feel like a nice touch until you count them. At $3-8 per card across, say, 120 guests, you're looking at $360 to $960 for pieces of cardstock that get glanced at and set aside. If you're serving a buffet or stations, the menu is even less useful, because guests can see the food.

If you love the look of a menu on the table, print one per table instead of one per seat, or hand-letter a single large menu board near the entrance. Same aesthetic, a tenth of the cost.

### favors

Favors are the most emotionally sticky item on this list, because cutting them can feel cheap. It isn't. Walk any reception hall at 11pm and you'll see the truth: most favors are left on the table by end of night. Guests came for you, the dancing, and the late-night fries. They did not come for a monogrammed bottle opener.

At $4-12 each for 120 guests, favors run roughly $480 to $1,440. If you genuinely want to give something, make it consumable and communal (a late-night snack, a coffee cart) so it gets used instead of abandoned.

## the florals no one looks up at

Cocktail hour florals are the quiet budget killer, because they're bundled into a larger floral quote where they're easy to miss. Your florist lists arrangements for the cocktail space at $800-2,000, and it looks like a normal line among many normal lines.

Here's the thing about cocktail hour: it's the one stretch of the night where every single guest is standing, mingling, and holding a drink. Their eyes are at face level, on each other. Nobody is admiring a raised arrangement on a high-top table. Reuse your ceremony florals here (have a member of your team move them during the transition), or skip the standalone cocktail arrangements entirely and put that $800-2,000 toward reception centerpieces, which people actually sit and look at for hours.

## the cake trick that saves you real money

Extra cake tiers cost $200-500 each, and a lot of couples pay for tiers they will never eat. A five-tier cake for 100 guests is mostly for the photo. You don't need that much cake, and you're paying per serving for tiers that get boxed up or tossed.

Ask your baker for a **dummy tier**: a styrofoam form iced to look identical to the real thing. It photographs exactly the same, costs a fraction of a real tier, and lets you serve a sheet cake from the kitchen to feed everyone. Your cake looks tall and dramatic in every photo, and you're not paying $200-500 per tier for cake that ends up in a to-go container.

### how this adds up

Say you were quoted a four-tier cake and you swap the top two for dummies:

1. Two dummy tiers instead of real ones saves you $400-1,000.
2. A back-of-house sheet cake covers the actual servings for less than the display would have cost.
3. Your photos look identical.

That's real money for zero visible difference.

## charger plates and other clear-before-the-salad costs

Charger plates are the decorative plate that sits under your dinner plate. They run $3-6 each to rent, and here's the punchline: they get removed before the first course arrives. The server takes them away when they bring the salad. Guests see them for the time it takes to sit down and unfold a napkin.

At $3-6 each for 120 place settings, that's $360 to $720 for a decorative moment that lasts about ninety seconds. If your table already has a nice linen, a folded napkin, and centerpieces, no one will notice the missing charger. Not one guest will go home and say the tablescape felt incomplete.

## watch for: how these costs sneak onto your quotes

Vendors aren't villains here, but you should know how these line items hide, so you can catch them before you sign.

- **Bundled florals.** Cocktail hour arrangements often get folded into one big floral total. Ask for an itemized quote so you can see the $800-2,000 broken out and decide for yourself.
- **"Per person" language.** Menus and chargers get quoted per guest, which makes each one look tiny ($3-8, $3-6) until you multiply by your headcount. Always multiply.
- **Cake priced by the tier.** If your quote lists tiers without mentioning dummy options, ask directly whether display tiers can be faux. Bakers rarely volunteer this.
- **Favors added as a "finishing touch."** These often appear late, after you're emotionally attached to the vendor. A $4-12 add-on per guest is still a four-figure line for a big list.
- **Anything described as "for the photos."** Photos are wonderful, but ask whether the same shot is achievable for less. It usually is.

The pattern across all five: small-sounding per-unit prices that balloon at scale, and items that vendors have no incentive to talk you out of. Reading your own [contracts and invoices](/blog/category/contracts) carefully is where the savings actually happen.

## the quick version

Here's what to remember when you sit down with your budget:

1. **Printed menus** ($3-8 per card): print one per table or a single board, not one per seat.
2. **Guest favors** ($4-12 each): skip them, or make it something consumable that gets used.
3. **Cocktail hour florals** ($800-2,000): reuse ceremony flowers or cut them; nobody looks up.
4. **Extra cake tiers** ($200-500 each): ask for a dummy tier and serve sheet cake from the kitchen.
5. **Charger plates** ($3-6 each): skip them; they're cleared before the salad anyway.

None of these are worth it if your budget is tight, and every dollar you pull out of them can go toward the things guests genuinely remember. Cutting all five on a 120-guest wedding can easily free up a few thousand dollars, which is the difference between an open bar that closes early and one that actually stays open.
