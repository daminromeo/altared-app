say it is the Tuesday before your wedding. the escort cards are printed, the chart is laminated at the venue, and a text comes in at 9:40pm: so sorry, we can't make it anymore. the group chat is already blowing up. your brain goes straight to the table that now has an empty chair in the middle of it.

someone just cancelled, and the seating chart is already printed. here is what to do, in order. the order matters more than the speed, because one of these steps has a deadline attached to it and the rest do not.

## step 1: confirm first

reply within 24 hours. something short: "got it, we'll take care of it from here."

that is the whole message. you are not asking why, you are not negotiating, you are not sending the "are you sure?? we'd love to have you" follow-up that makes a guest feel like they have to justify themselves. confirm with the guest so they feel heard and nothing gets awkward. the awkwardness almost always comes from silence, not from the cancellation itself.

there is a practical reason to close the loop fast too. until you confirm, you do not actually know what you are dealing with. is it one seat or two? is the plus-one still coming? is this the person driving three other guests? one reply gets you the real number, and the real number is what every other step depends on.

### if it is a close family member or the cancellation is upsetting

handle the feelings on your own timeline. handle the logistics on the vendor's timeline. those are two separate tracks and they do not have to move at the same speed. the "got it, we'll take care of it from here" text buys you room to do both.

## step 2: call catering immediately

this is the step with the clock on it. most caterers lock guest counts anywhere from three to fourteen days before the wedding, and the window closes faster than you think. call today, not tomorrow, not after you have redone the chart.

call, do not email. an email sent at 10pm on a Tuesday sits unread until Thursday, and Thursday might be on the wrong side of your cutoff. if you reach voicemail, leave a message with your date, your name, and the new count, then follow up in writing so there is a paper trail of when you told them.

what to actually say on the call:

1. your wedding date and couple name, first, so they can pull your file.
2. the exact revised headcount, not "one fewer" (counts get miscommunicated when you make them do math).
3. ask whether their guest-count deadline has already passed for your date.
4. ask what the change does to your invoice: credit, no change, or partial.
5. ask them to confirm the new number back to you in writing.
6. ask whether the plated meal selection changes anything (a cancelled entrée is not always the same line item as a cancelled head).

even if you are past the cutoff, make the call. some caterers will still adjust, some will swap the plate to a vendor meal, some will not move at all. you will not know which you are dealing with until you ask, and you cannot ask after the wedding.

## step 3: check the seat

now decide what happens to the chair itself. you have three options and each one has a ripple:

**pull the chair.** cleanest look at the table, no obvious gap. but it changes your rental count, and rentals have their own deadline that is not the same as catering's.

**fill it.** slide in someone from the b-list. this works best when the invite is genuinely warm and not transparently last-minute, and when the person you are adding actually fits the table. a b-list guest dropped into a table of college roommates is a long night for them.

**leave it.** sometimes the fastest and kindest answer, especially at a table of eight where pulling one chair makes the spacing look odd. an empty seat reads as a spare, not as an absence, once people are seated and talking.

each choice has a downstream effect on the table, so look at the neighbors before you decide. do not pick based on what is easiest for you at 10pm. pick based on what the table looks like from the inside.

## step 4: adjust the neighbors

here is the part people skip. if the gap splits a couple or a family, move the whole row rather than patching one chair.

patching means sliding one person over to close the hole. it feels efficient and it almost always creates a second, worse problem: a partner sitting across the table from their partner, a parent separated from a kid, a single guest suddenly marooned between two groups who already know each other.

shifting the full row or the full table side keeps the social groupings intact and costs you the same five minutes. redraw the table, not the seat.

### watch for these red flags

- **a caterer who says "just tell us the day before."** get that in writing. verbal flexibility is not a contract term, and the person who said it may not be the person working your wedding.
- **a chart that lives in exactly one place.** if the only current version is a printed board at the venue and a PDF in someone's downloads folder, you have no current version.
- **a b-list invite sent without a reason.** "we had a cancellation" is honest and fine. no explanation at all is what makes it sting.
- **changes made after the escort cards are already alphabetized.** if you move people, the cards have to move too, or your guests will be hunting for a table that no longer exists.
- **anyone telling you "we'll sort it on the day."** the day is the one time nobody has a spare ten minutes.

## step 5: tell your point person

update your day-of coordinator or point person with the revised chart before venue setup begins. not the morning of. before setup.

your coordinator is the one placing cards, counting chairs, and talking to the catering captain about final covers. if they are working from the old chart, they will set the room to the old chart, and you will be fixing it in a dress.

send them: the revised chart with a version date on the file name, the updated headcount you gave catering, and a one-line note about what changed ("table 7, two seats removed, row shifted left"). if you do not have a coordinator, this is whoever is running your timeline: a planner, a venue manager, or the friend who agreed to be the point person. tell them in the same message thread you have been using so nothing lives in a separate inbox.

## step 6: check the refund

finally, pull your catering and rental contracts now and find the guest-count cutoff clause. that is the number that tells you whether you get a credit.

search the document for the words "final count," "guaranteed count," or "guarantee." the clause usually tells you three things: the date the count locks, whether you can lower it after that date, and whether unused meals are credited or forfeited. rentals often have a separate clause with a different date, so check both contracts, not just the one you remember signing.

do this now, not after the wedding. a credit you find this week is a conversation with your caterer. a credit you find next month is a dispute. if the clause is vague or you cannot find it at all, that is your question for the caterer on the same call from step 2.

## the short version

save this for the moment it happens:

1. confirm with the guest within 24 hours: "got it, we'll take care of it from here."
2. call catering today, because most caterers lock guest counts three to fourteen days out.
3. decide the seat: pull the chair, fill it, or leave it.
4. if the gap splits a couple or a family, shift the whole row, not one seat.
5. send the revised chart to your coordinator before venue setup begins.
