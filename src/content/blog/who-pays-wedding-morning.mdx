say it's four months out and someone in the group chat asks, very politely, "so are we splitting the hotel suite?" nobody answers for six hours. then three people answer at once with three different assumptions. that silence is the whole problem: the wedding morning has its own bill, and almost nobody prices it until the week it lands.

here's the thing that's easy to miss. the morning gets planned like it's free time. it isn't. it's a venue (or a suite), a meal, a vehicle, extra hours on a photography contract, and a pile of small purchases that add up quietly. none of it shows up as a line item in most budget templates, which is exactly why it ambushes people.

so: here's who covers each line before anyone asks.

## the default rule, stated plainly

if you booked it, you pay for it.

that one sentence resolves about eighty percent of wedding morning money confusion. the wedding party isn't a group of co-hosts. they're guests you've asked to arrive early. when you book a room, a van, a breakfast order or an extra block of photo coverage, you're the one who signed, and the invoice follows the signature.

the exception that proves the rule is personal appearance. hair, makeup, attire and travel to the city are traditionally the wedding party's own expenses, though plenty of couples choose to cover some or all of it. that's a gift, not a default. the important part isn't which way you go, it's that you say which way you're going, in writing, early enough that people can plan.

## the five lines of the wedding morning

### 01. getting-ready suite → the couple

if you book it for the wedding party, it goes on your bill.

this is the one people fumble most often, because it hides inside a hotel room block. a room block is a convenience you negotiate for guests, and guests pay their own rooms. a getting-ready suite is different. it's a workspace you chose for its window light, its square footage, and its proximity to the venue. you picked it for the wedding, so it's a wedding cost.

a few things to settle when you book it:

1. check-in time. getting-ready suites are useless if you can't get in until 4pm, and early check-in is usually a separate charge or a separate negotiation.
2. whether the room is also someone's overnight room, or purely a day space.
3. how many people the space actually fits with chairs, mirrors, garment racks and a photographer moving around.
4. whether outside food and drink are allowed, and whether there's a fee attached to bringing them in.
5. who is responsible for damage and for checkout, since you will not be the person in that room at checkout time.

if you'd rather not carry the suite cost, there's a legitimate alternative: get ready somewhere you're already paying for. plenty of venues include a prep room in the rental. ask before you book a second space you don't need.

### 02. breakfast → whoever hosts

the suite host feeds whoever is getting ready there.

this is the cleanest line in the whole list. if you booked the room, you're hosting the room, and hosts feed people. that doesn't mean a catered spread. it means nobody who has been in a chair since 7am is left to figure out their own food while wearing a robe.

if a parent or a member of the wedding party books and pays for the suite, hosting shifts to them, and so does breakfast. same logic, different payer.

one practical note: plan the food around timing, not around photos. the person going first in the makeup chair eats at a completely different hour than the person going last, and a single delivery window usually misses one of them.

### 03. wedding party ride → the couple

getting everyone to the ceremony is a wedding cost, not theirs.

if the ceremony is twenty minutes from the suite and parking is a nightmare, you can't reasonably ask five people in formalwear to sort out their own arrival. transportation you arrange is transportation you pay for. that includes the ride back from the ceremony to the reception if those are separate sites.

what's not yours: their flights, their train tickets, their drive in from out of state. getting to the wedding city is on them. getting from the suite to the aisle is on you.

### 04. getting-ready photos → the couple

morning coverage adds hours to the photographer's booking.

this is the sneakiest one, because it doesn't feel like a purchase. it feels like "the photographer will just come earlier." the photographer will come earlier, and their contract will reflect it. so will the second shooter's, if you want simultaneous coverage in two getting-ready locations, which is a genuinely common gap people discover a week out.

before you lock a start time, ask your photographer what their hours look like end to end, and get the all-in number for the coverage you actually want rather than the package number you first quoted. if morning coverage pushes you into a higher tier, you want to know that while you can still choose between it and something else. (more on reading these contracts closely in our [contracts](/blog/category/contracts) posts.)

### 05. emergency kit → the couple

safety pins, stain pen, snacks. small, but it is yours.

nobody's wedding is saved by a single safety pin, but a surprising number of mornings are rescued by the box that had one. deodorant, blister bandages, a lint roller, tissues, a phone charger, clear nail polish, headache medicine, a sewing kit, water, and snacks that don't leave crumbs or stains.

it's the smallest line on this list and it's still yours. don't outsource it to whoever happens to have a big purse.

## watch for these

- **"we'll figure it out that morning."** you will not. you will be in a chair with wet hair while someone tries to venmo a hotel.
- **a suite booked under one person's name with an unspoken plan to split it.** either it's split and everyone agreed in advance, or it's yours. there is no third option that survives contact with a credit card hold.
- **vendors quoting a start time instead of a total.** "we'll start at 9" is not a price. ask what the full coverage costs.
- **assuming the room block rate applies to the suite.** different room type, often a different rate, sometimes a different cancellation policy.
- **telling the wedding party what you're covering only after they've booked something.** if you're gifting hair and makeup, say so before they schedule their own appointment elsewhere.
- **early check-in treated as a favor.** get it confirmed on the reservation, not promised over the phone.

## how to settle it before anyone asks

send one short message to the wedding party, months out, that says exactly three things: what you are covering, what they are covering, and when each thing needs to be booked. that's it. people are overwhelmingly fine with paying their share. what they resent is finding out late.

then put the morning in your actual budget as its own section, not as a rounding error inside "venue" or "photography."

**the short version:**

1. getting-ready suite: the couple. if you book it for the wedding party, it goes on your bill.
2. breakfast: whoever hosts the suite.
3. wedding party ride to the ceremony: the couple.
4. getting-ready photo coverage: the couple, and confirm the added hours in the contract.
5. emergency kit: the couple. small, but it is yours.
6. hair, makeup, attire and travel into town: traditionally the wedding party's, unless you say otherwise in writing.
