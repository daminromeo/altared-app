two photographers send you quotes on the same Tuesday. one is a three-page PDF with a logo, a mood board, and a number at the bottom: $4,200. the other is a paragraph in an email: "$3,800 for full day coverage, 50% retainer to hold the date."

the email looks cheaper by $400. so you start mentally booking the email one.

then you read closer. the $4,200 PDF includes eight hours, a second shooter, an engagement session, and all edited files delivered in eight weeks. the $3,800 email means "full day," which turns out to mean six hours, solo, with a $350 travel fee added later because your venue is 42 miles out, and an album the first photographer threw in for free listed at $600 as an add-on.

the cheaper quote was never cheaper. it was just written differently.

this is the part of wedding planning nobody warns you about. vendors are not trying to trick you (most of them, anyway). they just build quotes in whatever format their business grew into, and there's no industry standard. one bundles everything. one itemizes everything. one buries travel fees, one doesn't mention a second shooter, one says "full day" and means six hours. comparing them line by line is the only way to know which is actually cheaper.

here are the six things to match before you sign either one.

## 1. strip both quotes to the base rate

start by getting both numbers naked. the base rate is the flat fee for the vendor's time and core service, before tax, before gratuity, before travel, before any add-on.

this sounds obvious. it is not, because quotes rarely label it clearly. you'll see "package total," "investment," "all-in," "starting at." none of those words mean the same thing twice.

what to do: write down one number per vendor on a piece of paper or in your [budget tracker](/get-started). if a quote gives you a bundled total, email and ask directly: "what's the base rate before tax, gratuity, and travel?" any good vendor answers this in one line.

### why this matters more than it seems

sales tax on services varies by state and sometimes by county. a quote that includes tax looks higher than one that doesn't, even when the underlying service costs less. gratuity policies differ too. some caterers build in a 20% service charge (which, notably, is not always a tip to staff), some leave it to you. if you compare totals instead of base rates, you're comparing apples to a tax code.

once you have two clean base rates, you have a starting line. everything after this is adjustment.

## 2. match the exact hours covered

"full day" is the single most misleading phrase in vendor quotes. one photographer's full day is twelve hours, ceremony prep through last dance. another's is six, and they're packing up during the cake cutting.

one may say 8 hrs, one may say "full day." ask both for the exact clock-out time.

the question to send, word for word: "what time would you arrive, and what time would you leave, based on a ceremony at [your time]?"

now you can do real math. if vendor A is $4,200 for eight hours, that's $525 an hour. if vendor B is $3,800 for six hours, that's $633 an hour. the "cheaper" quote costs more per hour of coverage, and you're going to want those extra two hours. overtime rates, by the way, are usually higher than the base hourly rate. ask what they are before you need them, not at 10pm when the dance floor finally fills up.

### the same test applies to every vendor

- **caterers**: how many hours of service staff, and does teardown count?
- **DJs and bands**: is setup and soundcheck inside or outside the contracted block?
- **videographers**: do they leave with the photographer or stay for the send-off?
- **planners**: "day-of" can mean eight hours on site or four, plus vague "unlimited email."

## 3. list every deliverable side by side

this is where the bundled quote usually wins and the itemized quote usually surprises you, or the reverse. you won't know until you line them up.

make a two-column list. everything one vendor includes goes in the left column. everything the other includes goes in the right. empty cells are the whole point of the exercise.

for photography, the usual suspects:

1. **number of edited images delivered** (and whether raw files are available at all)
2. **turnaround time** (six weeks versus six months is a real difference in value)
3. **second shooter** (included, optional at a cost, or not offered)
4. **assistant** (not the same as a second shooter, and some quotes conflate them)
5. **engagement session** (included, or a separate line)
6. **album or prints** (and how many spreads, which is where album pricing hides)
7. **print release or usage rights** (can you order your own prints, or only through them?)
8. **online gallery and how long it stays live**

add a dollar value to every empty cell. if vendor A includes a second shooter and vendor B charges $800 for one, vendor B's real number just went up $800. if vendor A includes an album vendor B lists at $600, same move.

do this honestly, including the things you'd skip. if you genuinely don't want an engagement session, don't credit it as value. you're comparing what you'll actually use.

## 4. flag how each one handles travel

travel is the fee that shows up after you've emotionally committed.

some charge mileage past 30 mi, others bill a flat $150-$400 travel fee. some charge nothing within a radius and then a lot outside it. some bill for a hotel room if your venue is more than a certain distance out, and that line can be open-ended ("at cost") rather than capped.

what to pin down in writing:

- is there a free radius, and from what address? (their studio? their home? the nearest major city?)
- what's the per-mile rate past that radius, or is it a flat fee?
- does travel apply to a second shooter separately?
- is lodging required for your date, and is it capped at a dollar amount?
- do rehearsal dinner or next-day brunch visits trigger a second travel charge?

run your actual venue address through their stated radius before you compare anything. a flat $150 travel fee and a mileage-based charge can land $300 apart on the same drive.

## 5. map both payment schedules onto your own calendar

two vendors can charge the identical total and still feel completely different in your bank account.

retainer due dates and final-payment deadlines differ; map both to your calendar.

vendor A: 25% retainer at signing, 25% at six months out, 50% due fourteen days before the wedding.
vendor B: 50% retainer at signing, 50% due thirty days before.

if you book twelve months out, vendor B wants thousands of dollars right now, in the same month you're also paying your venue deposit. that's a cash flow problem even if the total is lower.

build one shared calendar with every vendor's due dates on it. you're looking for pileups: the month where three final payments land at once, usually six to eight weeks before the wedding, right when you're also paying for rentals, alterations, and the rehearsal dinner. if you can shift one vendor's deadline by two weeks, ask before you sign. almost nobody asks, and a lot of vendors will say yes.

also check: are payments refundable or credited if you reduce scope? does a late payment trigger a fee or cancel the booking outright? is there a discount for paying by check or bank transfer instead of card? a 3% card fee on a $6,000 catering bill is $180.

## 6. read both cancellation clauses word for word

this is the one that matters more than most couples realize until it doesn't go to plan.

one may keep 50% after 90 days, another keeps 100% after 60. same service, same price, wildly different exposure if your venue floods, a family member gets sick, or you move the date.

read for these specific things:

1. **the retainer's status.** is it non-refundable in all cases? almost always yes. know the number.
2. **the sliding scale.** what percentage is owed at 180 days, 90 days, 60 days, 30 days?
3. **date changes versus cancellation.** some vendors let you move once at no cost if they're available. some treat any change as a cancellation plus a rebooking.
4. **the force majeure clause.** what counts, and does it get you a refund or only a credit?
5. **vendor-side cancellation.** if they cancel on you, what do you get back, and do they owe you a replacement referral?
6. **illness and substitution.** if your named photographer can't shoot, does someone else from the studio cover it, and do you get approval rights?

a quote that's $400 cheaper but keeps 100% of your money at 60 days is not the safer booking.

## red flags to watch for

- **"full day" with no clock-out time in writing.** if they won't commit to hours in the contract, that's the whole flag.
- **a total with no itemization and no response when you ask for one.** you're allowed to ask. the answer tells you a lot.
- **travel described as "TBD" or "at cost."** uncapped is not a number.
- **no cancellation terms at all.** a contract that doesn't address cancellation isn't protecting you, it's protecting them by default.
- **pressure to sign within 24 hours "because someone else is asking about your date."** sometimes true. often not. a real vendor will give you 48 hours to read a contract.
- **verbal promises that never make it into the written agreement.** if they said it on the call and it's not in the PDF, it doesn't exist. reply-all and get it added.
- **auto-renewing or auto-escalating fees** (price increases if you add guests, hours, or change venue) with no stated cap.

if you want more on where these costs hide across categories, the [hidden costs](/blog/category/hidden-costs) and [contracts](/blog/category/contracts) archives go deeper on specific vendor types.

## the short version

before you sign either quote:

1. strip both to the base rate before tax, gratuity, and travel.
2. get exact arrival and clock-out times in writing, then calculate cost per hour.
3. list every deliverable side by side and assign a dollar value to each empty cell.
4. confirm the travel radius, the rate past it, and whether lodging is capped.
5. put both payment schedules on one calendar and look for the pileup month.
6. read both cancellation clauses word for word, including date changes and vendor-side cancellation.

do this once and you'll never look at a wedding quote the same way. the goal isn't to find the cheapest vendor. it's to know, with actual numbers, which one you're really choosing.
